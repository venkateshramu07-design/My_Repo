Updated 23 October 2026

Quick answer: Will interest rates go up? Interest rates are rising again for the first time in about three years. The US Federal Reserve raised its policy rate to 3.75% to 4% on 16 September 2026, the European Central Bank and the Reserve Bank of India have also raised rates, and the Bank of England came within one vote of doing the same. If you borrow at a variable rate, your costs are going up; if you save, you can earn more, but only if you move your money. The seven moves that matter most: pay down expensive variable debt first, check whether to fix your mortgage or loan rate, move savings to an account that actually pays the higher rate, lock in some longer-term deposits, look again at bonds, keep investing steadily rather than reacting, and rebuild your emergency fund. The calculator below shows what a rate rise means for your own numbers.

For most of the past fifteen years, the direction of interest rates was a safe bet. They fell, or they stayed low, and when they rose after the pandemic, everyone expected them to come back down. By the start of 2026 they were doing just that. Then inflation, pushed up again by energy prices, refused to settle, and central banks turned around. A generation of homebuyers and borrowers who only knew falling rates now faces something new.

Rising rates are not good or bad in themselves. They move money from borrowers to savers. Which side of that transfer you are on, and how quickly you act, decides whether this turn in the cycle costs you or pays you. This guide explains what has changed, why it happened, how it reaches your wallet, and the practical steps worth taking now, wherever you live.

What Has Changed in 2026

The turn came quickly. In the first half of the year, most forecasters expected central banks to keep cutting. Instead:

  • The US Federal Reserve raised its target range by a quarter point to 3.75% to 4% on 16 September 2026, its first increase since July 2023. Its policymakers' median forecast points to one more rise before the end of the year.
  • The European Central Bank raised its three key rates by a quarter point on 10 September, taking its deposit rate to 2.50%.
  • The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 17 September, but three of its nine policymakers voted to raise it. Markets have been betting on a rise in November.
  • The Reserve Bank of India raised its repo rate by a quarter point to 5.50% on 7 October, its first rise since February 2023, and changed its stance to "calibrated tightening".

At the same time, long-term borrowing costs have climbed even faster. The US 10-year Treasury yield, which sets the price of mortgages and corporate loans around the world, touched about 5.3% in early October, its highest level since 2002. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate in the United States rose to around 7.4%, according to Freddie Mac.

Why Rates Are Going Up Again

The short answer is inflation that would not go away. US consumer prices rose 3.7% in the year to September 2026, up from 3.4% in August, with petrol prices alone jumping by about 9% in a single month. UK inflation was 3.1% in August, also driven largely by energy. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, has been lower, but central banks worry that high energy prices will feed into wages, rents and everything else if they are allowed to persist.

Central banks have one main tool against inflation: making borrowing more expensive. Higher rates cool spending, slow hiring and, eventually, ease the pressure on prices. The cost is slower growth and, sometimes, a recession. Policymakers have also been stung by the experience of 2021 and 2022, when they waited too long to respond to rising prices and then had to raise rates far and fast. This time, they are moving earlier.

Whether this proves to be a short episode of one or two rises or the start of a longer cycle depends on what happens to energy prices and wages over the coming months. Nobody knows, including the central banks themselves; the minutes of the Fed's September meeting showed a committee deeply divided about what to do next. The sensible approach is to prepare for rates staying higher for longer, and be pleasantly surprised if they do not.

How a Rate Rise Reaches Your Wallet

A central bank does not set the rate on your credit card or mortgage directly. It sets the rate at which banks borrow overnight, and that ripples outwards at different speeds:

  • Credit cards and overdrafts move almost immediately. Most US cards are tied to the prime rate, which changes within days of a Fed decision. The average US card rate was about 19.7% in mid-October, according to Bankrate.
  • Floating-rate loans follow within weeks or months: variable mortgages in the UK and India, home equity lines in the US, many business and student loans. In India, loans linked to the repo rate are reset at the next reset date set by your lender.
  • Fixed-rate mortgages and loans do not change until you take a new one, remortgage or reach the end of a fixed period. But new fixed rates follow long-term bond yields, which have risen sharply.
  • Savings accounts should pay more, but banks are often slow to pass on increases, especially to existing customers. Online banks tend to move first.
  • Fixed deposits and certificates of deposit pay more on new money, but existing deposits keep their old rate until they mature.

The lag matters. Your borrowing costs tend to rise faster than your savings income, which is why acting quickly on both sides pays.

Move 1: Pay Down Your Most Expensive Variable Debt First

Credit card debt was already the most expensive money most people borrow; at close to 20% a year in the US, and higher in many other countries, it now costs even more. Every pound, dollar or rupee you pay off is a guaranteed, tax-free return equal to the interest rate, which no savings account or investment can match.

List your debts by interest rate. Put every spare amount towards the highest-rate balance while paying the minimum on the rest, then roll that payment on to the next one; this is the "avalanche" method, and it saves the most interest. If you need the motivation of quick wins, paying off the smallest balance first, the "snowball", works too. Either beats paying evenly across all of them.

Look at balance-transfer offers carefully. A 0% transfer can buy you a year or more without interest, but the fee, usually 3% to 5%, and the rate when the offer ends both matter. Use the time to pay the balance down, not to add more to it.

Move 2: Decide Whether to Fix Your Mortgage or Loan

If you have a variable-rate mortgage or loan, you now face a choice: stay variable and absorb any further rises, or switch to a fixed rate for certainty. There is no universally right answer, but there are some useful rules of thumb.

  • Fixing makes most sense if a further rise of one or two percentage points would strain your budget, if you value certainty, or if you expect to stay in the property for the length of the fixed period.
  • Staying variable makes most sense if your budget has room to absorb rises, if fixed rates are already priced well above variable ones, or if you may sell or repay early, since fixed loans often carry penalties for leaving early.
  • Splitting the loan, part fixed and part variable, is an option many lenders offer and a reasonable middle way.

Remember that fixed rates already include the market's expectations of future rises. If markets expect rates to keep climbing, fixed deals will be expensive. You are not trying to beat the market; you are buying insurance against a budget shock. For a deeper look at the trade-offs, see our guide to [[2023/04/a-study-on-fixed-interest-vs-floating]].

Move 3: Make Sure Your Savings Actually Earn the Higher Rate

This is the easiest money most people will ever make, and the most often missed. Banks rarely raise rates on existing savings accounts as quickly as they raise rates on loans. Many still pay a fraction of a percent on the money in traditional savings accounts, while online banks and competitive accounts pay several times as much.

In October 2026, the best easy-access accounts in the United States were paying above 4%, with promotional deals reaching 5% for new customers. In the UK, the best fixed-term savings bonds were paying more than 5%. If your money sits in an account paying 0.5%, moving it costs you an hour and could earn you an extra 3% or more a year.

Check three things before you move: that the account is covered by your country's deposit protection scheme (the FDIC in the US, the FSCS in the UK, the DICGC in India), whether a headline rate is a temporary bonus that drops after a few months, and whether you need to meet conditions such as monthly deposits to earn it.

Move 4: Lock In Some Longer-Term Rates

When rates are rising, it is tempting to keep everything short and wait for them to peak. The trouble is that nobody knows when that will be, and rates on longer deposits often start falling before central banks stop raising, because markets look ahead.

A sensible compromise is a ladder: split money you will not need soon across deposits of different lengths, say one, two and three years. Each time one matures, you reinvest it at the longest rung. You always have some money coming free, and you capture higher long-term rates without betting everything on one moment. Fixed deposits in India, certificates of deposit in the US and fixed-rate bonds in the UK all work for this.

Keep your emergency fund out of the ladder. That money needs to be available immediately, without penalty.

Move 5: Take a Fresh Look at Bonds

Rising rates are painful for bonds you already own, because their prices fall when new bonds pay more. But they also make bonds far more attractive to buy. A government bond yielding 5% offers a return that, for much of the past decade, investors could only dream of.

For investors who have held almost nothing but shares for years, this is a good moment to reconsider. High-quality bonds or bond funds can provide steady income and tend to rise in value if the economy slows and rates fall again, which makes them a natural balance to shares. Shorter-dated bonds carry less price risk; longer-dated ones lock in today's yields for longer but swing more if rates keep climbing.

You do not need to pick individual bonds. Low-cost government bond funds, or in some countries direct government savings schemes, do the job for most people.

Move 6: Keep Investing, but Check Your Mix

Share markets often wobble when rates rise, particularly the fast-growing technology companies whose value depends on profits far in the future. That is no reason to stop investing. Over long periods, shares have beaten inflation by a wide margin, and trying to jump in and out around central bank decisions usually costs more than it saves.

What a rate rise is a good reason to do is check that your mix of shares, bonds and cash still matches your goals and your nerves. If a sharp fall would push you to sell in a panic, you probably hold too much in shares. If you are decades from retirement and holding mostly cash, inflation and the opportunity cost of missing out on growth are bigger risks than a market dip.

Regular investing, such as a monthly contribution to an index fund or a systematic investment plan, takes the decision about timing away from you. Keep it going.

Move 7: Rebuild Your Emergency Fund

Rate rises are designed to slow the economy, and slower economies mean fewer jobs and smaller pay rises. This is the moment to make sure you could cope with a shock: a job loss, a medical bill or a big repair.

The usual guidance is three to six months of essential expenses, more if you are self-employed, the only earner or supporting a family. The good news is that higher rates mean your emergency fund now earns something meaningful in a good savings account. What the Rise Means Where You Live

The broad direction is the same in most large economies, but the way rate rises reach households differs from country to country.

  • United States: most mortgages are fixed for 30 years, so existing homeowners are largely protected. The pain falls on new buyers, people with credit card debt, and anyone with a home equity line or other variable loan. Savers in online banks and money market funds feel the benefit quickly.
  • United Kingdom: most mortgages are fixed for only two to five years, so hundreds of thousands of households move on to new, higher rates every year. If your fixed deal ends in the next six months, most lenders let you secure a new rate in advance; it is worth doing early.
  • Euro area: practice varies widely. Spain, Portugal and Italy have many variable-rate mortgages that reprice quickly; Germany and France mostly fix for ten years or more. Bank deposit rates have been slow to follow the ECB.
  • India: most home loans are floating and linked to the repo rate, so the October rise will feed through at your loan's next reset. Lenders often extend the tenure rather than raise the EMI; ask yours which it will do, because a longer tenure can add a great deal of interest. Fixed deposit rates usually rise within weeks of a repo hike.
  • Elsewhere: in Canada and Australia, where many mortgages are variable or fixed for short periods, households are especially sensitive to rate changes; check your own lender's terms rather than relying on national averages.

If You Are Planning to Buy a Home

Higher rates change the arithmetic of buying. In the United States, a 30-year mortgage at 7.4% costs far more each month than the same loan at 6%; on a $400,000 loan, the difference is roughly $370 a month, or more than $130,000 over the life of the loan. That does not mean you should not buy, but it does mean:

  • Work out what you can afford at today's rates, not at the rates you hope to refinance to later.
  • A larger deposit reduces both the loan and, often, the rate you are offered.
  • Compare lenders. Rates offered to the same borrower can differ by half a percentage point or more.
  • Consider whether waiting makes sense for you. House prices sometimes soften when rates rise, but not always, and rent keeps costing money while you wait.

If You Are Retired or Living on Savings

Rising rates are generally good news for savers and retirees living on interest. Annuity rates, which depend on long-term bond yields, have improved considerably. In India, senior citizens' deposit schemes and in the UK fixed-rate bonds offer better income than for years.

The main risk is inflation eroding the value of that income. A deposit paying 5% when prices are rising at 4% leaves a real return of only about 1%. Keep a portion of long-term savings in assets that can grow, and avoid locking all your money away at one fixed rate.

Mistakes to Avoid When Rates Rise

  • Doing nothing with your savings. Leaving money in a low-paying account is the costliest mistake, and the easiest to fix.
  • Panic-selling investments. Markets usually price in rate rises before they happen. Selling after a fall locks in the loss.
  • Taking on new variable debt. A loan that looks affordable today may not be after two more rises.
  • Chasing the highest headline rate blindly. Check the deposit protection, the conditions and how long the rate lasts.
  • Trying to time the peak perfectly. Ladders and regular investing beat guessing.

Frequently Asked Questions: Will Interest Rates Go Up

Why did the Federal Reserve raise rates in September 2026?

Because inflation had stayed above its 2% target for years and had started rising again, driven largely by energy prices. US consumer prices rose 3.7% in the year to September. The Fed raised its range by a quarter point to 3.75% to 4% to cool demand and bring inflation back down.

Will interest rates keep rising?

Possibly. The Fed's own forecasts point to one more rise in 2026, and other central banks have signalled they could move again. But forecasts change with the data, especially energy prices and wages. Plan for rates staying higher for a while rather than betting on a quick reversal.

Should I pay off debt or save when rates are rising?

Pay off high-interest debt first, especially credit cards, because the interest you save is almost always higher than the interest you could earn. Keep a small emergency fund at the same time, so a surprise bill does not push you back into debt.

Should I switch to a fixed-rate mortgage now?

It depends on how much a further rise would strain your budget and how long you plan to keep the loan. Fixing buys certainty, but fixed rates already reflect expected rises and may carry exit penalties. Splitting between fixed and variable is a reasonable compromise.

Are bonds a good investment when rates rise?

Bonds you already own fall in price when rates rise, but new bonds pay higher interest, which makes them more attractive to buy. With government bond yields near 5% in the US, high-quality bonds offer income and balance to a share-heavy portfolio.

How do rising rates affect the stock market?

Higher rates tend to weigh on share prices, especially for fast-growing companies, because future profits are worth less today and borrowing costs more. Markets often fall ahead of rate rises and recover once the path becomes clearer. For long-term investors, steady investing matters more than timing.

Final Thoughts

The return of rising interest rates ends a long period in which borrowing was cheap and saving paid almost nothing. That period shaped the habits of a generation: stretch for the biggest mortgage, keep cash in any old account, put everything in shares. Those habits now need revisiting.

The moves that matter are not complicated. Shrink expensive variable debt. Decide deliberately whether you want the certainty of a fixed rate. Make your savings work as hard as the bank's loans do. And keep investing steadily rather than reacting to every headline from Washington, Frankfurt, London or Mumbai. Do those things, and a rising-rate world can leave you better off rather than worse.
