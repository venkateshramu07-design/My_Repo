Quick answer: Buying a home with mortgage rates above 7% can still make sense, but only if you expect to stay put long enough, usually at least seven to ten years in the United States, and only if the monthly cost fits comfortably in your budget today, not after a refinance you hope for later. In October 2026, the average US 30-year fixed rate reached about 7.4%, the median existing home sold for $417,500 in September, and the median apartment rent was about $1,388, slightly lower than a year earlier. That combination means renting is cheaper month to month in most places, while buying builds equity and protects you from future rent rises. Waiting for rates to fall is a reasonable choice if you are unsure how long you will stay or if buying would stretch you; it is not a guaranteed win, because prices and rates can move either way. For a generation of buyers, the rule was simple: buy as soon as you can, because renting is "throwing money away" and house prices only go up. Mortgage rates of 3% in 2021 made that advice look obvious. In the autumn of 2026, with rates more than twice that and prices still near record highs, the arithmetic is very different, and a lot of would-be buyers are wondering whether they should wait, rent for longer or give up on owning altogether.

There is no single right answer, because the decision depends on your income, savings, local market and plans. But there is a right way to think about it. This guide explains what has changed, the real costs of owning and renting, how long you need to stay to come out ahead, what waiting does and does not gain you, and how the picture differs in the US, the UK, India and elsewhere.

What Has Changed in 2026

  • Mortgage rates are back above 7%. The average 30-year fixed rate in the US, as measured by Freddie Mac, averaged about 6.9% in September 2026 and reached 7.4% in early October, the highest since 2023. It follows the 10-year Treasury yield, which rose above 5%.
  • Prices have barely moved. The median US existing-home price was $417,500 in September, up just 1.3% on a year earlier according to the National Association of Realtors, after years of rapid gains.
  • More homes are for sale. Inventory reached 1.6 million homes, the highest since late 2019, equal to 4.8 months of sales. Homes took a median of 33 days to sell. Buyers have more choice and more room to negotiate than at any time since before the pandemic.
  • Rents have flattened. The median US apartment rent was about $1,388 in September, roughly 0.4% lower than a year earlier, according to Apartment List, after a wave of new apartment construction. Vacancy was around 7%, high by historical standards.

In short: buying has become much more expensive month to month, while renting has become slightly cheaper. That is the opposite of the market most people remember.

The Real Cost of Owning

The mortgage payment is only part of the cost of owning a home. To compare fairly with renting, add up everything:

  • Mortgage interest: the true cost of borrowing. In the early years of a mortgage, most of each payment is interest.
  • Principal repayment: this is not really a cost, because it becomes equity you own. But it is money you cannot spend or invest elsewhere.
  • Property tax: roughly 0.5% to 2% of the home's value a year in the US, depending on the state; council tax in the UK; municipal property tax in India.
  • Insurance: homeowners' insurance has risen sharply in parts of the US exposed to storms, floods and wildfires.
  • Maintenance and repairs: a common rule of thumb is 1% to 2% of the home's value a year, more for older houses. Roofs, boilers, plumbing and appliances eventually need replacing.
  • Buying and selling costs: closing costs, legal fees, stamp duty or registration charges, and estate agents' fees can add up to several percent of the price at each end.
  • Opportunity cost: the deposit you put into a house could otherwise be invested. Over many years, that forgone return is one of the biggest hidden costs of owning.

Take a typical American example. On a $417,500 house with a 20% deposit, the mortgage is $334,000. At 7.4%, principal and interest come to about $2,313 a month. Add property tax, insurance and upkeep of around 2% of the price a year, about $700 a month, and the total monthly outlay is around $3,000. A similar house might rent for $2,400. The owner pays more each month, but part of that payment is building equity, and the house may rise in value.

The Real Cost of Renting

Renting looks simpler: you pay the rent and, usually, contents insurance. But it has costs of its own.

  • Rent rises: over long periods, rents tend to rise roughly in line with incomes and inflation. A mortgage payment on a fixed-rate loan, by contrast, stays the same for decades.
  • No equity: rent buys a place to live but builds no ownership.
  • Less control: landlords can raise rents, decline to renew, restrict pets or renovations, and sell the property.
  • Moving costs: renters tend to move more often, which costs money and disruption.

Renting's great advantage is flexibility, and the money it frees up. A renter who invests the deposit and the monthly difference between owning and renting can build substantial wealth, provided they actually invest it rather than spend it. That "if" is important: owning works as a kind of forced savings plan, which is one reason homeowners tend to have more wealth than renters on average.

How Long You Need to Stay

The most important number in the rent-versus-buy decision is how long you will live in the home. Buying involves large up-front costs and selling costs. Those are spread over every year you stay, so the longer you stay, the more buying makes sense.

Using typical US assumptions, a $417,500 home bought with a 20% deposit at 7.4%, ownership costs of 2% a year, home prices and rents rising 3% a year, and a renter investing the difference at 6%, buying overtakes renting after roughly nine years. At 6% mortgage rates, the break-even comes several years sooner. If you might move within five years, for a job, a relationship or a growing family, renting is usually the better financial choice at today's rates.

The calculator above shows how sensitive the answer is to each assumption. Small changes in expected home price growth or investment returns can swing the result by years. That is not a flaw in the calculator; it is the nature of the decision.

Three Buyers, Three Answers

The same market can give different answers to different people. Three illustrative examples:

Priya, 31, newly promoted, may relocate

Priya has saved enough for a 10% deposit and her employer has hinted at a move to another office within three years. Buying now would mean paying closing costs, a higher rate for a small deposit, and selling costs if she moves, all in a short period in which the home may not rise much in value. For her, renting and investing her savings is very likely the better financial choice. If she does settle, she will have a bigger deposit and a clearer idea of where she wants to live.

Daniel and Grace, late 30s, two children, settled

They have lived in the same town for eight years, both work locally, and want their children to stay in the same schools. They have a 20% deposit and an emergency fund of six months. The full monthly cost of owning a suitable house would be about 30% of their take-home pay. They expect to stay at least fifteen years, well beyond the break-even point, and a fixed rate protects them from rising rents. Buying makes sense, even at 7.4%, as long as they choose a house they can afford at today's rate and treat any future refinance as a bonus.

Ahmed, 58, renting in an expensive city

Ahmed rents a flat in a city where homes cost more than 25 times a year's rent. He has substantial savings invested and plans to move somewhere cheaper when he retires in about seven years. For him, the price-to-rent ratio strongly favours renting, and buying would tie up savings he may need. Continuing to rent, and buying later in the cheaper area where he intends to retire, is likely to leave him better off.

None of these answers depends on predicting where rates or prices go next. Each depends on how long the person will stay, how secure their finances are, and how expensive owning is compared with renting where they live.

Should You Wait for Rates to Fall?

Many buyers are tempted to wait for lower rates. That can work, but it is a bet, not a sure thing.

  • Rates may not fall soon. With inflation at 3.7% and central banks raising rates, many forecasters expect mortgage rates to stay elevated for some time. Waiting for 5% could mean waiting years.
  • Prices could rise when rates fall. Lower rates bring more buyers back into the market. If many people are waiting for the same moment, competition and prices may jump when it arrives.
  • You keep paying rent. Each year of waiting is a year of rent and a year without equity.
  • Refinancing is possible but not guaranteed. "Marry the house, date the rate" is popular advice: buy now and refinance when rates fall. It works if rates do fall and you still qualify, but refinancing costs money, and you should only buy if you can afford the payment at today's rate indefinitely.

The strongest reason to wait is not to time the market, but to strengthen your position: build a larger deposit, improve your credit score, pay down other debts and become surer about where you want to live. Those things lower your rate and your risk whatever the market does.

Signs You Are Ready to Buy

  • You expect to stay in the area for at least seven to ten years.
  • The total monthly cost of owning, not just the mortgage, is no more than about a third of your take-home pay, and you could still afford it if rates on any variable portion rose.
  • You have a deposit of at least 10% to 20% plus money for closing costs, moving and furnishing.
  • You will still have an emergency fund of three to six months of expenses after buying.
  • Your income is stable and you have no high-interest debt.
  • You have found a home you would be happy living in even if prices fall for a few years.

Signs It May Be Better to Keep Renting

  • You might move within five years.
  • Buying would use up your emergency fund or leave you with a payment that would be hard to meet if you lost income.
  • Local rents are low relative to prices. A useful check is the price-to-rent ratio: divide the price of a home by a year's rent for a similar one. Above about 20, renting tends to be cheaper; below about 15, buying tends to win. The US example above, $417,500 against $28,800 a year in rent, gives a ratio of about 14.5, which favours buying for those who stay long enough.
  • You value flexibility, or your career is likely to move you around.
  • You are disciplined enough to invest what you save by renting.

How to Buy More Wisely When Rates Are High

  1. Shop around for the mortgage. Rates offered to the same borrower can differ by half a point or more between lenders. On a $334,000 loan, half a point is roughly $110 a month.
  2. Improve your credit score before applying. A higher score can lower your rate significantly. Pay bills on time, reduce card balances and avoid new credit applications in the months before.
  3. Consider a larger deposit. It reduces the loan, may avoid mortgage insurance and can earn a better rate.
  4. Negotiate. With inventory at its highest since 2019, sellers are more willing to cut prices, pay part of your closing costs or fund a temporary "buydown" that lowers your rate for the first few years.
  5. Look at the total cost, not the monthly payment. Stretching a loan over a longer term lowers the payment but adds a great deal of interest.
  6. Be cautious with adjustable rates. An adjustable-rate mortgage may start lower, but you need to be able to afford it if rates rise further.
  7. Get an independent inspection. Unexpected repairs are the fastest way for ownership to become more expensive than you planned.

The Picture Outside the United States

  • United Kingdom: most mortgages are fixed for two to five years, so buyers face a new rate every few years. House prices have been broadly flat in 2026, with London prices falling on a year earlier. With the Bank of England holding rates at 3.75% and considering a rise, fixed deals have become more expensive. First-time buyers benefit from stamp duty relief on cheaper homes, and Lifetime ISAs add a government bonus to deposit savings.
  • India: most home loans are floating and linked to the repo rate, which the Reserve Bank of India raised to 5.50% in October 2026. Rental yields in large cities are low, often around 2% to 4% of the property's value a year, which means renting is frequently cheaper than owning in pure financial terms, especially if you invest the difference. Buying is often driven by security, family and the tax benefits on home loan interest and principal under the old tax regime.
  • Canada and Australia: high prices relative to incomes and mortgages that reset every few years make households very sensitive to rate rises. Affordability is among the worst in the developed world in major cities.
  • Euro area: practices vary. Germany has a large rental market and long fixed-rate loans; Spain and Italy have higher ownership and more variable-rate lending.

The principles are the same everywhere: compare the full cost of owning with renting, think about how long you will stay, and make sure the payment fits your budget with room to spare.

Common Mistakes

  • Buying the most expensive home the bank will lend for. Lenders' limits are not a budget; they leave little room for repairs, children or a loss of income.
  • Ignoring the costs beyond the mortgage. Tax, insurance and maintenance can add 30% or more to the monthly payment.
  • Counting on a refinance. If rates do not fall, you are stuck with today's payment.
  • Treating a home purely as an investment. A home is mainly a place to live. Over long periods it has usually held its value against inflation, but it is not guaranteed to beat a diversified investment portfolio.
  • Emptying your savings for the deposit. New homeowners almost always face unexpected costs in the first year.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it a bad time to buy a house with rates above 7%?

Not necessarily. It is a more expensive time to borrow, which raises the bar. If you will stay at least seven to ten years, can afford the payment comfortably at today's rate and have found the right home, buying can still make sense. If you might move soon or buying would stretch you, renting is usually wiser for now.

Will mortgage rates go down in 2027?

Nobody knows. They depend on inflation, central bank decisions and long-term bond yields. Many forecasters expect them to stay relatively high while inflation remains above target. Plan as if today's rate is the one you will pay.

Will house prices fall if rates stay high?

They might soften in some areas, especially where inventory is rising, but national prices have proved resilient because many owners locked in low rates and do not want to sell. In September 2026, US prices were still 1.3% higher than a year earlier.

Is renting really throwing money away?

No. Rent buys housing, flexibility and freedom from repair bills, just as mortgage interest, property tax and maintenance are costs of owning. What matters is the total cost of each path and what you do with the money you save.

How much deposit do I need?

Many lenders accept 3% to 10%, but a deposit of 20% usually avoids mortgage insurance in the US and gets better rates everywhere. Remember to budget separately for closing costs, moving and furnishing.

What is a good price-to-rent ratio?

Divide a home's price by a year's rent for a similar home. Below about 15, buying usually works out cheaper over time; above about 20, renting usually does; in between, the length of time you stay and your assumptions decide it. Many big cities around the world, from London to Mumbai to Sydney, have ratios well above 20.

Should I buy now and refinance later?

Only if you can afford the payment at today's rate for as long as you own the home. Refinancing is an option if rates fall, not a plan.

Final Thoughts

The old certainty that buying always beats renting was a product of falling interest rates and rising prices. With mortgage rates above 7%, rents flat and prices near their peak, the decision is closer than it has been for a generation, and the right answer depends on your own circumstances more than ever.

Buy if you are settled, financially secure and have found a home you want to live in for many years, at a payment you can manage today. Rent, and invest the difference, if your plans are uncertain or buying would stretch you. Either can be a good decision. The mistake is to make it on autopilot, either rushing to buy because everyone says you should, or waiting forever for a perfect market that may never come.
