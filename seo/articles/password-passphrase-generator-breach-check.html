Quick answer: A strong password today is long, random and used for one account only. Length matters far more than symbols: the US National Institute of Standards and Technology's 2025 guidance asks for at least 15 characters when a password is the only thing protecting an account. The easiest strong password to remember is a passphrase of six or more random words, such as Fable-Orbit-Crumb-Stash7-Lilac-Kiwi. The generator below makes passphrases and random passwords on your own device, rates their strength, and can check whether a password has appeared in a data breach without ever sending the password.

Most people know their passwords are weak; the problem is that strong ones are hard to remember and many generator websites are hard to trust. Any site that creates a password on its server could, in theory, keep it. This generator runs entirely in your browser using its built-in cryptographic random-number generator, the same source that encryption software uses, and nothing is stored or sent.

The words come from the Electronic Frontier Foundation's list of 1,296 short, memorable words designed for this purpose. Each random word adds about 10 bits of strength, so the number of words matters more than how clever they are. Never choose the words yourself or use a famous quote; people are predictable, and attackers' word lists know it.

What Makes a Password Strong in 2026

  • Length first. NIST's SP 800-63B (revision 4, 2025) sets a minimum of 15 characters for passwords used alone and tells websites to allow at least 64. Every extra character multiplies the work for an attacker.
  • Random, not clever. "Summer2024!" meets most old rules but falls in seconds, because attackers try words, years and a symbol at the end first. Test it in the checker to see.
  • One password per account. When one site is breached, criminals try the same email and password everywhere else ("credential stuffing"). A unique password limits the damage to that one site.
  • No forced changes. Current guidance says not to change passwords on a schedule, only when there is a sign one has been exposed, because forced changes lead to weaker, predictable passwords.
  • Turn on two-step verification for email, banking and social media. Even a stolen password is then not enough.

How the Breach Check Works (and Why It Is Safe)

"Check if it appeared in a data breach" uses the Pwned Passwords service run by security researcher Troy Hunt, which holds hundreds of millions of passwords leaked in real breaches. Your password is never sent. The tool scrambles it on your device into a SHA-1 fingerprint, sends only the first 5 of its 40 characters, receives a list of every leaked fingerprint starting with those 5 characters, and compares them on your device. This method, called k-anonymity, means the service can't tell which password you checked. If yours appears even once, stop using it everywhere.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it safe to use an online password generator?

It is safe when the password is created on your device, as this one is, using your browser's cryptographic random-number generator. Avoid generators that create passwords on their server.

How long should a password be?

At least 15 characters for a password that protects an account on its own, following NIST's 2025 guidance. A six-word passphrase or a 16–20 character random password is a good standard.

Are passphrases more secure than passwords?

A passphrase of six or more truly random words is about as strong as a 10-character random password, and much easier to remember and type. Random passwords stored in a password manager are stronger still.

How do I know if my password has been leaked?

Type it into the checker above and press the breach-check button. Only a partial, scrambled fingerprint is sent, so the password itself stays private. If it is found, change it on every site where you use it.

Do I still need a password manager?

Yes, for most people. Remember one strong passphrase for the manager, and let it generate and store a unique random password for every other account.
