Quick answer: Cars have never cost more to buy or to finance. In September 2026, the average new car in the United States sold for $50,562, just below the record, according to Kelley Blue Book, and the average new-car loan payment reached a record $777 a month in mid-2026, according to Edmunds. One in five new-car buyers who borrow now pays $1,000 a month or more, and a record 23.9% of loans run seven years or longer. To afford a car without damaging your finances, aim to keep all car costs, including insurance and fuel, to around 15% of your take-home pay; put at least 20% down; borrow for no more than four or five years; avoid rolling an old loan into a new one; and seriously consider a reliable used car. For most households, a car is the second-largest purchase after a home, and often the largest debt after a mortgage. It also loses value from the day it leaves the showroom. That combination, an expensive, depreciating asset bought largely on credit, is why cars quietly wreck more budgets than almost anything else.

In 2026, the pressure is unusually strong. Prices rose sharply after the pandemic and have not come down, tariffs have added to the cost of many models, and interest rates are rising again. This guide explains what a car really costs, how much you can sensibly afford, how to finance it without falling into the traps that have caught millions of buyers, and how the picture looks in the US, the UK, India and elsewhere.

Why Cars Cost So Much in 2026

  • Prices are near record highs. The average transaction price for a new vehicle in the US was $50,562 in September 2026, only slightly below the record of $50,614 set in December 2025. Before the pandemic, it was under $40,000.
  • Tariffs. US tariffs on imported cars and parts, introduced in 2025, have pushed up costs for carmakers. Many absorbed part of them at first, but price increases on 2026 models averaged nearly $2,000, against about $400 the previous year, according to one industry analysis.
  • Bigger, pricier vehicles. Carmakers have shifted towards SUVs, pickup trucks and well-equipped models, and many have dropped their cheapest cars.
  • Used cars are expensive too. The average used-car listing price was $27,235 in September 2026, the highest since 2022, because fewer new cars were sold during the supply shortages of 2021 and 2022, leaving fewer good used ones now.
  • Borrowing costs are rising. Car loan rates follow broader interest rates, which central banks have started raising again in 2026.

The Real Cost of Owning a Car

The sticker price and the monthly payment are only the beginning. To judge whether you can afford a car, add up the total cost of owning it each month:

  • Loan payment: principal and interest.
  • Depreciation: a new car typically loses 15% to 25% of its value in the first year and around half within five years. You do not pay this monthly, but you lose it when you sell.
  • Insurance: premiums have risen sharply in many countries, especially for newer cars with expensive sensors and electronics.
  • Fuel or charging.
  • Maintenance and repairs, tyres and servicing.
  • Taxes, registration and parking.

Across these items, owning a typical new car in the US costs well over $1,000 a month once depreciation is included. Seen that way, a car is less an asset than a large, ongoing expense.

How Much Car Can You Afford?

There is no single right number, but several guidelines are widely used:

  • The 15% test: keep all car costs, including loan, insurance, fuel and maintenance, to around 15% of your take-home pay. If you have other large debts or live in an expensive city, aim lower.
  • The 20/4/10 rule: put at least 20% down, borrow for no more than four years, and keep the payment plus insurance under 10% of your gross income. It is strict, and many buyers today cannot meet it on a new car, which is itself a useful signal.
  • The half-of-income rule: the total price of all your vehicles should be no more than about half of your annual gross income.

The Long-Loan Trap

The most common way car buyers get into trouble is not the price of the car but the length of the loan. Stretching a loan to seven years makes an expensive car look affordable each month. It also means paying far more interest, and owing more than the car is worth for most of the loan.

[[embed:car-loan-terms-chart]]

On a $40,000 loan at 7%, a three-year term costs $1,235 a month and about $4,500 in interest. A seven-year term cuts the payment to about $604, but the interest rises to more than $10,700. And because the car loses value faster than a long loan is paid down, the borrower spends years "underwater", owing more than the car would sell for.

That matters when life changes. If the car is written off in an accident, stolen, or must be sold because of a job loss or a growing family, an underwater borrower has to find the difference in cash, or roll it into the next loan.

Negative Equity: The Debt That Follows You

Rolling an old loan into a new one is now startlingly common. In 2026, about three in ten US car shoppers trading in a vehicle owed more than it was worth, according to Edmunds, and the average amount of negative equity reached $6,884 in the second quarter. Buyers who roll that debt into a new loan pay an average of more than $900 a month, and more than four in ten of them choose loans of seven years or longer.

That is a cycle that is hard to escape: each new car starts its life with extra debt attached, which makes the next loan longer, which makes the borrower more likely to be underwater again. The way out is to keep the current car longer, pay down the loan until it is worth more than you owe, and save for a larger down payment before buying again.

A Worked Example: Two Ways to Buy a Car

Two neighbours, Alex and Jordan (an illustrative example), each need to replace a car. Both earn about $5,500 a month after tax.

Alex buys a new SUV for $48,000 including taxes and fees. Alex puts $2,000 down and still owes $6,000 more on the old car than it is worth, which the dealer adds to the new loan. The total borrowed is $52,000, over 84 months at 8%. The payment is about $810 a month, and the total interest over seven years comes to about $16,100. Add $400 a month for insurance, fuel and upkeep, and car costs take about 22% of Alex's take-home pay. For most of the next five years, Alex will owe more than the SUV is worth.

Jordan buys a three-year-old version of a similar, smaller SUV for $30,000, puts $6,000 down from savings, and borrows $24,000 over 48 months at 8.5%. The payment is about $592 a month, and the total interest is about $4,400. With $350 a month in running costs, car costs take about 17% of take-home pay, and the loan is paid off in four years.

After four years, Jordan owns the car outright and can put the $592 a month into savings or investments. Alex still has three years of payments left. The difference in interest alone, about $11,700, is roughly the price of a decent used car. Both neighbours drive a capable SUV every day. Only one of them is paying for it for the rest of the decade.

Electric Cars: The Cost Question

Electric cars change the arithmetic in both directions. They usually cost less to run, especially when charged at home, and need less maintenance, with no oil changes and less brake wear. But they often cost more to buy, insurance can be higher, and their resale values have fallen sharply in some markets as new models have improved quickly and prices have been cut.

Incentives matter a great deal and have been changing. In the United States, the federal tax credit of up to $7,500 for new electric cars ended on 30 September 2025. Several European countries have also cut or ended subsidies, while others, and some US states, still offer support. China, now the world's largest producer of electric cars, has driven prices down sharply, though tariffs limit how many of its cars reach the US and Europe.

The fair comparison is total cost over the years you will keep the car: purchase price minus expected resale value, plus energy, insurance, maintenance and financing. For high-mileage drivers with home charging, an electric car can be cheaper overall. For low-mileage drivers who would charge at expensive public chargers, it may not be.

Before You Sign: A Checklist

  • Do I know the full out-the-door price, including taxes, fees and any add-ons?
  • Have I compared the dealer's financing with at least one pre-approved offer from a bank or credit union?
  • Is the loan term four or five years at most?
  • Am I putting down at least 10% to 20%, and not rolling in debt from my old car?
  • Do all my car costs, including insurance and fuel, stay around 15% of my take-home pay or less?
  • Have I had a used car independently inspected and checked its history?
  • Have I got an insurance quote for this exact car before buying it?
  • Will I still have an emergency fund after the down payment?

If the answer to any of these is no, slow down. A car that is right for your life and your budget will still be available next week; a seven-year loan, once signed, will not go away.

New or Used?

For most people, the cheapest car to own is a reliable used car a few years old. The first owner absorbs the steepest part of the depreciation, and a well-maintained three- to five-year-old car from a reputable brand can last many more years.

  • Advantages of used: a much lower price for a similar car, lower insurance in many cases, and slower depreciation.
  • Disadvantages: higher loan rates on used cars, shorter or no warranty, more risk of hidden problems, and less choice.
  • Certified pre-owned cars, inspected and sold with an extended warranty by dealers, offer a middle ground at a somewhat higher price.
  • New makes most sense if you plan to keep the car for ten years or more, want the latest safety features, or can get unusually good financing offers from the manufacturer.

Whatever you buy, get an independent inspection of a used car before paying, and check its history for accidents, flood damage and outstanding finance.

How to Get a Better Car Loan

  1. Check your credit score first. The difference between the rates offered to borrowers with excellent and poor credit can be ten percentage points or more. Paying down card balances and fixing errors in your credit report before applying can save thousands.
  2. Get pre-approved by a bank or credit union. Arriving at the dealer with a loan offer in hand gives you a benchmark and stops the dealer from quietly marking up the rate.
  3. Negotiate the price, not the payment. Dealers like to ask "what monthly payment are you looking for?", because they can hit any payment by stretching the term. Agree on the out-the-door price first, then discuss financing.
  4. Look at manufacturer offers. Carmakers sometimes offer very low or 0% financing on certain models, usually for buyers with strong credit. Compare them with any cash discount offered instead.
  5. Keep the term as short as you can afford. Four or five years at most.
  6. Say no to add-ons you do not need, such as extended warranties, paint protection or credit insurance, which are often rolled into the loan and charged interest.

Leasing: When It Makes Sense

A lease is a long-term rental: you pay for the car's expected depreciation over two to four years plus interest and fees, then hand it back. Monthly payments are usually lower than a loan on the same car.

Leasing can suit people who want a new car every few years, drive predictable distances and value having the latest technology, especially for electric cars, whose resale values have been volatile. But you never own anything, mileage limits and wear-and-tear charges can be expensive, and over a lifetime of driving, leasing one car after another nearly always costs more than buying a car and keeping it for many years.

Ways to Spend Less on Driving

  • Keep your current car longer. Once a car is paid off, every extra year of driving it is the cheapest motoring you will ever have, as long as repair bills stay reasonable.
  • Choose a cheaper model. The gap between a basic and a top-of-the-range version of the same car can be $10,000 or more.
  • Shop for insurance every year. Prices for the same driver and car vary widely between insurers.
  • Consider whether you need a second car. Car-sharing, public transport and occasional rentals can cost far less for households that drive the second car rarely.
  • Look at total cost for electric and hybrid cars. They can cost less to run, especially with home charging, but check insurance, purchase price and local incentives.

Buying a Car Around the World

  • United States: prices and payments are at records, and long loans have become normal. Tariffs on imported cars and parts are adding to prices in 2026.
  • United Kingdom: most new cars are bought on personal contract purchase (PCP) deals, a form of finance with a large final "balloon" payment that is closer to leasing than to a traditional loan. Read the total amount payable carefully. A regulator-led review of historic commission arrangements on car finance is leading to compensation for many past borrowers.
  • India: car loans are mostly floating or fixed for three to seven years from banks and non-bank lenders; prices for small cars fell after the 2025 cut in goods and services tax on vehicles. The same principles apply: keep the loan short, put more down and compare the total cost across lenders.
  • Europe: leasing and PCP-style finance are widespread, and many countries offer incentives for electric cars, though several have been reduced.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a reasonable car payment?

A common guideline is to keep the payment plus insurance under about 10% of your gross income, or all car costs under about 15% of take-home pay. The US average of $777 a month for new-car loans in 2026 is more than many households can comfortably manage.

Is an 84-month car loan a bad idea?

Usually, yes. It lowers the monthly payment but greatly increases the interest you pay and keeps you owing more than the car is worth for years. If a car only fits your budget over seven years, it is probably too expensive.

How much should I put down on a car?

Aim for at least 20% of the price for a new car, and at least 10% for a used one. A larger down payment reduces the loan, the interest and the risk of being underwater.

Is it better to buy new or used in 2026?

For most people, a reliable used car a few years old is the better value, because the first owner absorbs most of the depreciation. New cars make more sense if you keep them for a decade or get exceptional financing.

What should I do if I owe more than my car is worth?

If you can, keep the car and pay extra towards the loan until you owe less than it is worth. Avoid trading it in and rolling the negative equity into a new loan, which starts the next car deeper in debt.

Should I pay cash for a car?

Paying cash avoids interest entirely, which is especially valuable when loan rates are high. Just make sure you keep an emergency fund afterwards. If a manufacturer offers 0% or very low financing and you can earn more on your savings, borrowing and keeping the cash invested can make sense, as long as the loan term is short.

Does my credit score affect my car loan rate?

Very much. Borrowers with the best credit can be offered rates several percentage points lower than those with fair or poor credit, and on a five-year loan that can mean thousands in interest. Check your score and fix any errors before you shop.

How long should I keep a car?

Financially, the longer the better, as long as it stays reliable and repairs remain affordable. Modern cars often run well past 200,000 kilometres, or about 125,000 miles, with good maintenance, and every year without a payment is a year of savings.

Should I lease or buy?

Buy and keep the car for many years if you want the lowest long-term cost. Lease if you value driving a new car every few years, drive predictable mileage and accept paying more over time for that convenience.

Final Thoughts

A car should get you where you need to go without taking you somewhere you do not want to be financially. With prices near records and borrowing costs rising, the safest path is also the least glamorous: buy less car than the dealer offers, put real money down, borrow for as short a time as you can manage, and drive the car for years after the loan is paid off.

If you take only one thing from this guide, make it this: decide what you can afford before you visit a showroom or open a car app, write the number down, and walk away from any deal that needs a longer loan to fit it.

The monthly payment is the number the industry wants you to focus on. The number that matters is the total cost, and what else that money could have done for you.
