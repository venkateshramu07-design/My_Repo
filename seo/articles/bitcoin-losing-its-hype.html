Quick answer: Bitcoin has lost much of its hype, but not most of its substance. By the measures of excitement, interest has collapsed: Google searches for "bitcoin" fell to multi-year lows in October 2026, small investors have been net sellers, and the price, around $81,000 on 23 October, sits about a third below its October 2025 record of roughly $126,000. By the measures of adoption, the picture is sturdier than at any point in its history: US spot Bitcoin funds hold more than $100 billion, companies and governments hold millions of coins between them, and the network has never been more secure. Bitcoin is turning from a story people talk about into an asset people quietly own. That shift is less exciting, and it is not the same thing as decline.

For most of its seventeen years, Bitcoin has run on attention. Every surge in price brought a surge in headlines, which brought new buyers, which pushed the price higher still. Taxi drivers gave tips, celebrities launched coins, and the people who said it was a bubble were told they "didn't get it". This year, something unusual happened. The price fell by more than half from its peak, and almost nobody seemed to care. There were no great obituaries, no stampede for the exits, and very little public argument. The silence itself became the story.

Silence can mean two very different things. It can mean an idea has run its course and the crowd has moved on, as it did with tulips, Beanie Babies and the metaverse. Or it can mean an idea has stopped being novel because it has become part of the furniture, as happened with the internet once the dot-com bubble burst. This guide looks at the evidence on both sides: what the data says about attention, what it says about ownership and use, how this cycle compares with earlier ones, and what it all means for anyone who owns Bitcoin or is wondering whether to.

Where Bitcoin Stands in October 2026

Start with the numbers, because the mood around Bitcoin often runs well ahead of them.

  • Price: about $81,500 on 23 October 2026, roughly 26% lower than a year earlier.
  • The record: about $126,200 on 6 October 2025, set in the weeks after a long run of inflows into Bitcoin funds.
  • The low so far: about $58,000 in late June and early July 2026, around 54% below the record. There was an earlier slide to about $60,000 in early February.
  • Interest rates: on 16 September 2026 the US Federal Reserve raised its policy rate by a quarter point, to 3.75% to 4%, its first increase since July 2023, citing stubborn inflation. Higher rates make assets that pay no income, Bitcoin included, less attractive to hold.
  • Supply: nearly 20 million of the 21 million Bitcoins that will ever exist have been created. The April 2024 "halving" cut the reward for new blocks to 3.125 coins; the next halving is due around 2028.

A fall of more than half from a record would, in most markets, be called a crash. In Bitcoin's history it is closer to ordinary. To see why, we need to separate two things that usually move together and this year did not: attention and adoption.

What "Hype" Actually Means

Hype is attention running ahead of use. It is measurable. You can count it in search volumes, app downloads, social media mentions, television segments and the number of people asking friends whether they should buy. Adoption is measured differently: in how many people and institutions hold an asset, how much money is committed to it, how much infrastructure has been built around it and whether it is used for anything.

In 1995 the research firm Gartner gave this distinction a shape. Its "hype cycle" describes how new technologies tend to move through five stages: a trigger, a peak of inflated expectations, a trough of disillusionment, a slow slope of enlightenment and, eventually, a plateau of productivity. The model is a pattern rather than a law, and plenty of technologies have reached the trough and stayed there. But it captures an old observation, often credited to the American futurist Roy Amara, that we overestimate the effect of a technology in the short run and underestimate it in the long run.

Bitcoin is unusual in that it has gone round this loop several times, with a price chart that doubles as a hype chart. The question for 2026 is not whether the hype has faded. It plainly has. The question is whether use is still rising underneath it.

The Case That the Hype Is Fading

The sceptics have real evidence, and it deserves to be taken seriously.

Nobody is searching

Google search interest in "bitcoin" fell to its lowest level in several years in October 2026, according to data highlighted by several market analysts. Broader searches for crypto fell to roughly a quarter or a third of their August 2025 peak by the middle of this year. Searches did spike briefly during the sell-offs in February and June, but those were people checking on a falling investment, not newcomers arriving. In every earlier cycle, new money followed search interest. This time there is very little search interest to follow.

Small investors have been selling

On-chain data, which tracks the movement of coins between wallets, showed holders of small amounts selling into both 2026 sell-offs rather than buying the dip. Leveraged traders in futures markets, a group that tends to be young, retail and short-term, were liquidated in waves. Much of the speculative energy that once went into crypto appears to have moved elsewhere: to artificial intelligence stocks, which kept rising while Bitcoin fell, and to prediction markets such as Kalshi and Polymarket, where weekly trading reached an estimated $6 billion early this year.

The big buyers are slowing down

Strategy, the American software company turned Bitcoin holder, owns about 848,500 coins bought at an average of around $75,400 each, according to its October 2026 filings. It is still buying, but its weekly purchases have shrunk to tens of millions of dollars from billions at the height of 2025, and it has turned to buying back its own shares. Index provider MSCI has moved to classify such "treasury companies" as non-operating businesses, which could push them out of major stock indices and force passive funds to sell. The model that powered much of the 2025 rally, raising money on the stock market to buy Bitcoin, looks much less like a perpetual motion machine than it did a year ago.

Even the miners are hedging

The companies that secure the Bitcoin network by running vast farms of specialised computers are steadily turning those sites into data centres for artificial intelligence. CoinShares, a digital asset manager, estimates that listed miners could earn as much as 70% of their revenue from AI and high-performance computing by the end of 2026. Core Scientific, one of the largest, earned about five times as much from renting out capacity as from mining Bitcoin in its second quarter. When the industry's own builders start diversifying, it says something about their expectations.

The Case That Bitcoin Is Simply Growing Up

The other side of the ledger is just as concrete, and in some ways more striking, because it has been built during a period of falling prices and little public enthusiasm.

Wall Street now holds the coins

Spot Bitcoin exchange-traded funds, which let ordinary investors buy Bitcoin through a stock-market account, launched in the United States in January 2024. By October 2026 they had taken in more than $57 billion of net new money and held more than $100 billion in assets, with BlackRock's iShares Bitcoin Trust alone accounting for roughly half. Those funds lost money to withdrawals this year, at one point falling from about $104 billion to $80 billion in assets, but most of the money stayed put through a fall of more than 50%. That is not how hype behaves.

The price is calmer than it used to be

Bitcoin is still volatile, but less so than it was. Its historical volatility in 2025 was about 42%, according to figures published by Charles Schwab, lower than that of Nvidia (50%) or Tesla (63%). In early June 2026 its one-week realised volatility fell to around 17%, a multi-year low. An asset that swings less than the most popular technology shares is not a fringe instrument any more, even if it remains a risky one.

The network has never been stronger

Bitcoin's hash rate, the total computing power securing the network, first passed one zettahash per second in April 2025, a number with 21 zeros, and was back above that level in August 2026 despite the miners' turn to AI. Machines that are switched off by one operator tend to be resold and switched back on by another. A higher hash rate makes the network more expensive to attack, which matters more to long-term holders than any amount of public excitement.

The fall was shallower

Every previous Bitcoin cycle ended with a fall of between three-quarters and nine-tenths of its value. This one, so far, has been closer to half.

There are two ways to read that chart. One is that the asset is maturing: deeper, more institutional ownership means fewer forced sellers and a higher floor. The other is that the cycle simply is not over, and the worst is still to come. Both readings fit the data so far. Anyone who tells you with confidence which one is right is guessing.

Every Cycle Has Looked Like the End

A sense of perspective helps. Bitcoin has been declared dead hundreds of times by newspapers, economists and bankers since 2010. After the collapse of the Mt. Gox exchange in 2014, it lost about 85% of its value and spent more than two years in the doldrums. After the initial coin offering boom of 2017 it fell by more than 80%, and "blockchain" became a punchline. After the 2022 collapses of the Terra stablecoin, the lender Celsius and the exchange FTX, the price fell to about $15,500, and many serious observers concluded that crypto as a whole was finished.

Each time, the price eventually went on to a new high. That history is often cited as proof that Bitcoin always comes back. It is no such thing. It is proof that it has come back so far, which is not the same, and past patterns in a seventeen-year-old asset are thin evidence for the future. But the history does show that collapsing attention, on its own, has never been a reliable signal that Bitcoin was finished. Interest has dropped away after every peak, and the market has often been at its quietest close to its lows.

Some analysts go further and read low search interest as a buy signal, on the logic that the crowd is always late. That argument has a weakness: it is unfalsifiable. If interest returns and the price rises, it was right; if interest never returns, the analyst can always say the bottom has not come yet. Treat contrarian signals as a reason to look harder, not as a reason to act.

The ETF Effect: A Different Kind of Owner

The most important change in this cycle is not the price but who holds the coins. In 2017 and 2021, the marginal buyer was a retail trader on a crypto exchange, often using borrowed money and checking prices on a phone at two in the morning. In 2024 and 2025, the marginal buyer was increasingly a pension adviser, a wealth manager or an investor in a retirement account, buying a fund listed on the Nasdaq and settling it through the same plumbing as any other share.

That kind of owner behaves differently. They rebalance rather than panic. They hold Bitcoin as a small slice of a diversified portfolio, often 1% to 5%, rather than as a life-changing bet. They do not post about it. Much of the hype that used to surround Bitcoin was produced by its owners talking about it. An owner base made up of financial advisers and pension funds generates almost none.

The same shift is happening outside the United States. The UK's Financial Conduct Authority allowed ordinary investors to buy crypto exchange-traded notes from October 2025, reversing a ban imposed in 2021. Hong Kong, Australia and much of Europe have listed Bitcoin products. In India, crypto gains are taxed at a flat 30% with a 1% deduction at source on transactions, rules that have pushed many traders offshore or out of the market but have also given the asset a clear legal status. Bitcoin is becoming boring in many countries at once, which is what happens to an asset class once it has been absorbed by the financial system.

Governments: Reserves, Rules and Retreats

Government policy has moved in both directions, and the gap between the announcements and the reality is worth noting.

  • The United States created a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve by executive order on 6 March 2025. It is stocked with coins seized in criminal cases, estimated at between 200,000 and about 328,000, and the Treasury has said it will not buy more on the open market. A bill to put the reserve into law passed a House committee in September 2026 but has not become law. The reserve is a signal of acceptance, not a source of demand.
  • El Salvador, the first country to make Bitcoin legal tender in 2021, agreed in December 2024 to scale back its Bitcoin policy in return for a $1.4 billion loan from the International Monetary Fund. Since early 2025, businesses no longer have to accept it. The government has kept buying small amounts and holds about 7,700 coins, but the experiment in using Bitcoin as everyday money has quietly ended.
  • The European Union now regulates crypto under its Markets in Crypto-Assets rules, known as MiCA, which are fully in force. They have made the market more orderly and forced some products out: Tether's USDT, the largest stablecoin, has been delisted for retail users across the bloc because its issuer has not sought authorisation.
  • Stablecoins in the United States are now governed by the GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025. Its detailed rules have been delayed, but it is due to take full effect by January 2027 at the latest.

The direction of travel is clear enough. Governments have largely stopped trying to ban crypto and started trying to regulate it. Regulation brings legitimacy, but it also removes much of the outlaw glamour that once fed the hype.

Stablecoins: The Part of Crypto People Actually Use

If you want to know where crypto's real-world use has gone, look at stablecoins rather than Bitcoin. These are tokens pegged to a currency, usually the US dollar, and backed by cash and short-term government debt. Their total value was somewhere between $300 billion and $320 billion in mid-2026, according to DefiLlama data, and they are used for cross-border payments, for moving money between exchanges and, in countries with weak currencies such as Argentina, Turkey and Nigeria, as a way to hold dollars.

This matters for the Bitcoin debate because it undercuts one of Bitcoin's original promises. Satoshi Nakamoto's 2008 white paper described "a purely peer-to-peer version of electronic cash". In practice, almost nobody pays for things in Bitcoin. Its price moves too much, and transaction fees rise at busy times. The job of digital cash has been taken by stablecoins, while Bitcoin has settled into a different role: something closer to digital gold, a scarce asset held as a store of value. That is a narrower story than the one that powered the early hype, but it is a coherent one.

Digital Gold, or Just a Risk Asset?

The digital gold argument rests on scarcity. There will only ever be 21 million coins, no central bank can print more, and the supply schedule is fixed in software. For anyone worried about inflation or reckless government borrowing, that is an attractive property.

The trouble is that Bitcoin has not yet behaved like gold when it matters. In 2025, a year of inflation worries, trade wars and geopolitical tension, gold rose by about 64%, its best year since 1979, while Bitcoin ended the year well below its October peak. In 2026 both have fallen as interest rates turned upwards, but not equally: gold fell by more than a quarter from its January record of around $5,600 an ounce, while Bitcoin fell by more than half from its high. Bitcoin has tended to move more like a technology share than a safe haven, falling when rates rise and investors turn cautious.

That does not prove the digital gold thesis wrong. Gold took decades to earn its reputation, and Bitcoin's correlations may change as its owner base changes. But anyone holding Bitcoin as a hedge should be honest that, so far, it has behaved more like a bet on easy money and investor appetite for risk than like insurance against a crisis.

The Dot-Com Comparison

The parallel people reach for most often is the internet. Between March 2000 and October 2002, the Nasdaq index fell by about 78%. Pets.com, Webvan and hundreds of others disappeared. Even Amazon's share price fell by more than 90%. Commentators concluded that the internet had been overhyped, and in the narrow sense they were right: expectations had run years ahead of reality.

Yet the number of people using the internet kept rising throughout the crash. Broadband spread, online shopping grew and the companies that survived went on to become the largest in the world. The hype collapsed; the use did not.

The comparison flatters Bitcoin in one way and warns against it in another. It shows that a collapse in excitement can coexist with steady growth in use. But the internet had obvious, measurable uses that grew every year regardless of share prices: email, search, shopping. Bitcoin's main use is being held. Its value depends far more on people continuing to believe in it than the value of the internet ever did. That makes the loss of attention more dangerous for Bitcoin than it was for the web.

What Would Prove the Sceptics Right

It is easy to tell stories after the fact. A more useful exercise is to decide in advance what evidence would change your mind. Here are the signals worth watching over the next year or two:

  • Sustained fund outflows. Short bursts of withdrawals are normal. A steady drain from the spot funds, month after month, would mean the new institutional owners are giving up.
  • A collapse of the treasury-company model. If Strategy and its imitators were forced to sell coins to meet debts or redemptions, it would remove one of the market's biggest buyers and add a large seller.
  • A falling hash rate. A long decline in the computing power securing the network, as miners leave for AI work, would raise questions about security as the block reward keeps halving.
  • A new low well below $58,000. A fall closer to the 75% to 85% of earlier cycles would undermine the argument that ownership has matured.
  • Regulatory reversal. A major economy moving from regulating Bitcoin to restricting it would hit demand directly.

And the opposite signals would strengthen the case that Bitcoin is maturing: steady inflows into the funds during a quiet market, more pension funds and endowments adding small allocations, a rising hash rate despite the miners' diversification, and a cycle low that holds well above previous ones.

What This Means If You Own Bitcoin, or Are Thinking About It

The most useful lesson of this cycle has nothing to do with predicting the price. It is about size. Bitcoin can fall by half in a year, and has done so repeatedly. The question for any investor is not whether that could happen again but whether they could live with it if it did.

A simple test helps. If Bitcoin makes up 10% of your investments and it falls by half, your total falls by 5%. That is painful but survivable. If it makes up half of your investments, the same fall takes a quarter off everything you own, and Bitcoin then has to double just to get you back to where you started. The arithmetic is unforgiving: the deeper the fall, the steeper the climb back.

A few principles apply wherever you live:

  • Build your foundations first. Bitcoin belongs after an emergency fund, after paying off expensive debt and after regular saving for retirement, not before. Our guide, [[post:articles/emergency-fund-guide]], explains where to start.
  • Keep it small enough to ignore. Many professional advisers who hold Bitcoin for clients keep it to between 1% and 5% of a portfolio. At that size, a crash is an irritation rather than a disaster.
  • Prefer regulated routes. Spot funds and exchange-traded products in your own country, where available, remove the risk of losing coins to a hacked exchange or a forgotten password, at the cost of a small annual fee.
  • Know your tax rules. They differ widely. India taxes crypto gains at 30% with no offsetting of losses; the US and UK treat it broadly like other investments for capital gains. Check the rules where you live before you buy, not after you sell.
  • Ignore the mood. Hype makes people buy at peaks; despair makes them sell at lows. Decide on a size, buy gradually if you buy at all, and rebalance on a schedule rather than on headlines.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Bitcoin dead?

No. It has more owners, more money in regulated funds and more computing power securing it than at any point in its history. What has faded is public excitement and the flood of short-term retail traders. Bitcoin has been declared dead hundreds of times since 2010; it may yet fail, but low attention alone has never been a sign of that.

Why has Bitcoin's price fallen since 2025?

Several forces combined. The rally into October 2025 was driven by fund inflows and companies borrowing to buy coins, both of which slowed. Leveraged traders were forced to sell in waves. And from mid-2026, stubborn inflation pushed the US Federal Reserve back towards raising rates, which hurts assets that pay no income.

Has Bitcoin fallen like this before?

Yes, and further. It fell by about 93% in 2011, 85% from 2013 to 2015, 84% in 2017 to 2018 and 77% in 2021 to 2022. The 2025 to 2026 fall of about 54% to the mid-2026 low is the shallowest of the major cycles so far, though it may not be over.

Are big institutions still buying Bitcoin?

Some are, more cautiously. US spot Bitcoin funds still hold more than $100 billion, though they have seen withdrawals this year. Strategy keeps buying in smaller amounts. Governments mostly hold seized coins rather than buying new ones.

Is low public interest a good time to buy?

Historically, Bitcoin's quietest periods have often been near its lows, and some analysts read today's low search interest that way. But that pattern is not a guarantee, and it is impossible to know in advance whether a low is the final one. If you buy, buy an amount you could lose without serious harm.

Will Bitcoin replace money?

It looks increasingly unlikely in the way its creator imagined. Very few people pay for things in Bitcoin, and El Salvador has stepped back from its experiment with Bitcoin as everyday money. Its more realistic role is as a scarce store of value, while dollar-backed stablecoins handle most crypto payments.

Is Bitcoin a good hedge against inflation?

Not so far, in the short run. Its fixed supply makes it attractive as a long-term hedge in theory, but it has tended to fall when inflation pushes interest rates up, behaving more like a growth share than like gold.

Final Thoughts

The fair answer to the question in the title is yes: Bitcoin is losing its hype, and it may never get the old version back. The crowds, the celebrities and the late-night trading frenzies belong to an earlier phase, when Bitcoin was new, unregulated and owned mostly by enthusiasts. That phase is ending, and its end has been harder on the price than the believers expected.

What is replacing it is quieter and harder to read: an asset held by funds, companies and a few governments, priced alongside interest rates and technology shares, regulated in most large economies and secured by more computing power than ever. Whether that turns out to be the slope of enlightenment or a long, slow fade depends on things no chart can yet show, above all on whether a generation of patient institutional owners stays patient. The sensible response is neither excitement nor dismissal. It is to treat Bitcoin as what it has become: a real, risky, volatile asset that deserves a small, deliberate place in a portfolio, or no place at all.
