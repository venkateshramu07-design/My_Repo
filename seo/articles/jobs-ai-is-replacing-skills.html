Updated 24 October 2026

Quick answer: AI is not replacing whole professions so much as the routine tasks inside them, and the people most affected so far are those starting out. The jobs with the most work that AI tools can already do include customer service, data entry, routine coding, translation, basic writing and back-office administration. In the United States, AI became the most frequently cited reason for announced job cuts in 2026, according to the outplacement firm Challenger, Gray & Christmas, and a Stanford study found employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations about 19% below where it would otherwise have been, mainly because fewer young people are being hired. At the same time, demand is rising for skilled trades, AI-infrastructure technicians and roles built on judgement, relationships and physical work. The skills that protect you are the ones AI does badly: judgement, accountability, working with people, hands-on expertise, and the ability to use AI tools well yourself.

Every big wave of technology has come with a wave of fear about jobs. Mechanised looms, tractors, assembly-line robots, personal computers and the internet were all supposed to bring mass unemployment, and none did. Each destroyed some kinds of work, created others and changed almost everything in between. The question about artificial intelligence is not whether it will change work, which it already is, but how fast, for whom, and what anyone can do about it.

This guide looks at what the data says about AI and jobs in 2026, which kinds of work are most and least exposed, why young workers are feeling it first, where new demand is appearing, and the practical steps that make a career more resilient, whatever your field and wherever you work.

What the Data Says in 2026

Three sets of evidence give the clearest picture so far.

  • Announced job cuts. Challenger, Gray & Christmas tracks the reasons US employers give when they announce layoffs. In 2025, AI was cited in about 55,000 announced cuts, around 5% of the total. In 2026 it became the most frequently cited reason of the year so far, and in the first four months alone accounted for about 16% of announced cuts. Technology companies have led, but finance, retail and logistics firms have also pointed to automation.
  • Hiring of young workers. Researchers at Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, led by Erik Brynjolfsson, analyse anonymised payroll records from ADP covering more than 26 million US workers. Their August 2026 update found that employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the occupations most exposed to AI was about 19% below where it would have been had it grown like employment of the same age group in less-exposed jobs, up from about 15% a year earlier. The gap comes mostly from fewer hires rather than more firings, and they found no similar gap for experienced workers.
  • Which tasks AI performs. Studies of how people actually use AI tools, including Anthropic's Economic Index, find the heaviest use in computer programming, customer service, writing and editing, data processing and analysis. These are tasks, not whole jobs, but jobs made mostly of them are the most exposed.

It is worth stressing what the data does not show. There is no sign so far of economy-wide mass unemployment caused by AI. Overall US job cuts in the first seven months of 2026 were about 40% lower than in the same period of 2025, when large federal government cuts inflated the numbers. Companies also sometimes cite AI for layoffs that have other causes, such as over-hiring during the pandemic or weak demand. The effect is real, concentrated and growing, but not yet a flood.

Tasks, Not Jobs

The most useful way to think about AI and work is to break a job into its tasks. Very few jobs consist entirely of things AI can do. Most combine routine tasks, which AI handles increasingly well, with tasks that need judgement, physical presence, trust or accountability, which it handles poorly.

The Stanford researchers draw a useful distinction between occupations where AI mostly automates tasks, doing them instead of the worker, and occupations where it mostly augments, helping the worker do more. Employment has weakened in the first group and held up in the second. A radiologist is a good example of augmentation: AI has been reading medical scans for a decade, yet the number of radiologists has grown, because the technology handles routine screening while doctors focus on difficult cases and patients.

The practical question for any worker is therefore not "will AI take my job?" but "how much of my week is spent on tasks AI can do, and what would I do with that time instead?"

The Jobs Most Exposed to AI

These roles involve a large share of tasks that current AI tools can perform well. Exposure does not mean the job will vanish; it means the job will change most, and fewer people may be needed to do the same amount of work.

Customer service and call centres

Chatbots and voice agents now handle a large share of routine queries, such as order tracking, password resets and billing questions. Human agents increasingly deal with complaints, complex cases and customers who insist on a person. Many companies have cut contact-centre hiring, and outsourcing hubs in the Philippines, India and elsewhere face pressure.

Data entry and back-office processing

Extracting information from invoices, forms and emails and entering it into systems is exactly what modern AI does well. Roles in claims processing, accounts payable and records management are shrinking fastest where volumes are high and rules are clear.

Routine programming

Computer programming shows the highest measured use of AI tools. Writing standard code, tests and documentation is far faster with AI assistants, which has reduced demand for junior programmers in particular. Experienced engineers who design systems, review AI-written code and understand the business remain in demand.

Translation, transcription and basic writing

Machine translation and transcription are now good enough for most routine purposes, and AI can produce first drafts of marketing copy, reports and product descriptions in seconds. Specialist translators in legal, medical and literary work, and writers whose value lies in reporting, expertise or a distinctive voice, are much less affected.

Administrative and entry-level analytical roles

Scheduling, summarising meetings, drafting standard letters, basic research and building routine spreadsheets used to be how many graduates learned a profession. They are now done much faster with AI, which is part of why entry-level hiring has slowed in fields such as consulting, law, finance and marketing.

Why Young Workers Are Feeling It First

The Stanford findings point to a worrying pattern: AI is not mainly replacing experienced workers, it is reducing the number of jobs for beginners. The tasks that junior staff traditionally did, and through which they learned, are the tasks AI does most easily. Experienced workers have tacit knowledge, judgement and relationships that are hard to automate; new graduates mostly do not yet.

That creates a long-term problem for employers as well as workers. If firms stop hiring juniors, where will the experienced professionals of 2040 come from? Some companies are already redesigning entry-level roles around supervising AI output, handling exceptions and working directly with clients from day one. For young people, the lesson is to look for roles and employers that offer real responsibility and learning, not just routine work that a tool could do.

The Jobs Least Exposed, and Some That Are Growing

Some kinds of work are protected by what AI cannot do: work with your hands in unpredictable physical environments, work that depends on trust and personal relationships, and work where someone must take legal or moral responsibility.

  • Skilled trades: electricians, plumbers, heating and cooling engineers, welders and construction workers. The AI boom is actually increasing demand, because data centres need enormous amounts of electrical, cooling and construction work.
  • Healthcare and care work: nurses, therapists, care workers and doctors. AI helps with records and diagnosis, but ageing populations mean demand keeps growing.
  • Teaching and training: AI tutors can help, but motivating, supervising and caring for learners remains human work.
  • Management and leadership: deciding priorities, resolving conflict, taking responsibility for outcomes.
  • Sales and client relationships built on trust, especially for complex or expensive products.
  • AI-related technical roles: people who build, deploy, secure and maintain AI systems and the infrastructure behind them.

Data-centre construction spending in the United States passed an annual rate of $50 billion for the first time in April 2026, according to Census figures cited by the labour-market analysts at Linkup, larger than spending on general office construction. Business leaders including BlackRock's Larry Fink have warned of a shortage of electricians to build the facilities, and companies such as Google have announced programmes to train tens of thousands of electrical workers and apprentices. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects electrician employment to grow about 11% between 2023 and 2033, much faster than the average for all occupations.

The same pattern appears beyond the United States. In Australia, job site Seek has reported strong demand for tradespeople linked to data-centre construction, and similar shortages of electricians and engineers are reported across Europe and parts of Asia.

The Global Picture

The International Monetary Fund estimated in 2024 that about 40% of jobs worldwide are exposed to AI, rising to around 60% in advanced economies, where more work is done in offices. Roughly half of those exposed jobs could benefit from AI raising productivity; for the other half, AI could take over key tasks, lowering demand for labour and wages.

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 projected that technological and economic change would create about 170 million jobs worldwide by 2030 and displace about 92 million, a net gain of about 78 million. The fastest-growing roles included specialists in AI and big data, fintech engineers and software developers, alongside large numbers of farmworkers, delivery drivers, construction workers, nurses and care workers. The fastest-declining included cashiers, data entry clerks, administrative assistants and bank tellers.

Countries whose economies rely on outsourced back-office and customer-service work, such as India and the Philippines, face particular pressure, but also have large technology workforces positioned to build and run AI services. Lower-income countries with more agricultural and manual work are less exposed in the short term, but also less able to capture the productivity gains.

The Skills That Keep You Employed

If AI handles more routine work, the value of the remaining human contribution rises. The skills employers report finding hardest to hire, and that AI does least well, fall into a few groups.

1. Judgement and problem framing

AI is very good at answering well-defined questions and poor at deciding which questions matter. Knowing what problem to solve, weighing trade-offs with incomplete information and deciding when an answer is good enough are human strengths. They come from experience, which is why deliberately seeking responsibility early in a career matters so much.

2. Working with AI, not against it

The workers most at risk are not those whose jobs AI could do, but those who refuse to use it while colleagues who do become two or three times as productive. Learn the AI tools in your field: how to prompt them, check their output, spot their errors and combine them with your own expertise. Being the person in your team who uses AI well is one of the best forms of job security available.

3. Communication and relationships

Explaining complex ideas simply, persuading, negotiating, building trust with clients and colleagues, and leading teams through change all depend on human understanding. As more written work is produced by machines, the ability to connect in person becomes more valuable, not less.

4. Deep domain expertise

AI is broad but often shallow and confidently wrong. Someone who truly understands tax law, structural engineering, a particular disease or a specific industry can use AI to work faster while catching the mistakes it makes. Generic knowledge is easy to automate; deep expertise combined with experience is not.

5. Hands-on and technical skills

Physical skills in electrical work, mechanics, construction, healthcare and laboratory science are among the hardest to automate, and some of the most in demand. They are also reachable without a university degree, through apprenticeships and vocational training.

6. Learning how to learn

The WEF estimates that about 39% of workers' core skills will change by 2030. The most durable skill is the ability to pick up new ones quickly. Our guide to [[2021/07/how-to-learn-anything-fast]] covers practical techniques.

A Practical Plan for Your Career

  1. Audit your week. Use the check above to estimate how much of your work AI tools can already do. Be honest; the point is to see the change coming.
  2. Automate your own routine work first. Use AI tools to handle the repetitive parts of your job, and use the time saved to take on harder, more visible work. You want to be the one who redesigns the job, not the one designed out of it.
  3. Move towards judgement and people. Volunteer for tasks that involve decisions, clients, cross-team projects or managing others. They build the skills and relationships AI cannot replace.
  4. Build one deep specialism. Pick an area within your field where you can become the person others ask, and keep deepening it.
  5. Keep a financial cushion. Career transitions are easier with savings. An emergency fund of three to six months of expenses buys time to retrain or search properly if your role changes. See our [[post:articles/emergency-fund-guide]].
  6. Consider the trades seriously. For people early in their careers or looking for a change, electrical, mechanical and technical trades offer strong demand, good pay and protection from automation.
  7. Keep learning on a schedule. An hour or two a week on a course, a certification or a side project compounds over years.

How to Talk to Your Employer About AI

Many workers keep quiet about using AI, worried it will make their role look unnecessary. Usually the opposite approach works better. Managers are under pressure to show productivity gains from AI, and the employees who help them do it become more valuable, not less.

  • Show the result, not just the tool. "I cut the monthly report from two days to half a day, and used the time to analyse why sales fell in the north" is far more persuasive than "I use ChatGPT".
  • Offer to help others. Running a short session for colleagues on how you use AI tools positions you as someone who shapes the change.
  • Ask what the job will look like in two years. It is a reasonable question, and the answer tells you whether to build skills inside the company or start looking outside it.
  • Ask for training. Many employers have budgets for AI and digital skills that go unused because nobody asks.
  • Follow the rules. Check your employer's policy before putting company or customer data into any AI tool; breaches of confidentiality are a quick way to lose a job.

What Employers and Governments Are Doing

Responses vary widely. Some employers are retraining staff whose roles change, moving customer-service agents into quality control of AI systems or sales, and redesigning graduate schemes. Others are simply hiring fewer people. Governments are expanding apprenticeship schemes and digital-skills programmes, and some are debating stronger safety nets for displaced workers. Large firms including BlackRock and Google have funded programmes to train electricians and other trades for the data-centre build-out.

No country has a complete answer yet. For individual workers, the practical implication is not to wait for one: the people who adapt earliest tend to do best.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which jobs will AI replace first?

Jobs made up mostly of routine, rule-based tasks done at a computer are most exposed: customer service, data entry, routine programming, translation and transcription, and basic administrative work. In most cases, AI is reducing the number of people needed rather than eliminating the job entirely.

Is AI causing mass unemployment?

Not so far. AI became the most cited reason for announced US job cuts in 2026, but there is no evidence of economy-wide mass unemployment, and overall job cuts were lower than in 2025. The clearest effect is slower hiring of young people into AI-exposed occupations.

Which jobs are safe from AI?

No job is completely untouched, but the safest combine physical work in unpredictable environments, personal trust and care, or legal and moral responsibility: skilled trades, healthcare, care work, teaching, management and complex sales. Demand for electricians and data-centre technicians is rising because of AI.

Should I learn to code in 2026?

Coding is still valuable, but routine coding is one of the tasks AI does best, and junior programming jobs have become harder to get. Learn to code if you want to build things, understand systems or work alongside AI, but combine it with domain knowledge, design and problem-solving skills rather than relying on writing standard code alone.

How can graduates find jobs when AI is doing entry-level work?

Look for employers that offer genuine responsibility and training, build practical skills with AI tools before you apply, gain experience through internships, projects or freelance work, and consider sectors where demand is growing, such as healthcare, energy, skilled trades and AI infrastructure.

Will AI create new jobs?

Yes. The World Economic Forum projects about 170 million new jobs worldwide by 2030 against about 92 million displaced. New roles are appearing in building, running and securing AI systems, in the construction and maintenance of data centres and power networks, and in fields where AI makes services cheaper and more widely available.

Final Thoughts

AI is changing work faster than any technology since the personal computer, and its early effects are concentrated on routine office tasks and on young people trying to get a foot in the door. Pretending otherwise helps no one. Neither does panic: the evidence so far points to a reshaping of jobs, not their disappearance, and to rising demand in fields from electrical work to healthcare.

The workers who will do best are those who treat AI as a tool to master rather than a threat to wait out: who use it to clear away routine work, invest the time saved in judgement, relationships and deep expertise, and keep learning. That has always been the way to stay employable through technological change. The difference now is that the change is happening in years rather than decades.
