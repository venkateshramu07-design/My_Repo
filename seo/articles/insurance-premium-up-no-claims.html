Updated 24 October 2026

Quick answer: Your insurance premium can rise even if you have never made a claim, because you are not only paying for your own risk; you are paying your share of the claims of everyone like you. When the cost of those claims goes up, everyone's price goes up. Between 2021 and 2025 the main drivers were the rising cost of repairing cars and rebuilding homes, more expensive parts and labour, more severe storms, floods and wildfires, higher reinsurance costs, car theft and, in the US, larger legal payouts. US car insurance prices rose by more than 45% between late 2021 and early 2024, and US home insurance premiums are up about 46% since 2021. Your own details matter too: a new address, a new car, a change in your credit-based insurance score in the US, or losing an introductory discount. In 2026 the pressure has eased in some markets, with UK car premiums lower than a year earlier and reinsurance prices falling, so this is a good time to shop around, check your details and ask your insurer to explain the increase.

Few bills cause as much frustration as an insurance renewal. You have driven carefully, looked after your home and never troubled your insurer, and yet the renewal letter asks for 15%, 20% or even 50% more. It feels like a penalty for loyalty, or simply unfair.

Some of it is unfair, and some practices that made it worse have been banned. But much of it reflects real changes in what insurers pay out. This guide explains how insurance prices are set, the reasons premiums have risen for people with clean records, what has changed in 2026, the rules that protect you in different countries, and the practical steps that can cut your bill.

How Insurance Pricing Works

Insurance is a pool. Thousands or millions of people pay premiums into it, and the insurer uses the money to pay the claims of the few who suffer a loss, along with its running costs. Most people in any year make no claim at all, which is exactly how the system is meant to work.

Your premium is an estimate of your share of the expected claims of people with similar characteristics to you: the same type of car, similar age and driving history, the same area, a similar home. Insurers group people into risk categories using these characteristics and set a price for each.

So your premium can rise for two broad reasons:

  • The cost of claims in your group has risen, even if you personally have not claimed.
  • Your details have changed, moving you into a different group.

Your no-claims record does matter, and usually earns you a discount. But a discount on a rising base price can still mean a higher bill.

Reason 1: Repairs Cost Much More

For car insurance, the biggest driver in recent years has been the cost of repairs. Modern cars are full of sensors, cameras and radar used for safety features such as automatic emergency braking and lane-keeping. A minor bump that once meant a new bumper now often means replacing and recalibrating expensive electronics. Replacing a windscreen with built-in cameras can cost more than $1,500 in the US.

Parts prices rose sharply after supply chain disruptions in 2021 and 2022, and many have stayed high. Skilled repair technicians are in short supply in many countries, pushing up labour costs and waiting times. Longer repair times also mean insurers pay for courtesy or rental cars for longer. Electric vehicles can be particularly expensive to repair, especially after damage to the battery.

In the UK, the average accidental damage claim on car insurance reached about £3,700 in early 2026, according to the Association of British Insurers. In the US, repair costs and used-car prices pushed car insurance prices up by more than 45% between late 2021 and early 2024, the fastest rise in decades.

Reason 2: Rebuilding Homes Costs More

Home insurance covers the cost of rebuilding your home and replacing your possessions, not the market value of the house. Building materials and labour costs rose steeply after 2020, so the cost of rebuilding the same house increased, and so did the sums insurers have to cover. Many policies automatically increase the rebuild value each year in line with construction costs, which raises the premium even before any other change.

Reason 3: Weather, Wildfire and Climate

Natural catastrophes have become more costly. Insured losses from natural disasters have exceeded $100 billion a year worldwide in recent years, driven by more people and property in exposed areas, higher rebuilding costs and, increasingly, more intense storms, floods and fires.

The US has been hit hardest. Hurricanes in Florida, severe convective storms and hail across the Midwest and South, and wildfires in California have caused huge losses. The Los Angeles wildfires of January 2025 were among the costliest wildfires ever, with insured losses estimated at about $40 billion. Some insurers have stopped writing new policies in parts of California and Florida, and state-backed insurers of last resort have grown and raised their prices.

In the UK, property insurance payouts reached a record £6.1 billion in 2025, including £1.6 billion from weather, according to the ABI. In Europe, floods in Germany, Belgium, Italy, Spain and central Europe have caused large losses since 2021.

When an area's risk is re-assessed, everyone there can see a rise, regardless of their own history. In the US, the federal flood insurance programme's Risk Rating 2.0, introduced from 2021, moved many homeowners towards prices based on their individual property's flood risk, with increases phased in each year.

Reason 4: Reinsurance

Insurers buy their own insurance, called reinsurance, to protect themselves against very large losses. After heavy catastrophe losses and rising interest rates, reinsurance prices rose sharply in 2023, and insurers passed the extra cost on to customers.

That has started to reverse. After several years without exceptionally large losses globally and with plenty of capital in the market, property catastrophe reinsurance rates fell by about 12% to 15% at the January 2026 renewals and further at mid-year, the steepest fall since the late 1990s. That should, over time, ease pressure on home insurance prices, although not everywhere and not immediately.

Reason 5: Theft, Fraud and Legal Costs

  • Car theft has risen in many countries. Keyless entry systems can be exploited with relay devices, and some models have been targeted by theft trends spread on social media. Models that are stolen often cost more to insure.
  • Fraud, from staged accidents to exaggerated claims, adds to everyone's costs. The UK insurance industry detects more than £1 billion of fraudulent claims a year.
  • Legal costs and larger court awards, sometimes called "social inflation", have pushed up the cost of liability claims in the US, especially for injuries in car accidents.
  • Medical costs for injury claims have risen with healthcare inflation.

Reason 6: Your Own Details Changed

Sometimes the increase reflects a change in your own risk profile, even if you have not claimed:

  • Moving home, to an area with more claims, theft or flood risk.
  • Changing your car, to one that is more powerful, more expensive to repair or more often stolen.
  • Age: premiums usually fall from the early twenties into middle age, then rise again for older drivers.
  • A new driver added to your policy.
  • Credit-based insurance scores: in most US states, insurers can use a version of your credit history to set home and car insurance prices. A drop in your credit can raise your premium. California, Hawaii and Massachusetts ban this for car insurance, and some other states restrict it.
  • Minor convictions or penalty points, such as speeding.
  • Claims by other people on your property, or an incident you reported but did not claim for; in many countries you must disclose accidents even if you did not claim.
  • End of an introductory discount from the first year of your policy.

Loyalty Penalties and the Rules Against Them

For years, many insurers charged long-standing customers more than new customers for the same cover, a practice known as "price walking". In the UK, the Financial Conduct Authority found that loyal customers were paying billions of pounds a year more than new ones, and banned the practice from January 2022: insurers must now offer renewing customers a price no higher than they would offer an equivalent new customer through the same channel.

The ban removed the worst loyalty penalties, but it did not stop renewal prices rising; it means that new-customer prices rose too. Shopping around still often finds a cheaper price, because insurers differ in how they price particular risks.

Several other countries and US states regulate how insurers can set or change prices, and many US states require insurers to justify rate increases to the state insurance regulator. Elsewhere, rules are lighter, and loyalty penalties can still be significant.

What Has Changed in 2026

After several years of sharp increases, the picture in 2026 is mixed:

  • UK car insurance prices fell from their 2024 peak; the ABI's average premium was about £560 in early 2026, lower than a year earlier, though repair costs remain high.
  • US car insurance is still rising, but slowly: around 1% in the first half of 2026, after falls in some states in 2025.
  • US home insurance premiums are expected to rise about 4% in 2026, after a 12% jump in 2025, with the largest increases in California.
  • UK home insurance premiums were slightly lower than a year earlier in mid-2026, but insurers are starting to raise rates again after record weather claims.
  • Reinsurance prices are falling, which should eventually feed through to home insurance prices.
  • Elsewhere in Europe, car insurance prices are still rising in countries such as France and Switzerland, where insurers cite repair costs and storm damage.

In short, if your premium rose sharply again at your 2026 renewal, it is more likely than in recent years that you can find a better price elsewhere.

How to Bring Your Premium Down

Shop around every year

Use comparison sites and get quotes directly from insurers that do not appear on them. Then call your current insurer, tell them what you have found, and ask whether they can match it. Insurers often have more room than the renewal letter suggests.

Check every detail

Make sure your mileage, job title, address, parking, security features and named drivers are correct. Overestimating your annual mileage, or listing a job title that sounds riskier than necessary, can cost you. Never give false information, though: it can invalidate your policy.

Raise your deductible or excess, carefully

Agreeing to pay more of any claim yourself lowers your premium. The calculator above shows how many claim-free years it takes for the savings to cover the higher amount you would pay. Only choose an amount you could afford to pay at short notice.

Pay annually if you can

Paying monthly often includes interest, which in the UK can add 10% or more to the cost.

Bundle, or unbundle

Buying home and car insurance from the same company can bring a discount, but it is not always the cheapest option. Compare both ways.

Consider telematics

"Black box" or app-based policies that monitor how you drive can reduce premiums for careful drivers, especially young ones.

Improve security and resilience

Approved alarms, locks, a garage, a steering lock for a commonly stolen car, or flood-resistant measures at home can lower premiums with some insurers.

Avoid small claims

Claiming for small amounts can cost you your no-claims discount and raise future premiums by more than the claim was worth. Keep insurance for losses you could not easily afford.

In the US, look after your credit

Where credit-based insurance scores are allowed, paying bills on time and keeping card balances low can help your insurance prices too.

Are Insurers Simply Making More Profit?

It is a fair question, and the answer varies by year and market. Insurers measure their underwriting performance with the "combined ratio": claims plus expenses, divided by premiums. A ratio above 100% means they paid out more than they collected in premiums, before counting investment income.

In 2022 and 2023, many car insurers in the US and UK reported combined ratios well above 100%, meaning they were losing money on the policies they sold, because claim costs rose faster than prices. The large premium increases of 2023 and 2024 were partly an effort to catch up. By 2025, profitability had recovered for many car insurers, which is one reason prices have stabilised and, in the UK, started to fall.

Home insurance has been harder. Deloitte forecast a combined ratio of about 102% for UK home insurers in 2026, after record weather claims, and several US home insurers have lost money in states with frequent disasters. Higher interest rates since 2022 have helped insurers' investment income, which offsets some underwriting losses.

Competition is the best protection against excessive profits. When insurers are making good margins, new competitors and price cuts follow, which is why comparing quotes matters most when the market is turning, as it is in parts of 2026.

Health, Travel and Other Policies

The same logic applies to other kinds of insurance, with their own drivers:

  • Health insurance premiums follow medical costs, which have risen faster than general inflation in most countries for decades, driven by new treatments, ageing populations and higher use of care. In the US, employer health premiums for family cover passed $25,000 a year in 2024. In countries with private health insurance alongside public systems, such as Australia, the UK and Ireland, longer public waiting lists have increased demand and claims.
  • Travel insurance prices reflect medical costs abroad, cancellations and disruption. They rose after the pandemic, when cancellation claims surged.
  • Pet insurance premiums often rise sharply each year as pets age and veterinary costs climb.
  • Life insurance is different: a fixed-term policy usually locks in the price for the whole term, so it does not rise unless you change your cover.

For all of them, the questions are the same: what has changed in the cost of claims, what has changed about you, and can someone else offer the same cover for less?

Ask Your Insurer to Explain

You are entitled to ask why your premium has gone up. A good insurer will tell you whether the increase reflects general market costs, a change to your details, a change in your area's risk rating or a discount ending. In the US, if your premium rises because of information in your credit report, the insurer must tell you and explain how to get a free copy.

You can also check what claims history insurers can see. In the US, the CLUE database records claims on your home and car; in the UK, the Claims and Underwriting Exchange (CUE) does the same. Errors are not unusual and can be corrected.

If you believe you have been treated unfairly, complain first to the insurer, then to an independent body: the Financial Ombudsman Service in the UK, your state insurance department in the US, or the equivalent ombudsman or regulator in your country.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did my premium go up when I have a no-claims discount?

Because the base price for people like you has risen, mainly due to higher repair, rebuilding and catastrophe costs. Your discount still makes you pay less than someone with claims, but it is applied to a higher starting price.

Is it worth switching insurer every year?

It is worth comparing every year. Switching often saves money, but loyalty can occasionally bring benefits such as protected discounts. In the UK, the price-walking ban means renewal and new-customer prices should be the same for the same channel, but different insurers can still offer very different prices.

Will making a claim raise my premium?

Usually, yes, for several years, especially for at-fault car claims. That is why it is often better not to claim for small losses.

Why is insurance so expensive where I live?

Premiums reflect local claims experience: theft, accidents, flooding, storms, wildfire and the cost of repairs in your area. Areas with frequent or severe losses cost more to insure.

Can an insurer refuse to renew my policy?

Generally yes, at renewal, subject to local rules. Some US states limit non-renewals after disasters. If you cannot find cover, state-backed insurers of last resort exist in many US states, and schemes such as Flood Re in the UK help homes at high flood risk get affordable cover.

Should I protect my no-claims discount?

Protection lets you make one or two claims without losing your discount, for an extra fee. It can be worth it if your discount is large, but it does not stop your base premium rising after a claim, so compare the extra cost with what you would save.

Do electric cars cost more to insure?

Often, yes, because repairs and battery damage can be expensive, and specialist repairers are fewer. Prices are falling as repair networks grow.

Will premiums fall in 2027?

Possibly in some markets, especially for home insurance, as reinsurance becomes cheaper, provided there are no exceptionally costly disasters. Car insurance depends mainly on repair costs, which remain high.

Final Thoughts

A higher premium without a claim feels like a penalty for being careful, but it usually reflects the rising cost of the pool you belong to: dearer repairs, costlier rebuilds, more severe weather and more expensive reinsurance. None of that is your fault, and some of it is now easing.

What you can control is how much of it you pay. Compare prices every year, check your details, choose a sensible deductible, avoid small claims and ask your insurer to explain any increase. Those few steps can save hundreds of dollars, pounds or euros a year, and give you a clearer view of what you are actually paying for.
