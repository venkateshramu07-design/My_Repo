Quick answer: Take the monthly pension if you want guaranteed income for life, expect to live at least an average lifespan, value protection against running out of money, and the pension is secure. Take the lump sum if you are in poor health, have other reliable income, are confident managing investments, want to leave money to heirs, or have doubts about the pension scheme's finances. The key number is the implied payout rate: divide the yearly pension by the lump sum. If a $250,000 lump sum is offered in place of $18,000 a year from age 65, that is 7.2% a year for life, which is difficult to match safely by investing the lump sum yourself. When interest rates are high, as in 2026, lump sum offers tend to be smaller and annuities pay more, which usually tilts the decision towards the monthly pension. Get the details in writing, check whether the pension rises with inflation and what it pays to a surviving spouse, and in the UK and some other countries, take regulated advice before giving up a guaranteed pension.

Many people approaching retirement face a choice that cannot be undone: take a pension as a regular income for the rest of their life, or take its value as one large payment. Employers offer this choice for traditional "defined benefit" pensions in the US, the UK, Canada and elsewhere; in other systems, people choose between buying an annuity with their savings and drawing them down gradually.

It is one of the largest financial decisions most people ever make, and the lump sum is often far more money than they have seen in one place. That makes it tempting. This guide explains how the two options compare, the questions that decide the answer, the effect of interest rates and inflation, how to calculate a break-even age, and the mistakes to avoid.

What You Are Really Choosing Between

A monthly pension is an income stream, usually guaranteed for life, sometimes rising with inflation and often continuing at a reduced rate to a surviving spouse. A lump sum is the scheme's estimate of what that stream is worth today, paid in one go.

The choice comes down to who carries two big risks:

  • Longevity risk: the risk of living longer than your money lasts. With a pension, the scheme carries it. With a lump sum, you do.
  • Investment risk: the risk that investments perform badly, especially early in retirement. With a pension, the scheme carries it. With a lump sum, you do.

In return for taking on those risks, a lump sum gives you flexibility, control and the possibility of leaving money to your family.

How Lump Sums Are Calculated

A pension scheme calculates the lump sum by estimating the payments it would otherwise make for the rest of your life, and then "discounting" them back to today using an interest rate and a mortality table.

The interest rate matters enormously. The higher the rate, the less money the scheme needs today to fund the same future payments, so the smaller the lump sum. In the US, private pension lump sums are calculated using corporate bond rates set by the IRS under section 417(e) of the tax code. Those rates rose sharply from 2022, cutting lump sum values by a fifth or more for many people approaching retirement, and they remain relatively high in 2026, with the main segment rates around 4.5% to 5.5% in mid-2026.

So when interest rates are high, lump sums are less generous, and income from the alternative, buying an annuity, is more generous. When rates are low, the reverse applies.

The Payout Rate: A Simple First Test

Divide the annual pension by the lump sum. That is the implied payout rate, the income you get per dollar of lump sum given up.

Example: a pension of $1,500 a month ($18,000 a year) or a lump sum of $250,000. The payout rate is 18,000 ÷ 250,000 = 7.2%.

Then ask: could I safely draw 7.2% a year from $250,000 for the rest of my life? Most retirement research suggests that a sustainable withdrawal rate from an investment portfolio is closer to 4% to 5% a year, rising with inflation, if you want a high chance of the money lasting 30 years. So a 7.2% guaranteed payout for life is very hard to match. If the payout rate were 4%, the lump sum would look much more attractive.

As a rough comparison, in mid-2026 a healthy 65-year-old in the UK could buy a level annuity paying about 7.7% a year, for example about £7,750 a year for £100,000, close to an 18-year high. US annuity rates for the same age were in a similar range. If your pension's payout rate is above the market annuity rate, the pension is offering good value.

Break-Even Age

A simple break-even calculation asks how long you would need to live for the monthly payments to add up to more than the lump sum. Using the example above, $250,000 ÷ $18,000 = about 13.9 years. If payments start at 65, you break even at about 79.

That understates the pension, because it ignores what the lump sum could earn if invested. A better comparison asks how long the lump sum would last if you invested it and withdrew the same income. At a 5% return, withdrawing $18,000 a year from $250,000 runs the money out after about 24 years, at around age 89. If you live longer than that, the pension wins; if you die sooner, the lump sum would have left money for your heirs.

Life expectancy matters. In the US, a 65-year-old can expect to live to about 83 if male and about 86 if female, on average, and many people live much longer. For a married couple aged 65, there is roughly an even chance that at least one will live past 90. Planning for the average is risky, because half of people outlive it.

Reasons to Choose the Monthly Pension

  • Guaranteed income for life, however long you live and whatever happens in markets.
  • Protection against behavioural mistakes, such as spending too fast, panicking in a market crash or falling for a scam. Studies of people who take lump sums find that some spend them much faster than planned.
  • Good health and family longevity. If your parents lived into their nineties, the pension's insurance value is high.
  • Inflation protection, if the pension rises with prices. Inflation-linked annuities are expensive to buy privately, so a pension with built-in increases is especially valuable.
  • Survivor benefits. A joint pension that continues to your spouse provides security for both of you.
  • Simplicity, which becomes more valuable as people age and managing money gets harder.
  • A good payout rate, especially when it beats what you could buy on the open market.

Reasons to Choose the Lump Sum

  • Poor health or a family history of shorter lifespans, which reduces the value of lifetime income.
  • Other secure income. If Social Security, a state pension or another pension already covers your essential spending, you may need less guaranteed income and can afford to invest.
  • Leaving money to heirs. A pension usually ends when you (and any spouse) die; a lump sum that is not spent can be passed on.
  • Doubts about the pension scheme. If the employer is in financial trouble and the pension is not fully protected, a lump sum removes that risk.
  • Flexibility for large expenses, such as paying off a mortgage or helping family, although spending a large share early defeats the purpose of a pension.
  • Investment experience and discipline, and a willingness to accept market ups and downs.
  • A low payout rate, especially when interest rates were low when the lump sum was calculated.

Inflation: The Hidden Difference

Check carefully whether the monthly pension rises each year. A pension that is fixed in money terms loses purchasing power steadily. At 3% inflation, $1,500 a month buys only about $1,100 worth of today's goods after 10 years, and about $830 after 20 years.

Many public sector pensions and UK private pensions rise with inflation, often with a cap. Most US private pensions do not. If your pension is fixed, compare it with a lump sum invested in a way that can grow, and plan to use other savings to offset rising prices. If it is inflation-linked, its value is considerably higher than a fixed pension with the same starting amount.

How Safe Is the Pension?

The security of the promise is central to the decision.

  • United States: private pensions are insured by the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC) up to annual limits. For a single-employer plan, the maximum guarantee for someone retiring at 65 in 2025 was about $7,400 a month, enough to cover most people's pensions in full. Multi-employer plans have much lower guarantees.
  • United Kingdom: the Pension Protection Fund (PPF) pays compensation if an employer fails, generally 100% for people already over the scheme's pension age and 90% for younger members, subject to a cap.
  • Canada: protection depends on the province; Ontario has a guarantee fund, most others do not.
  • Public sector pensions are backed by governments and are generally very secure, though benefits can be changed by law.

If your employer is financially weak and your pension exceeds the protection limits, the lump sum looks more attractive.

Taxes

Tax rules can strongly favour one option.

  • United States: a lump sum paid directly to you is taxed as ordinary income in the year you receive it, which could push you into a high bracket, and before 59½ there may be a 10% penalty. A direct rollover to an IRA avoids immediate tax, and you pay tax only on what you withdraw later. Monthly pension payments are taxed as income as you receive them.
  • United Kingdom: you can usually take 25% of a defined contribution pension tax-free, up to a lifetime limit of £268,275, with the rest taxed as income. Transferring out of a defined benefit pension worth more than £30,000 requires regulated financial advice, and many advisers recommend against it.
  • Elsewhere: Australia generally does not tax pension income or lump sums after age 60 from taxed funds. In India, part of a government pension can be "commuted" into a tax-free lump sum. Rules vary widely, so check your own.

A Middle Path

The choice does not have to be all or nothing. Options include:

  • Partial lump sums. Some schemes let you take part of the value as cash and the rest as a smaller pension.
  • Taking the pension and investing other savings, so that guaranteed income covers essential spending and investments cover the rest.
  • Taking the lump sum and buying an annuity with part of it, if the market annuity rate is better than the scheme's payout rate, which is rare.
  • Delaying Social Security or a state pension while using a lump sum to bridge the gap. Each year of delay in US Social Security after full retirement age increases the benefit by 8%, which is one of the best guaranteed returns available.

Financial planners often suggest covering essential spending, such as housing, food, utilities and insurance, with guaranteed income from pensions and state benefits, and using investments for everything else.

If You Have Savings, Not a Pension

Many people never receive a defined benefit pension. Instead, they retire with a pot of savings in a 401(k), an IRA, a UK personal pension, Australian superannuation or a similar account, and face a related choice: buy an annuity that pays a guaranteed income for life, or keep the money invested and draw it down gradually.

The same logic applies. An annuity converts a lump sum into lifetime income, transferring longevity and investment risk to an insurer. Drawdown keeps flexibility and the chance of leaving money to heirs, but leaves you exposed to markets and to living longer than expected. Annuity rates rise with interest rates and with age: a 75-year-old receives a considerably higher rate than a 65-year-old, because the insurer expects to pay for fewer years.

That is why some people combine the two: they draw down their savings in their sixties, when flexibility matters most, and buy an annuity with part of what remains in their seventies, when rates are higher and protection against a very long life becomes more valuable. People with health conditions, or who smoke, can often get "enhanced" annuities that pay more. Always compare quotes from several insurers, because rates for the same person can differ significantly.

A Worked Example for a Couple

Consider a married couple, both 65. One is offered either a lump sum of $300,000 or a joint pension of $1,650 a month, which continues at two-thirds to the surviving spouse. They also expect $3,200 a month in combined Social Security.

The payout rate on the pension is $19,800 ÷ $300,000 = 6.6% a year, for two lives. Buying a joint annuity with similar survivor terms on the open market would typically pay somewhat less. Their essential spending is about $4,500 a month, so the pension plus Social Security covers it with room to spare, leaving their other savings free for travel, home repairs and gifts to family.

If instead they took the lump sum, they would need to withdraw about 6.6% a year to replace the income, well above the 4% to 5% usually considered sustainable, and their essential bills would depend on markets. For this couple, the pension is the stronger choice. If one of them had a serious illness and they had large savings elsewhere, the answer might well be different.

Common Mistakes

  • Judging by the size of the cheque. A six-figure sum feels like more than a modest monthly payment, but often it is not.
  • Underestimating how long you might live. Many people plan only to average life expectancy.
  • Ignoring the spouse. Choosing a single-life pension to get a higher payment can leave a surviving spouse with nothing. In the US, a spouse must consent in writing to waive survivor benefits.
  • Cashing out instead of rolling over, paying unnecessary tax.
  • Trusting an adviser paid to manage the lump sum. Someone who earns fees on investments has an incentive to recommend the lump sum. Seek advice from a fee-only adviser who is not paid by results.
  • Deciding under deadline pressure. Employers often offer lump sums for a limited period. Ask for the documents early.

Questions to Ask Your Pension Scheme

  1. What exactly is the monthly pension at each possible start date?
  2. Does it increase each year, and by how much?
  3. What happens to a surviving spouse, and what are the joint options?
  4. How was the lump sum calculated, and with what interest rate?
  5. Can I take part as a lump sum and part as a pension?
  6. What is the deadline, and what happens if I do nothing?
  7. Is the scheme fully funded, and what guarantees apply if the employer fails?

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it better to take a pension lump sum or monthly payments?

For most people in reasonable health who need the income, monthly payments are safer and often better value, especially when interest rates are high. The lump sum suits people with shorter life expectancy, other secure income, a wish to leave money, or doubts about the scheme.

What is a good payout rate?

Compare the pension's payout rate with market annuity rates for your age and with sustainable withdrawal rates of about 4% to 5% from investments. A payout rate of 7% or more at 65 is generous by most standards; one below 5% is low.

Why did my lump sum offer go down?

Most likely because interest rates rose. Higher rates reduce the present value of future payments. Changes in mortality tables can also have a small effect.

Can I change my mind after choosing?

Usually not. Once a pension has started or a lump sum has been paid, the decision is generally permanent.

What if I die soon after taking the pension?

Unless you chose a joint or guaranteed-period option, payments may stop. Many schemes offer a guarantee that pays for at least 5 or 10 years, or a reduced pension to a spouse, in exchange for a slightly lower payment.

Should I take the lump sum to pay off my mortgage?

Only after comparing the mortgage rate with the pension's payout rate and considering your other income. Giving up a 7% lifetime income to repay a 3% mortgage is usually a poor trade.

Does taking the lump sum affect my state pension or Social Security?

Usually not. State pensions and Social Security are based on your contribution record, not on how you take a workplace pension. The lump sum can, however, affect taxes on your benefits or means-tested support in some countries.

Is an annuity the same as a pension?

An annuity is a product you buy from an insurer that pays income for life or a set period. A defined benefit pension works similarly but is provided by an employer. The comparison between them is useful for judging whether a lump sum offer is fair.

Final Thoughts

The lump sum is visible, tangible and flexible; the pension is quieter and harder to appreciate until you are 88 and still receiving it. For most people, the most valuable thing a pension provides is not the money in any particular year but the certainty that the money will keep coming, however long they live and whatever markets do.

Work out the payout rate, consider your health and your spouse, check whether the pension rises with inflation and how secure it is, and compare it with what the lump sum could realistically provide. Take your time, take independent advice if the sums are large, and choose the option that lets you sleep well for the next thirty years.
