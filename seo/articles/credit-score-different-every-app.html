Quick answer: Your credit score differs between apps because there is no single credit score. You have a separate credit file at each credit bureau, and dozens of scoring models turn those files into numbers, each with its own formula and sometimes its own scale. A free app might show a VantageScore based on two bureaus, your bank a FICO Score 8 from a third, and a mortgage lender three older FICO versions from all three bureaus. Gaps of 20 to 40 points are normal. In the US, FICO says its scores are used in about 90% of lending decisions; mortgage lenders still mostly use the older "Classic FICO" versions and take the middle of your three scores. In the UK, lenders do not use the score you see in an app at all; they run your credit file through their own systems. The number that matters is the one your lender uses, and the best way to improve all of them is the same: pay on time, keep card balances low and check your reports for errors.

Almost everyone who checks their credit score more than once has the same experience. One app says 742. The bank's app says 718. A credit card company emails to say 731. Then a mortgage broker pulls your file and comes back with 704. It is natural to suspect that one of them is wrong, or that the free apps are just guessing.

In fact, all of those numbers can be accurate at the same time. They are different measurements, taken with different instruments, from slightly different raw material. This guide explains where the differences come from, which score lenders use for which kind of loan in the US, the UK, Canada, Australia and Europe, when a big gap is a warning sign, and how to raise the score that actually decides your interest rate.

There Is No Single Credit Score

The phrase "your credit score" suggests a single official number, like your height or your date of birth. Nothing like that exists in most countries. What exists instead is a chain with three links:

  1. Your lenders report how you handle your accounts: balances, limits, payments, missed payments, new accounts and closed ones.
  2. Credit bureaus (also called credit reference agencies) collect those reports into a credit file. In the US the three national bureaus are Equifax, Experian and TransUnion; the UK has the same three; Canada has Equifax and TransUnion.
  3. Scoring models read a credit file and turn it into a number that predicts how likely you are to fall seriously behind on payments, usually over the next two years.

A difference can creep in at every link. Not every lender reports to every bureau, so your files differ. And there are many models, each with several versions in use at once, so the same file produces different numbers. Put those together and a person with three files and a dozen models in circulation can easily have thirty or more legitimate credit scores.

Reason 1: The Apps Use Different Scoring Models

In the US, two companies dominate scoring. FICO (originally Fair, Isaac and Company) has been selling scores since 1989. VantageScore was created in 2006 by the three bureaus themselves as a competitor. Both use the familiar 300 to 850 range, which makes them look interchangeable. They are not.

The models look at the same broad categories (payment history, how much you owe, how long you have had credit, new applications and your mix of accounts) but weigh them differently and treat particular situations differently. A few examples:

  • Thin files. VantageScore can score people with as little as one account and a short history; many FICO versions need at least one account that is six months old and one reported in the last six months. Someone new to credit may have a VantageScore and no FICO Score at all.
  • Paid collections. Newer models, such as FICO 9 and VantageScore 3.0 and 4.0, ignore collection accounts that have been paid. Older FICO versions still count them.
  • Medical debt. Newer models give less weight to unpaid medical collections than older ones.
  • Trended data. VantageScore 4.0 and FICO 10T look at how your balances have moved over about 24 months, rewarding people who pay balances down, while older models only see a snapshot.

The free US apps usually show a VantageScore. Credit Karma, for example, has long displayed VantageScore 3.0 based on your TransUnion and Equifax files. Many banks and card issuers show a FICO Score 8 from one bureau, and myFICO sells a range of versions. So two apps can disagree simply because they are running different formulas.

Reason 2: Each Bureau Has a Slightly Different File on You

Lenders are not required to report to all three bureaus, and many smaller lenders, credit unions and buy now, pay later providers report to only one or two. Even when they report to all three, they may send their updates on different days. So at any moment your Experian file might include a new car loan that your Equifax file has not yet received, or a store card that TransUnion never hears about.

Collections agencies, landlords and utilities add to the patchwork, because their reporting is even less consistent. The result is that the same scoring model applied to your three files on the same day can give three different scores. This is the reason US mortgage lenders pull all three.

Reason 3: Your Score Changes Every Time Your File Does

A credit score is not stored anywhere. It is calculated at the moment someone asks for it, from whatever your file shows at that moment. Your file changes constantly, mostly because card issuers report a new balance every month.

The balance they report is usually the one on your statement, not the one after you pay. If you spend $2,500 on a card with a $5,000 limit and pay it all off a week after the statement date, the bureaus may still see 50% of your limit in use, and your score can drop by tens of points until the next statement. An app that updated last week and one that updated yesterday will show different numbers even if you have done nothing unusual. This timing effect explains a large share of the "my score fell 30 points for no reason" complaints.

Reason 4: Lenders Use Industry-Specific Scores

FICO sells versions tuned for particular kinds of lending. FICO Auto Scores give extra weight to how you have handled car loans, and FICO Bankcard Scores focus on credit cards. These industry scores run from 250 to 900, not 300 to 850, so the number a car dealer quotes may not even be on the same scale as the one in your app. Someone with a perfect car-loan record but a messy card history might have an auto score well above their general score; the reverse is also common.

Reason 5: Educational Scores and Different Scales

Some apps and websites show a score that the provider built only for consumers, sometimes called an "educational" score. Lenders never use it. It can be useful for tracking whether your credit is improving, but it is not what anyone will look at when you apply. Credit professionals jokingly call these "FAKO" scores. The provider should say what model a score is; if it does not, assume it is a consumer score, not a lending one.

Which Score Do US Lenders Actually Use?

This is the question most people really want answered, and it depends on what you are borrowing for.

Mortgages

For years, mortgages sold to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac (most US home loans) have required what the industry calls Classic FICO: FICO Score 2 from Experian, FICO Score 5 from Equifax and FICO Score 4 from TransUnion. These are versions from the late 1990s and early 2000s. The lender pulls all three, a "tri-merge", and uses the middle score. If both partners apply, many lenders use the lower of the two middle scores.

That is changing slowly. In 2025 and 2026 the Federal Housing Finance Agency, which oversees Fannie and Freddie, approved the newer VantageScore 4.0 and FICO 10T for these loans, and in April 2026 it said approved lenders could begin using VantageScore 4.0 in a limited rollout, with FICO 10T to follow. For now, most mortgage lenders still use Classic FICO, which is why a mortgage score is often lower than the newer scores in your apps: the old models are harsher on paid collections and medical debt.

Credit cards and personal loans

FICO Score 8 is the most widely used general-purpose score, and many card issuers also use FICO Bankcard Scores or VantageScore. Banks often build their own models on top, using your income and your history with them.

Car loans

Auto lenders frequently use FICO Auto Score 8 or 9, sometimes from just one bureau. If you are shopping for a car loan, it is worth asking the lender which score and which bureau it pulls.

Renting and other checks

Landlords, mobile phone companies and insurers may use any of these, a tenant-screening score, or no score at all, just your report. In most US states, insurers can use a credit-based insurance score, which is different again.

The UK: Lenders Do Not Use Your App Score

The UK works differently, and the difference surprises many people. The scores you see in apps such as Experian, ClearScore (which uses Equifax data) or Credit Karma UK (TransUnion) are produced for consumers. Lenders do not receive them. Instead, a bank looks at your credit file from one or more agencies and runs it through its own scoring system, combined with what you put on the application and, often, your history with that bank.

Experian moved its UK score from a 0 to 999 scale to 0 to 1,250 from November 2025, and added data such as rent payments. It said at the time that this did not change anyone's eligibility for credit, because lenders were never using the number. A UK app score is best thought of as a rough guide to how lenders are likely to see your file. A high one is a good sign; a low one tells you where to look. Being registered on the electoral roll at your current address is one of the simplest ways to improve how UK lenders see you.

Canada, Australia and Europe

Canada

Canada has two bureaus, Equifax and TransUnion, both using a 300 to 900 range. A gap of 20 to 50 points between them is common. Mortgage lenders often look at Equifax's Beacon score, and most large banks use their own internal scores alongside it.

Australia

Australia moved to "comprehensive credit reporting" in recent years, which means positive information such as on-time payments is now included, not only defaults. Equifax scores run from 0 to 1,200, and other bureaus use different scales, so the same person might score 800 on one and 700 on another without either being wrong. Lenders combine bureau data with their own assessment and income checks.

Germany and the rest of Europe

In Germany, SCHUFA replaced its old percentage score with a points score from 100 to 999 in March 2026, and lets people see how it is calculated for the first time. Many European countries rely far more on negative information (defaults, court judgments) and less on scores; in France, for example, the central bank keeps a register of payment incidents rather than a general consumer score. If you move to Europe from the US, your FICO history does not travel with you, and you may need to build credit again from scratch.

How Big a Gap Is Normal?

As a rough guide:

  • Up to about 40 points between models or bureaus is ordinary and usually reflects formula and timing differences.
  • 40 to 80 points is common when one model treats something in your file, such as a paid collection or a recent spike in card balances, very differently from another.
  • More than about 80 points, or one bureau showing a missed payment or account the others do not, is worth investigating. It may be an error or, occasionally, a sign of identity fraud.

Watch the direction of travel as well as the level. If every score you can see is rising slowly, your credit is improving, whatever the individual numbers. If one falls sharply while the others stay still, something has changed in that one file.

Check Your Reports, Not Just Your Scores

A score is only as accurate as the file behind it, and files are not always accurate. A landmark US Federal Trade Commission study found that about one in five consumers had an error on at least one of their three reports, and about one in twenty had errors serious enough to push them into a worse pricing tier. Common problems include accounts belonging to someone with a similar name, payments marked late that were on time, closed accounts shown as open and debts listed twice.

Where to check for free:

  • United States: AnnualCreditReport.com, the official site, gives free weekly reports from all three bureaus.
  • United Kingdom: each agency must provide a free statutory report, and apps such as ClearScore, Experian's free tier and Credit Karma UK show your file at no cost.
  • Canada: Equifax and TransUnion both offer free online access to your report.
  • Australia: you can get a free report from each bureau every three months.

If you find an error, dispute it directly with the bureau that shows it, in writing or online, with evidence. In the US, bureaus generally have 30 days to investigate. It is also worth contacting the lender that reported it, since the bureau will ask them to confirm.

Does Checking Your Score Lower It?

No. Checking your own score or report is a "soft" inquiry and has no effect on any score. Lenders can see some soft searches in the UK, but they do not count against you. What can lower your score slightly is a "hard" inquiry, when you actually apply for credit.

Even then, the effect is usually small, a few points, and fades within a year. US scoring models also treat several applications for the same kind of loan within a short period as one, so that people can shop around. Newer FICO versions use a 45-day window for mortgage, car and student loans; older FICO versions and VantageScore use 14 days. In the UK, use lenders' eligibility checkers, which use soft searches, before making a full application.

How to Raise the Score That Matters

The good news is that every model rewards the same behaviour. You do not need to target one formula.

  1. Never miss a payment. Payment history is the biggest single factor in FICO scores, around 35% of the calculation. Set up automatic minimum payments so a forgotten bill never becomes a late mark, which can stay on your file for up to seven years in the US and six in the UK.
  2. Keep card balances low when the statement closes. Amounts owed, especially credit card utilisation, are roughly 30% of a FICO Score. Under 30% of your limits is the usual rule of thumb; people with the highest scores typically use less than 10%. Paying down a balance before the statement date, rather than after, is one of the quickest ways to raise a score.
  3. Keep old accounts open if they cost nothing. They lengthen your history and add to your available credit.
  4. Apply for new credit sparingly, and shop for big loans within a short window.
  5. Dispute errors as soon as you find them.
  6. Use rent and utility reporting where it is offered. Newer models in the US and Experian in the UK can now count on-time rent payments, which helps people with thin files.

The utilisation calculator above shows how much you would need to pay down to get under the 30% and 10% thresholds, overall and on your fullest card, which models also look at.

Before You Apply for a Big Loan

If you are planning to apply for a mortgage or car loan in the next few months:

  • Check all three US reports (or every agency in your country) at least two months ahead, so there is time to fix errors.
  • Ask the lender which score and bureau it uses. A mortgage lender will usually use Classic FICO; you can buy those versions from myFICO if you want to know where you stand.
  • Pay card balances down before their statement dates in the month or two before you apply.
  • Avoid opening new accounts or financing large purchases until the loan has closed.
  • Do not close old cards right before applying, since that cuts your available credit and raises utilisation.

In the US, a higher score can change a mortgage rate meaningfully. Lenders price in tiers, often in steps of 20 points, so moving from 699 to 720 can matter more than moving from 760 to 780.

What a "Good" Score Means

Labels such as "good" and "excellent" vary between providers, but for US FICO Scores a widely used guide is: below 580 poor, 580 to 669 fair, 670 to 739 good, 740 to 799 very good, and 800 or above exceptional. The average US FICO Score was 714 in spring 2026, according to FICO, a little below its 2023 peak. Above about 760, most lenders treat applicants alike, so the benefit of each extra point shrinks.

In other countries, compare your score only with that agency's own bands. A good score with one UK agency might be a number that would look poor on another agency's scale.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Credit Karma accurate?

It accurately shows the score it says it shows, usually a VantageScore based on your TransUnion and Equifax files. The issue is that many lenders use FICO Scores, often from different bureaus or versions, so the number a lender sees can be higher or lower. It is useful for tracking trends and spotting changes in your files.

Why is my mortgage score lower than my app score?

Most US mortgage lenders still use Classic FICO versions from the early 2000s, which treat paid collections and medical debt more harshly and do not use trended data. They also take the middle of your three scores, which is lower than your best one.

Which bureau is the most accurate?

None is more accurate in general. Each is only as accurate as the information lenders send it, and most lenders report to all three. The bureau that matters is the one your lender pulls.

Do I have a credit score if I have never borrowed?

Probably not, or only a VantageScore. In the US you usually need at least one account that is six months old for a FICO Score. A secured card, a credit-builder loan or being added as an authorised user on a relative's card are common ways to start.

Does my US credit score follow me to another country?

No. Credit files are national, so you usually start again when you move. A few card issuers and services can use your foreign credit history, and keeping one card open in your old country can help if you return.

Can I buy the exact score my lender uses?

In the US, yes, in most cases. myFICO sells most FICO versions, including those used for mortgages and car loans. In the UK, no, because lenders use their own internal scoring.

How long does it take to improve a credit score?

Lowering card balances can lift a score within one or two statement cycles. Recovering from missed payments or defaults takes much longer, since they stay on your file for six to seven years, though their effect fades over time.

Final Thoughts

Different numbers in different apps are not a sign that something is broken. They are a sign that "credit score" is a family of measurements, taken from several files, by models of different ages, for lenders with different needs. Getting worried about each individual reading is a little like worrying that the bathroom scales and the doctor's scales disagree by a pound.

What matters is the information underneath. If your reports are accurate, your payments are on time and your balances are modest at statement time, every model will rate you well, and the particular number a lender sees will look after itself.
