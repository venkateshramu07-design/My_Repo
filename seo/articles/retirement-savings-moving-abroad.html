Quick answer: What happens to my 401(k) if I move abroad? When you move to another country, your retirement savings do not move with you automatically, and in most cases they do not need to. State pensions you have earned stay yours and can usually be paid abroad, although some countries, such as the UK, freeze the amount if you retire to certain places. Workplace and personal pensions, like a US 401(k), a UK pension or Australian superannuation, can generally stay where they are and keep growing, and you draw them later from wherever you live. Moving them is possible in some cases but often triggers taxes or charges, and cashing them out early can cost a third or more in tax and penalties. The bigger risks are in taxes: the account that is tax-free at home, such as a Roth IRA, a UK ISA or a Canadian TFSA, may be taxable in your new country. Before you leave, record what you have, check your new country's tax treatment and any social security agreement, and keep contribution records. Do not cash out in a hurry.

Moving abroad for a job, for love or for retirement is one of the biggest financial changes a person can make, and pensions are usually the last thing anyone thinks about while packing. That is a mistake, but often not for the reason people expect. The danger is rarely that you lose your pension. It is that you make a hasty decision to cash it out, transfer it into an expensive product, or keep using an account whose tax benefits quietly disappeared when you crossed the border.

This guide explains the three kinds of retirement money most people have, what typically happens to each when you move, how social security agreements work, the specific rules for the US, UK, Canada, Australia, the EU and others, and a checklist for before and after you go.

Three Kinds of Retirement Money

Almost every country's retirement system has three layers, and each behaves differently when you move:

  1. State pensions funded by social security contributions, such as US Social Security, the UK State Pension, the Canada Pension Plan or Germany's statutory pension. You earn entitlement by working and paying contributions.
  2. Workplace and personal pensions, where money is invested in your name: a 401(k) or IRA in the US, a workplace or personal pension in the UK, superannuation in Australia, an RRSP in Canada, a provident fund in Singapore, Hong Kong or India.
  3. Other tax-advantaged savings that people use for retirement, such as a UK ISA, a US Roth IRA or a Canadian TFSA. These are often treated differently by other countries' tax systems.

State Pensions: Earned Rights You Usually Keep

Most countries let you keep the state pension entitlement you built up, even if you leave. You claim it when you reach that country's pension age, and it can usually be paid into a foreign bank account. US Social Security, for example, can be paid to American citizens in most countries, with a few exceptions such as Cuba and North Korea.

The catch is that you often need a minimum number of years to qualify. You need 40 "credits", roughly ten years of work, for US Social Security, and at least ten qualifying years for any UK State Pension. Someone who works for six years in each of three countries could, on that basis alone, qualify for none of them.

Social security agreements

To solve that problem, many countries sign social security agreements, which the US calls "totalization agreements". They typically do two things:

  • Prevent double contributions: if your employer sends you abroad temporarily, you usually keep paying into your home system only.
  • Add up periods of work: years worked in each country count towards the minimum needed to qualify in the other. Each country then pays a pension based on the contributions made there.

The US has agreements with about 30 countries, including Canada, the UK, Germany, France, Japan, South Korea and Australia. Within the European Union, rules on social security coordination do the same job for all member states plus Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein and Switzerland: periods worked across the EU are combined, and each country pays its share. Many countries have no agreement with each other, however, so it is worth checking the specific pair you are moving between.

One US change helps people with foreign pensions: the Social Security Fairness Act, signed in January 2025, repealed the "windfall elimination provision", which had reduced US Social Security for many people who also received a pension from foreign work.

The UK's Frozen Pension Problem

The UK State Pension can be paid anywhere in the world, but it only rises each year if you live in the UK, the European Economic Area, Switzerland, Gibraltar or a country with a social security agreement that requires increases, such as the US. In most other countries, including Canada, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa, it is frozen at the amount you first received, or the amount when you moved there if later.

Around 450,000 to 500,000 British pensioners abroad have frozen pensions, most of them in Australia and Canada. Over a long retirement, the loss can be large: a pension frozen at age 67 can be worth far less than half of the uprated UK amount by the time someone is in their nineties.

The UK also changed the rules on voluntary contributions from April 2026. People abroad can no longer pay the cheaper Class 2 contributions to fill gaps in their record and must pay the more expensive Class 3 contributions instead, and only if they have lived in the UK or paid contributions for at least ten years. Paying voluntary contributions can still be one of the best-value ways to increase a state pension, so check your record on the UK government website before deciding.

Workplace and Personal Pensions: Usually Best Left Where They Are

For most people, the simplest and cheapest choice is to leave a workplace or personal pension in the country where it was built, keep it invested, and draw it in retirement. The money remains yours, and you can usually manage it online from anywhere. The reasons to leave it include:

  • it keeps growing tax-free or tax-deferred in its home country;
  • moving it often triggers tax, charges or penalties;
  • home-country pension rules are often generous and well regulated;
  • you may move again, or return.

There are practical issues to manage. Some providers, especially in the US, restrict accounts for customers living abroad, or will not let you change investments if you have a foreign address. Keep at least one bank account, an address you can use, and online access in your home country, and update the provider with your contact details.

United States: 401(k)s and IRAs

You can keep a 401(k) or IRA if you move abroad. Most people leave a 401(k) in the plan or roll it into an IRA, which is not taxable if done directly. Withdrawals before age 59½ usually incur a 10% penalty on top of income tax.

For American citizens and green card holders, the US taxes worldwide income wherever you live, so US accounts usually keep their US tax treatment, but your new country may also tax them, or tax them differently. Tax treaties often help: the US-UK treaty, for example, generally lets each country respect the other's pension tax rules. Others, such as with many Latin American and Asian countries, may not cover Roth accounts, so Roth withdrawals that are tax-free in the US could be taxed locally.

Non-Americans who worked in the US and leave can also keep their 401(k)s and IRAs. Withdrawals made while living abroad are usually subject to 30% US withholding tax, which a tax treaty may reduce.

United Kingdom: Workplace and Personal Pensions

UK pensions can stay in the UK and be drawn from abroad, normally from age 55, rising to 57 in 2028. Tax on withdrawals depends on the tax treaty between the UK and your new country: many treaties give the country where you live the right to tax private pensions, and you can apply to HMRC to receive payments without UK tax deducted.

Transfers to overseas pension schemes are possible through a "qualifying recognised overseas pension scheme" (QROPS), but the rules have tightened. A 25% overseas transfer charge applies unless you live in the same country as the scheme. Since 30 October 2024, that exemption no longer extends to schemes elsewhere in the EEA. Transfers are worth considering only in specific cases, usually when you have settled permanently in one country and the receiving scheme is good value. Be very wary of unsolicited offers to transfer: pension transfer scams and high-fee schemes targeting expats have cost people their savings.

Canada, Australia and Others

Canada

RRSPs can stay open after you leave Canada. You cannot usually contribute more, and withdrawals by non-residents face 25% withholding tax, which some tax treaties reduce. TFSAs can also be kept, but contributions made while non-resident are penalised, and the new country may tax the income.

Australia

Australian citizens and permanent residents keep their superannuation and can access it from the usual preservation age, wherever they live. Temporary residents who leave permanently can claim a "departing Australia superannuation payment", but it is heavily taxed: generally 35% on the taxed element, and 65% for working holiday makers. Super not claimed within six months of leaving and the visa ending is eventually transferred to the tax office, from where it can still be claimed. Australia has a limited agreement with New Zealand that allows transfers between the two countries.

European Union

Occupational and private pensions are mostly national. Moving within the EU usually means leaving each pension where it was earned, and drawing several pensions in retirement. EU rules protect your right to keep accrued benefits, but transfers between countries are often difficult.

Asia and the Middle East

Provident funds in countries such as Singapore, Malaysia, Hong Kong and India have their own rules for people leaving. Some allow foreigners who leave permanently to withdraw their balances; others, like India's Employees' Provident Fund, can be kept but stop earning interest after some years without contributions. Gulf states such as the UAE often provide an end-of-service gratuity rather than a pension for foreign workers, so building your own retirement savings is essential.

Should You Cash Out Before You Leave?

Usually not. Cashing out a pension early tends to combine the worst possible tax treatment with the loss of decades of tax-advantaged growth. In the US, an early 401(k) withdrawal can lose 10% to a penalty plus income tax at your highest rate. In Australia, a working holiday maker loses 65% of their super's taxable element. In the UK, withdrawals before age 55 are treated as "unauthorised" and can be taxed at 55%.

Cashing out can make sense in a few cases: when the balance is small and fees would eat it, when you are a temporary resident who will never return and the tax cost is moderate, or when you urgently need the money and have no cheaper option.

Tax-Free at Home Can Mean Taxable Abroad

This is the trap that catches the most people. Accounts that are tax-free in one country are often ordinary taxable investments in another, because each country decides what it taxes for its own residents.

  • UK ISAs are tax-free only for UK tax. If you become a US resident, the US taxes ISA income and gains, and funds inside them may be treated as "passive foreign investment companies" with punishing tax rules. France, Spain and others also tax ISA income.
  • US Roth IRAs are tax-free in the US, but some countries tax the growth or withdrawals.
  • Canadian TFSAs are not recognised by the US and are taxable there.
  • Foreign pensions held by US citizens may need to be reported on forms such as the FBAR and Form 8938, with heavy penalties for not doing so.

Before moving, ask a tax adviser in your new country how each of your accounts will be treated. Sometimes it is better to sell investments and realise gains while you are still resident in the old country, where they may be tax-free.

Contributing to Pensions While Abroad

When you start working in a new country, you will usually join its pension system. That is generally good: you build a new state pension entitlement and may receive employer contributions. Keep contributing to old accounts only if the rules allow it and it makes sense: you usually cannot contribute to US IRAs without US earned income, and UK pension tax relief for non-residents is limited to a small amount for five years after leaving.

If you are an expatriate in a country without a pension system for foreigners, consider an international savings plan carefully. Many are sold with high charges and long lock-in periods. Low-cost index funds through a reputable broker are often a better option.

Currency Risk

If you plan to retire in a different currency from your pension, exchange rates matter. The pound lost about a fifth of its value against the dollar in the months after the 2016 Brexit vote, cutting the dollar value of every UK pension by the same amount. Over decades, currencies can move a long way in either direction. Some people keep their savings in the currency where they plan to retire, or split them between currencies.

A Checklist Before You Move

  1. List every pension and retirement account, with account numbers, provider contacts and login details. Note any old workplace pensions you may have forgotten.
  2. Download your state pension records and contribution history from each country.
  3. Check for a social security agreement between your old and new countries.
  4. Find out how your new country taxes foreign pensions and tax-free accounts.
  5. Update your address and contact details with every provider, and keep a home-country bank account.
  6. Review beneficiaries on each account, which may need updating under a new country's rules.
  7. Do not cash out or transfer in a hurry. Decisions can usually wait until you have advice.

When You Retire

If you have worked in several countries, you will probably claim several pensions. Most countries let you claim from abroad, often by post or online; some require a "certificate of life" each year to prove you are still alive. Start claims a few months before your pension age. If you have worked in an EU country, the pension authority in the country where you live can usually coordinate claims for all the EU countries you have worked in.

Think too about where you will be taxed. Many countries tax pensions only where you live, but some, such as the US for its citizens, also tax wherever you are. Double tax treaties usually prevent you paying twice on the same income, but you may need to claim relief.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Forgetting old pensions. People who change jobs and countries often lose track of small workplace pensions. Government tracing services in the UK, Australia and elsewhere can help find them.
  • Letting a provider lose contact. If statements go to an old address, you may miss changes to your scheme or fail to claim on time.
  • Assuming every country taxes pensions like home. Lump sums that are tax-free in one country, such as the UK's 25% tax-free cash, may be fully taxable in another.
  • Trusting a transfer offer because it sounds official. Scammers often use the names of real schemes and regulators.
  • Missing state pension deadlines. Some countries only backdate claims for a limited period, so claiming late can cost months of payments.

Frequently Asked Questions: What Happens to My 401(k) If I Move Abroad

Will I lose my pension if I move abroad?

Almost never. Pensions you have earned remain yours. The risks are in how they are taxed, whether they keep rising, and what you might lose by cashing out early.

Can I receive US Social Security if I am not an American citizen?

Often yes, if you earned enough credits, though some non-citizens living in certain countries face restrictions or withholding. Social security agreements can help you qualify by combining work periods.

Can I transfer my UK pension to another country?

Sometimes, to a qualifying overseas scheme, but a 25% charge applies unless you live in the same country as the scheme, and transfers carry risks. Most people are better leaving UK pensions in the UK.

What happens to my 401(k) if I leave the US?

You can keep it or roll it into an IRA. Withdrawals are taxed and, before age 59½, usually penalised, wherever you live.

Is my state pension frozen if I move?

It depends on the country paying it and where you go. The UK freezes state pensions in many countries, including Canada and Australia. Most other countries, including the US, Canada and EU members, pay inflation increases abroad.

Do I pay tax on my pension in two countries?

Possibly, but tax treaties usually prevent double taxation, either by giving one country the taxing right or by letting you credit the tax paid in one country against the other.

Should I use an international pension adviser?

Choose carefully. Use advisers regulated in your home country, or in the country where they are based, and ask how they are paid. Be wary of anyone approaching you unasked, promising high returns or pushing transfers.

Final Thoughts

Moving abroad does not wipe out your retirement savings, but it does make them more complicated. The pensions you have earned will usually wait for you, growing in the meantime, and international agreements protect much of what you build. The real dangers are rushed decisions: cashing out early, transferring into unsuitable schemes, and failing to notice that a tax-free account at home is now a taxable one.

A few hours spent listing your accounts, checking the rules in both countries and keeping your records up to date can be worth tens of thousands of dollars by the time you retire, wherever that turns out to be.
