Updated 24 October 2026

Quick answer: Prices are still high because a fall in inflation does not mean a fall in prices. Inflation measures how fast prices are rising, not how high they are. When inflation drops from 9% to 3%, prices are still going up, only more slowly, on top of all the earlier increases. In the US, consumer prices are close to 30% higher than at the start of 2021, and the picture is similar in the UK and the euro area. Prices would only return to their old levels with sustained deflation, which central banks work hard to avoid because it usually comes with recessions and job losses. What brings relief instead is wages rising faster than prices, which happened in most rich countries from 2023 to 2025. In 2026, inflation has picked up again, to about 3.7% in the US, 3.5% in the UK and 3.8% in the euro area in September, mainly because the conflict involving Iran pushed up oil and petrol prices.

It is one of the most common complaints of the past few years. The news says inflation has fallen. Politicians claim credit for bringing it down. Yet the weekly shop, the electricity bill, the rent and a meal out all cost far more than they did a few years ago, and they show no sign of coming back down. Many people conclude that the official figures must be wrong, or that someone is being dishonest.

The official figures are broadly right. The problem is the word. "Inflation is falling" sounds as if prices are falling, when it means only that they are rising less quickly. This guide explains the difference, why prices rarely fall, why some prices behave very differently from others, the role of company profits, what has happened in 2026, and what actually restores your spending power.

Inflation Is a Speed, Not a Level

The simplest way to understand inflation is to think of a car's speedometer. Inflation is the speed at which prices are climbing, measured as the percentage change over the past 12 months. The price level is how far the car has travelled.

When inflation falls from 9% to 3%, the car has slowed down, but it is still moving forward. Prices are still rising, by 3% a year. And all the distance covered at high speed is still behind you. Prices that rose 8% in 2022 and 4% in 2023 do not go back down because inflation in 2024 was 3%. They stay up, and the 3% is added on top.

So when people say "inflation is down, but prices are still high", they are describing exactly what the numbers say. The confusion comes from the language, not the data.

How Much Have Prices Risen?

The surge of 2021 to 2023 was the biggest in decades in most rich countries. Annual inflation peaked at 9.1% in the US in June 2022, 11.1% in the UK in October 2022 and 10.6% in the euro area the same month, the highest since the early 1980s.

By September 2026, the US consumer price index was close to 30% higher than in January 2021. In practical terms, a basket of goods and services that cost $100 then costs about $129 now. The UK and the euro area saw similar cumulative rises. Some essentials rose much faster than the average: food, energy, rents, insurance and car repairs in particular.

Even "normal" inflation of 2% a year, the target of most central banks, means prices roughly double every 35 years. A cup of coffee that costs $4 today would cost about $8 in the 2060s even if inflation stayed exactly on target.

Why Prices Almost Never Fall

For prices to come back down, a country would need deflation: a sustained fall in the general price level. That does happen, but rarely, and usually during a crisis.

  • During the Great Depression, US prices fell by about a quarter between 1929 and 1933. Wages and jobs collapsed with them.
  • Japan had mild deflation for much of the period from the late 1990s to the early 2010s, a time of weak growth often called its "lost decades".
  • Prices fell briefly in several countries in 2009 and 2020, during recessions.

Central banks treat deflation as a danger, not a relief, for several reasons. When people expect prices to fall, they delay purchases, which weakens demand and leads companies to cut jobs. Debts become harder to repay, because incomes fall while the amount owed stays the same. And wages are hard to cut, so falling prices often come with rising unemployment rather than cheaper living.

This is why central banks aim for low positive inflation, usually 2%, rather than zero. A little inflation gives some room for prices and wages to adjust, and keeps the economy away from the deflation trap.

Disinflation and Deflation: The Two Words That Matter

  • Inflation: prices are rising.
  • Disinflation: inflation is falling, so prices are rising more slowly. This is what happened in most countries from 2023 to 2025.
  • Deflation: prices are falling.
  • Reflation or re-acceleration: inflation picking up again after falling, as in 2026.

When a politician or central banker says "inflation has come down", they mean disinflation. They almost never mean deflation.

How Inflation Is Measured

National statistics offices measure inflation by tracking the prices of a "basket" of goods and services that represents what households typically buy. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics collects about 80,000 prices a month for its consumer price index. The UK's Office for National Statistics tracks more than 700 items, from bread and train fares to streaming subscriptions, and updates the basket each year to reflect changing habits. Eurostat combines national measures into a harmonised index for the euro area.

Each item is weighted by its share of household spending. Housing, transport and food carry heavy weights; small items such as matches or postage carry tiny ones. The headline inflation rate is the percentage change in the cost of the whole basket compared with 12 months earlier.

Some details explain why measures differ between countries. The US index includes an estimate of what homeowners would pay to rent their own homes, which makes housing a third of the basket. The UK's main measure, the CPI, leaves out owner-occupiers' housing costs, while a second measure, CPIH, includes them. Statistics offices also adjust for quality: if a new phone costs the same as last year's model but is faster, part of the price is treated as paying for the improvement.

Because the comparison is always with a year earlier, the annual inflation rate can fall sharply simply because a big rise a year ago drops out of the calculation. That "base effect" helped inflation fall quickly in 2023, as the energy spikes of 2022 rolled out of the figures, even though prices were not falling.

Why It Feels Worse Than the Official Figure

Many people feel that their own cost of living has risen faster than official inflation, and they are often right. The official index is an average across a typical household's spending. Your own inflation rate depends on what you buy.

  • Frequent purchases weigh heavily in our minds. We notice the price of milk, bread and petrol every week, but not the price of televisions, which has fallen. Food and fuel rose much faster than the average in 2022.
  • Renters and borrowers were hit harder. Rents rose sharply, and anyone remortgaging or borrowing faced much higher interest rates. In the US, the consumer price index does not include mortgage interest at all.
  • Lower-income households spend a larger share of income on food, energy and rent, so their personal inflation rate was often higher than the average.
  • Some costs are not prices in the index. Higher insurance premiums, new fees, tips and reduced service quality all add to the feeling that life costs more.

Shrinkflation adds to the frustration: when a chocolate bar or a packet of crisps gets smaller at the same price, the price per gram has risen. Statistics offices do adjust for package sizes, but shoppers notice it as a sneaky price rise.

Why Some Prices Did Fall, and Most Did Not

Not every price is equally sticky. Prices of goods traded on world markets can fall as quickly as they rise. Petrol prices fell sharply in late 2022 and 2023 after the spike that followed Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Used car prices in the US fell from their 2022 peaks. Eggs, which soared because of bird flu, fell back once flocks recovered, before rising again. Shipping costs fell back to near normal.

Other prices almost never come down:

  • Services, such as haircuts, restaurant meals, childcare and car repairs, depend mainly on wages, and wages rarely fall.
  • Rents are set in contracts and adjust slowly, and they reflect a shortage of housing in many cities.
  • Processed food prices include wages, packaging, transport and retail costs, so they stay up even when the price of wheat or sunflower oil falls.
  • Regulated prices, such as rail fares or energy price caps, often adjust once a year, with a lag.

Economists call this "downward price rigidity". The result is a ratchet: prices go up easily when costs rise, and come down only partly, slowly, or not at all when costs fall.

Did Company Profits Push Prices Up?

The term "greedflation" became popular in 2022 and 2023 to describe companies raising prices by more than their costs rose. The evidence suggests it was part of the story, though not all of it.

Studies by the International Monetary Fund and the European Central Bank found that rising profits accounted for a large share of euro area inflation in 2022 and early 2023, as firms in energy, transport and some consumer sectors raised prices faster than their costs. Similar patterns were found in the US and UK. The usual explanation is that, when everyone expects prices to rise, it is easier for firms to raise them without losing customers. As competition and demand weakened, profit margins in many sectors fell back, and wages caught up.

That matters because it suggests that strong competition, and shoppers willing to switch brands and stores, help keep prices down.

Why Inflation Rose Again in 2026

By 2025, inflation in most rich countries had fallen to between 2% and 3%. Then, at the end of February 2026, military action involving Iran disrupted tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, through which about a fifth of the world's oil normally passes. Brent crude rose from the mid-$60s a barrel to more than $100 in March and reached about $114 in May, before easing after a ceasefire.

Energy feeds quickly into consumer prices, through petrol, heating and electricity, and more slowly through transport and food. By September 2026, US petrol prices were about a third higher than a year earlier. Headline inflation rose to 3.7% in the US, 3.5% in the UK and 3.8% in the euro area, the highest in about three years.

Core inflation, which excludes food and energy, rose more modestly, to about 2.6% in the US, 2.8% in the UK and 2.5% in the euro area. That is why central banks watch it closely: it shows whether an energy shock is spreading into prices generally. In the US, tariffs introduced in 2025 have also added to the prices of many imported goods.

Emerging economies have felt the shock too. India's inflation rose to 5.5% in September 2026, with food prices up almost 8%, while Japan's core inflation was below 2% in August. Countries that import most of their energy, and those whose currencies weakened against the dollar, have generally been hit hardest.

What Actually Restores Your Spending Power

If prices are unlikely to fall, the only way back to where you were is for income to rise faster than prices. Economists call this growth in real wages.

In most rich countries, wages fell behind prices in 2021 and 2022, then caught up as inflation slowed and labour markets stayed tight. In the US, average real wages were back above their early-2021 level by 2023. In the UK, real pay took until 2024 to recover. Workers in some sectors and countries have still not fully caught up, and the 2026 energy spike has squeezed real incomes again.

That is why the experience of high prices differs so much between people. Someone whose pay rose 30% since 2021 is better off despite higher prices. Someone on a fixed income, or whose pay rose 15%, is noticeably worse off. The calculator above shows where you stand.

What You Can Do

Check your own inflation rate

Compare what you spend on your main categories now with a year or two ago. Knowing which costs are rising fastest tells you where savings matter most.

Shop around for sticky prices

Insurance, broadband, mobile phone plans and energy tariffs often rise year after year for loyal customers. Switching or negotiating can cut costs significantly. Supermarket own brands are typically much cheaper than branded goods.

Ask for a raise based on facts

If your pay has not kept pace with prices, your real pay has fallen. Market data, your own performance and the cost of replacing you are better arguments than inflation alone, but inflation explains why the conversation matters.

Protect savings from inflation

Cash in a current account loses value when inflation is above the interest rate. Higher-rate savings accounts, government bonds linked to inflation (such as US TIPS and I bonds, or UK index-linked gilts) and, over the long term, diversified investments help protect purchasing power.

Fix costs where it helps

Fixed-rate loans and fixed energy or phone contracts protect you from further rises, though they can cost more if prices fall.

Avoid spreading payments without a plan

When prices are high, it is tempting to use credit cards or buy now, pay later loans for everyday costs. High interest can turn a temporary squeeze into a lasting debt.

What Governments and Central Banks Can and Cannot Do

Central banks fight inflation mainly by raising interest rates, which slows borrowing and spending and eventually eases price rises. They cannot bring prices back down without causing a recession, and they do not try to. Their goal is to return inflation to about 2%, not to undo past increases.

Governments can ease the pain for specific groups, for example with energy subsidies, as many European countries did in 2022, or by raising benefits in line with inflation. They can also reduce costs over the long term by increasing competition, building more homes and investing in energy supply. Price controls on many goods tend to cause shortages and rarely work for long.

Will Prices Ever Go Back Down?

For most things, no, at least not in money terms. The general price level in rich countries has risen almost every year since the 1950s, and the price increases of 2021 to 2023 are now built into wages, rents and contracts.

Some individual prices will fall, especially for goods traded on world markets and for technology. Petrol could fall if the oil supply returns to normal. Food prices can fall when harvests are good. But the realistic hope is not lower prices; it is a period of low, stable inflation in which incomes rise faster than prices, so that the things that seem expensive today gradually become more affordable.

Frequently Asked Questions

If inflation is 3%, why did my shopping go up 10%?

Because 3% is an average across all spending, while the things you buy may have risen faster. Food, energy, rents and insurance have risen faster than the average in recent years. Your own basket determines your personal inflation rate.

Is deflation good for consumers?

Not usually. While lower prices sound attractive, sustained deflation tends to bring falling wages, rising unemployment and heavier debt burdens. Short-term falls in particular prices, such as petrol, are different and are welcome.

Why don't central banks aim for zero inflation?

A small positive rate, usually 2%, gives a buffer against deflation, lets real wages adjust without cutting pay, and gives central banks room to cut interest rates in a downturn.

Are official inflation figures accurate?

They are produced by independent statistics offices using thousands of prices and methods that are published and reviewed. They measure the average household well. They will not match any individual's experience exactly.

What is core inflation?

Inflation excluding the most volatile items, usually food and energy. It gives a better sense of the underlying trend, which is why central banks pay attention to it.

What is the difference between inflation and the cost of living?

Inflation is the rate of change of prices. The cost of living is what it actually costs to live, which depends on prices and on your income. A cost-of-living crisis happens when prices rise faster than incomes, as they did in 2022.

Will the 2026 oil shock cause another surge like 2022?

So far, the rise is smaller, because core inflation has stayed moderate and central banks have kept interest rates high. Much depends on whether oil supplies through the Gulf return to normal.

Final Thoughts

"Inflation is down" was always going to sound hollow to people paying far more for food, energy and rent than they did a few years ago. The phrase is accurate, but it answers a different question from the one most people are asking. Inflation is about how fast prices are rising; the frustration is about how high they already are.

The price increases of the early 2020s are, for the most part, permanent. The way through is not to wait for prices to fall, which would usually mean something worse is happening. It is to make sure your income, your savings and your spending habits keep up, so that over time the gap closes from the other side.
