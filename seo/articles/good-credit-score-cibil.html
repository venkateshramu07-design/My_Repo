Your credit score quietly shapes your financial life. It affects whether you get a home loan, car loan or credit card, how much interest you pay, and sometimes even whether a landlord or employer trusts you. A difference of 0.5% on a ₹50 lakh home loan over 20 years can add up to several lakh rupees in extra interest.

Yet many people don't know their score, what it means or how to improve it. This guide explains how credit scores work in India, what counts as a good score, the factors that raise and lower it, how to check it for free, how to fix errors and a step-by-step plan to improve it.

What Is a Credit Score?

A credit score is a three-digit number that summarises your credit history: how much you've borrowed, how you've repaid it and how you use credit today. Lenders use it to estimate how likely you are to repay a new loan on time.

In India, credit information companies (CICs), often called credit bureaus, collect data from banks and other lenders and calculate scores. Four are licensed by the Reserve Bank of India:

  • TransUnion CIBIL
  • Experian
  • Equifax
  • CRIF High Mark

CIBIL is the oldest and best known, so people often say "CIBIL score" to mean any credit score. Each bureau uses its own model, so your scores may differ slightly between them. Most use a range of 300 to 900.

Your credit report is the detailed record behind the score: your loans and cards, credit limits, balances, repayment history for each month, and enquiries made by lenders.

What Is a Good Credit Score?

Each lender sets its own criteria, but scores are commonly interpreted like this:

  • 750 to 900: excellent. You're likely to be approved for loans and cards, often with the best rates and higher limits.
  • 700 to 749: good. Most lenders will consider you, though you may not always get the lowest rates.
  • 650 to 699: fair. Approval is possible but less certain, and rates may be higher.
  • 550 to 649: poor. Many lenders may decline, or offer loans only at high rates or with security.
  • Below 550: very poor. Getting unsecured credit will be difficult until you rebuild.

If you've never had a loan or card, your CIBIL report may show "NA" or "NH" (no history) rather than a score. This isn't a bad score; it just means there's no data yet.

Why Your Credit Score Matters

  • Loan approval: lenders check your score for home loans, car loans, personal loans, education loans and business loans.
  • Interest rates: many banks link loan rates to credit scores, so a higher score can directly reduce your EMI.
  • Credit cards: premium cards with better rewards usually require strong scores.
  • Credit limits: a good history can lead to higher limits.
  • Faster processing: applicants with strong scores often get quicker approvals and pre-approved offers.

What Affects Your Credit Score?

Bureaus don't publish their exact formulas, but they and lenders agree on the main factors.

1. Payment History

This is the most important factor. Every missed or late EMI or credit card payment is reported and can lower your score significantly. Payments that are 30, 60 or 90 days late are progressively more damaging. Defaults, loans written off by the lender and "settled" accounts (where you paid less than the full amount owed) are among the most serious negative marks.

2. Credit Utilisation

This is the share of your credit card limits you're using. If you have a total limit of ₹2 lakh and owe ₹1.2 lakh, your utilisation is 60%. High utilisation suggests you may be relying heavily on credit. Many experts suggest keeping it below 30%.

3. Length of Credit History

A longer track record of responsible borrowing gives lenders more confidence. This is why closing your oldest credit card can sometimes lower your score: it shortens your average credit history and reduces your total available credit.

4. Credit Mix

A healthy mix of secured loans (such as home or car loans, backed by an asset) and unsecured credit (such as credit cards and personal loans), all handled well, can help. Too many unsecured loans can be a warning sign.

5. Credit Enquiries

When you apply for a loan or card, the lender makes a "hard enquiry" on your report. Several applications in a short period can suggest you're desperate for credit and may lower your score. Checking your own score is a "soft enquiry" and doesn't affect it.

What Doesn't Affect Your Score

  • Your income or salary (lenders check it separately).
  • Your savings or investments.
  • Checking your own credit score or report.
  • Your religion, caste, gender or marital status.
  • Debit card or UPI spending.

How to Check Your Credit Score for Free

RBI rules require each credit bureau to give individuals one free full credit report, including the score, every calendar year. You can request it on each bureau's official website. Since there are four bureaus, you can spread these out to check your credit several times a year at no cost.

Many banks, credit card apps and fintech platforms also show your score for free. They're convenient, but read what data you're agreeing to share, and be careful of apps that push loan offers aggressively.

Be careful of scams: genuine bureaus and banks never ask for your OTP, PIN or card CVV to show your credit score.

How to Read Your Credit Report

When you get your report, check:

  1. Personal details: name, date of birth, PAN, addresses and phone numbers.
  2. Accounts: every loan and card listed should be yours. Unknown accounts may indicate errors or identity fraud.
  3. Payment history: look for any late payments, often shown as "days past due" (DPD) for each month. "000" or "STD" generally means on time.
  4. Account status: look for "written off", "settled" or "suit filed", which are serious negatives. Closed loans should show as closed.
  5. Balances and limits: check they're up to date.
  6. Enquiries: look for applications you didn't make.

How to Fix Errors on Your Credit Report

Errors are more common than people expect: a loan you repaid still showing as open, a late payment that was actually on time, or someone else's account appearing on your report.

  1. Raise a dispute directly with the credit bureau through its website. You'll need your report's control number or details of the account.
  2. Contact the lender that reported the information, with proof such as a no-dues certificate or payment receipts.
  3. Follow up. Under RBI rules, complaints are expected to be resolved within 30 days, and lenders and bureaus can be required to pay compensation for delays beyond that.
  4. Escalate to the RBI Ombudsman through the RBI's complaint management system if the issue isn't resolved.

Always collect a no-dues certificate when you close a loan, and check your report a few months later to make sure it shows as closed.

How to Improve Your Credit Score: A Step-by-Step Plan

Step 1: Never Miss a Payment

Set up auto-debit for every EMI and at least the minimum due on every credit card. Better still, pay the full card balance every month. Payment history matters most, and even one missed payment can hurt for years.

Step 2: Bring Down Credit Card Balances

If your utilisation is high, focus on paying down card balances. Paying before the statement date, not just the due date, can lower the balance that gets reported. Our credit card payoff calculator shows how quickly you can clear your balance.

Step 3: Don't Close Old Cards Unnecessarily

If an old card has no annual fee, keeping it open and using it occasionally can help your credit history and utilisation. If it has a high fee you don't want to pay, ask the bank to switch it to a free card instead of closing it.

Step 4: Space Out Applications

Apply for new credit only when you need it, and avoid applying to many lenders at once. Use eligibility checkers that don't create hard enquiries where available.

Step 5: Ask for a Higher Limit Carefully

A higher credit limit lowers your utilisation if your spending stays the same. Only do this if you're confident it won't tempt you to spend more.

Step 6: Deal With Old Defaults Properly

If you have overdue accounts, contact the lender to repay. Where possible, repay in full rather than "settling" for less, because a settled status stays on your report as a negative. If you've already settled, you can sometimes pay the remaining amount to have the status updated to closed.

Step 7: Be Careful With Co-Signing

If you're a guarantor or co-applicant on someone else's loan, their missed payments can hurt your score. Only co-sign for people you trust completely.

Step 8: Give It Time

Lenders now report data more frequently than in the past, so positive changes can show up within weeks. But rebuilding after serious problems like defaults usually takes months to years of consistent, on-time payments.

How to Build a Credit Score From Scratch

Students, young professionals and people who've always used cash often have no credit history. To build one:

  • Get a secured credit card backed by a fixed deposit. Many banks offer these with no income proof, and they report to bureaus like regular cards.
  • Use it lightly and pay in full every month, for example for a phone bill set on auto-pay.
  • Consider a small loan you can easily repay, such as a consumer durable EMI, only if you genuinely need the item.
  • Be patient. A few months of on-time payments are usually enough to generate a score, and it improves with time.

Avoid "buy now, pay later" or instant loan apps that encourage overspending. Missed payments on these also get reported.

Common Myths About Credit Scores

  • "Checking my score lowers it." False. Checking your own score is a soft enquiry.
  • "Paying the minimum due is enough." It avoids a late payment mark, but the remaining balance attracts high interest and keeps utilisation high.
  • "Closing all my cards will improve my score." Often the opposite: it can shorten your history and raise utilisation.
  • "A high salary means a high score." Income isn't part of the score.
  • "Once my score is bad, it's ruined forever." Negative entries fade in impact over time, and consistent good behaviour rebuilds your score.
  • "Credit repair agencies can delete negatives for a fee." Only genuine errors can be removed, and you can dispute those yourself for free.

What Lenders Look at Besides Your Score

A good score is necessary but not always sufficient. Lenders also consider:

  • Income and stability: your salary or business income, how long you've been in your job, and your employer or business type.
  • Existing obligations: lenders often look at the share of your income already committed to EMIs, sometimes called the fixed obligation to income ratio (FOIR). Many prefer total EMIs to stay below roughly 40% to 50% of your income.
  • Age and remaining working years: especially for long home loans.
  • The asset: for secured loans, the value and legal status of the property or vehicle.
  • Your relationship with the bank: existing customers with a long record may get better terms.

Credit Scores for Specific Goals

  • Home loans: lenders typically look for scores in the 700s or higher, and many offer their lowest advertised rates to applicants with scores of 750 or more. Because home loans are large and long, even small rate differences matter a lot.
  • Car loans: secured by the vehicle, so approval may be possible with a slightly lower score, but at a higher rate.
  • Personal loans: unsecured, so lenders rely heavily on your score. Strong scores can mean much lower rates.
  • Credit cards: entry-level cards may accept modest scores; premium cards usually want strong scores and higher incomes.
  • Education loans: often based on the co-applicant's (usually a parent's) credit profile as well as the student's.

Rebuilding After a Default: A 12-Month Plan

  1. Month 1: get your free reports from all four bureaus. List every overdue account and its status.
  2. Months 1 to 3: contact lenders about overdue accounts. Aim to repay in full; if you must settle, ask in writing how the account will be reported, and try to close it fully later.
  3. Months 1 to 12: set up auto-debit for every remaining EMI and card so nothing is ever late again.
  4. Months 2 to 6: if you have no active credit, consider a secured card against an FD and use it lightly, paying in full.
  5. Months 3 to 12: keep card utilisation under 30%, make no new loan applications unless necessary, and check your report every few months using your free annual reports across the bureaus.
  6. Month 12: review your progress. Most people see meaningful improvement after a year of clean payments, and it continues to rise with time.

Joint Loans, Add-On Cards and Your Score

  • Joint loans: every co-borrower is responsible for the full loan, and it appears on each person's report. If one partner stops paying, both scores suffer.
  • Add-on cards: usually reported on the primary cardholder's account, so the primary holder is responsible for the spending.
  • Guarantees: if you guarantee a loan and the borrower defaults, it can affect your credit and you may have to repay it.

Protect Yourself From Credit Fraud

Identity fraud, where someone takes a loan or card in your name, can damage your score without your knowledge. It has become more common with instant digital loans. Some simple precautions help:

  • Guard your PAN and Aadhaar. When sharing photocopies, write the purpose and date across them, and never share them with unverified loan agents or apps.
  • Never share OTPs, PINs or passwords. No genuine bank, bureau or lender will ask for them.
  • Check your report regularly. Look for loans, cards or enquiries you don't recognise. Spreading your free annual reports from the four bureaus across the year lets you check several times a year.
  • Pay attention to alerts. If you receive a message about a loan application, enquiry or EMI you don't recognise, act immediately.
  • Be wary of loan apps. Use only lenders regulated by the RBI or their declared lending partners. Unregulated apps may misuse your data or harass borrowers.

If you find a fraudulent account:

  1. Contact the lender that reported it and tell them it isn't yours.
  2. Raise a dispute with every credit bureau that shows the account.
  3. Report cyber fraud on the national cybercrime portal (cybercrime.gov.in) or by calling the 1930 helpline, and file a police complaint if needed.
  4. Keep copies of all complaints and follow up until the account is removed from your report.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good CIBIL score?

750 or above is generally considered good to excellent. Many lenders offer their best rates to applicants in this range.

How can I check my CIBIL score for free?

Each credit bureau must provide one free full report with your score every calendar year through its official website. Many banks and apps also show your score for free.

How long does it take to improve a credit score?

Small improvements, such as lower card balances, can show up within one or two reporting cycles. Recovering from missed payments or defaults usually takes many months of consistent on-time payments.

Does a loan settlement affect my credit score?

Yes. "Settled" means you paid less than the full amount owed, and lenders see it as a serious negative. Repaying in full is better where possible.

Why are my scores different on different apps?

Each bureau has its own data and model, and reports may be updated at different times. Small differences are normal.

Does my credit score affect my job application?

Some employers, particularly in banking and finance, may check credit reports with your consent as part of background checks. A clean record helps.

Do rent, utility or phone bill payments improve my score?

Usually not directly, because most landlords and utility companies don't report payments to credit bureaus in India. Paying a utility or phone bill with a credit card you then clear in full does help, because the card payment is reported.

Can I get a loan with no credit history?

Yes, but options may be limited and rates higher. Secured loans, such as a loan against a fixed deposit or gold, or a secured credit card, are good ways to start building a record. Lenders will also look closely at your income and job stability.

What is a good credit utilisation ratio?

Under 30% of your total card limits is commonly recommended, and lower is better.

Final Thoughts

A good credit score isn't about borrowing a lot. It's about showing, consistently and over time, that you borrow responsibly. Pay every bill on time, keep card balances low, borrow only when you need to and check your report regularly for errors.

These habits cost nothing, and over a lifetime they can save you lakhs of rupees in interest and open doors when you need them most.
