What an Invoice Must Include

The rules differ by country, but these items are expected almost everywhere:

  • A unique invoice number that follows on from the last one (INV-0001, INV-0002…).
  • The invoice date and, usually, the date the work was done or the goods supplied.
  • Your name or business name and address, and the customer's name and address.
  • A clear description of each item or service, with quantity, price and line total.
  • The total, and any tax shown separately with its rate.
  • Payment terms and details: the due date and how to pay (bank transfer, UPI, PayPal).

If you are registered for VAT, GST or sales tax, you usually also need your tax registration number on the invoice; add it under your name in the "From" box. In the UK, for example, a VAT invoice must show your VAT number and the VAT amount for each rate. Limited companies often need to show the company's registered name and number. Check your tax office's rules for your country.

Tips to Get Paid Faster

  • Send it the day the work is done. The sooner the invoice arrives, the sooner the payment clock starts.
  • Use a short, specific due date ("Due 8 November") rather than just "30 days".
  • Make paying easy: put the full bank details, UPI ID or payment link on the invoice.
  • Describe the work in the client's words ("Logo design for the spring campaign"), so their accounts team can approve it without asking.
  • Quote the client's purchase order number if they gave you one; many companies won't pay without it.
  • Follow up politely on the due date with the invoice attached again.

Keeping Records

Tax offices expect you to keep copies of every invoice and receipt you issue, usually for several years (the IRS generally asks for at least three years of records, and many countries require five to seven). Save each PDF in a folder by year, and use "Items as CSV" to copy the lines into a spreadsheet. Because invoices are made in your browser, keep your own copies: nothing is stored online.
