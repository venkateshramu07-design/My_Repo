Quick answer: What happens if a bank fails? If a licensed bank fails, your deposits are protected up to a limit by a government-backed scheme: $250,000 per person per bank in the US, £120,000 in the UK, €100,000 in the European Union, C$100,000 in Canada, A$250,000 in Australia and ¥10 million in Japan. Protected money is normally repaid within a few working days. If a fintech app that is not a bank fails, the picture is very different. E-money and payment firms do not usually have deposit insurance; your money is meant to be kept separate, or "safeguarded", but getting it back can take months, and if the records are poor you may not get all of it. In 2024 the collapse of Synapse, a US company that sat between apps and banks, left tens of thousands of people unable to reach their money, with an estimated $65 million to $95 million missing. Crypto exchanges and most investment platforms have no deposit protection at all. The safest approach is to keep everyday savings with licensed banks, stay below the limit at each one, and treat app balances as a wallet, not a vault.

Most people never think about what happens if their bank disappears, and for good reason: in most rich countries, it has rarely cost ordinary savers anything for decades. But the way people hold money has changed. A typical person now has a current account at a bank, a balance in a payment app, savings in a fintech account that advertises a high rate, perhaps some cash in a brokerage account and some crypto on an exchange. Each of those sits under different rules, and the protections that people assume apply to all of them often apply to only one.

This guide explains what happens when a bank fails, how deposit insurance works in major countries, why fintech apps are different, what went wrong at Synapse and other failures, and how to arrange your money so that a collapse would be an inconvenience rather than a disaster.

What Happens When a Bank Fails

A bank fails when it cannot meet its obligations, usually because it has lost too much money on loans or investments, or because depositors withdraw faster than it can raise cash. Regulators then step in, and the process is surprisingly orderly in most countries.

In the US, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) usually closes a failing bank on a Friday and, over the weekend, either sells its deposits to a healthy bank or pays depositors directly. In the most common case, customers wake up on Monday to find their account now belongs to a different bank, with the same balance and debit card. When there is no buyer, the FDIC aims to pay insured deposits within two business days. Since FDIC insurance began in 1934, no depositor has lost a cent of insured money.

Other countries follow a similar pattern. The UK's Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS) aims to repay most people within seven days. EU rules require payouts within seven working days. Canada's CDIC and Australia's Financial Claims Scheme have similar targets.

Three details trip people up:

  • The limit is per person, per bank, not per account. Two savings accounts at the same bank share one limit.
  • Brands can share a licence. In the UK and elsewhere, several brands can operate under one banking licence. Halifax and Bank of Scotland, for example, share one FSCS limit. The bank's website must say which brands it covers.
  • Categories can multiply cover. In the US and Canada, accounts in different ownership categories (single, joint, certain retirement accounts, trusts) are insured separately. A married couple in the US can have $250,000 each in single accounts plus $500,000 in a joint account at the same bank, all insured.

What About Money Above the Limit?

Deposits above the limit become a claim on whatever is left of the failed bank. Depositors usually get some of it back, sometimes all of it, but it can take months or years.

In practice, governments often go further than the law requires when a failure threatens the wider system. When Silicon Valley Bank collapsed in March 2023, after customers tried to withdraw $42 billion in a single day, US authorities used an emergency "systemic risk exception" to guarantee all deposits, insured or not. They did the same for Signature Bank days later. But that was a choice, not a right. Smaller banks that fail without threatening the wider system do not normally get the same treatment, and uninsured depositors there can wait months and may lose part of their money.

Some countries also protect "temporary high balances", money that is above the limit only briefly because you have just sold a house, received an inheritance or been paid compensation. In the UK, these are covered up to £1.4 million for six months. EU countries have similar rules with different limits.

Why Fintech Apps Are Different

This is where most confusion lies. Many popular money apps are not banks. They are licensed as e-money institutions or payment companies, which means they are allowed to hold and move your money but not to lend it out. Because they do not lend, they are not meant to be at risk of the kind of failure that hits banks, so they are not covered by deposit insurance.

Instead, the rules in the UK and EU require them to safeguard customer money: keep it separate from their own funds, in a protected account at a bank or in safe assets, so that if the company fails, the money is returned to customers rather than to the company's creditors. In the US, apps usually work through partner banks, and the money sits in accounts at those banks.

Safeguarding sounds similar to deposit insurance, but it is not the same:

  • There is no guaranteed amount. If the firm has failed to safeguard properly, there may be a shortfall, and nobody makes it good.
  • There is no fast payout. An administrator has to work out who owes what, and the costs of that process can be taken from the safeguarded pool.
  • It depends on good records. If the company's ledgers do not match the bank's, working out each customer's share can take a very long time.

Some fintechs have become licensed banks, or offer accounts through a partner bank, in which case deposit insurance can apply. The only reliable way to know is to check the app's own terms and the regulator's register.

The Synapse Collapse: What Went Wrong

The clearest example of the risk is Synapse Financial Technologies, a US company that provided the plumbing between dozens of consumer apps and several small banks. Apps such as Yotta, a savings app, and Juno, a banking app, used Synapse to hold customer money at partner banks, including Evolve Bank & Trust. Their customers were told, in effect, that their money was in FDIC-insured banks.

In April 2024 Synapse filed for bankruptcy. It then emerged that its records did not match those of the banks: there was less money in the accounts than customers were owed. Payments were frozen, and more than 100,000 people were cut off from their savings for months. When the money was finally traced, there was an estimated shortfall of between $65 million and $95 million.

FDIC insurance did not help, because it only pays out when an insured bank fails. The banks had not failed; the middleman had, and the money it had lost track of was not insured. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau sued Synapse, and in November 2025 it set aside $46 million from its penalty fund to reimburse affected customers, which still leaves many people short. Synapse's bankruptcy was later dismissed because there was no money left to pay creditors.

The lesson for anyone using an app that says your money is "FDIC insured through our partner banks" is that this is "pass-through" insurance: it protects you if the partner bank fails, and only if the records show exactly what belongs to you. It does not protect you if the app or its middleman fails or loses track of your money.

Other Fintech Failures

Wirecard (2020)

When the German payments company Wirecard collapsed in a fraud scandal in June 2020, its UK subsidiary, which held money for several card and payment apps, was frozen by the UK's Financial Conduct Authority. Customers of apps such as Curve and Pockit could not use their cards or reach their money for several days. In that case the money had been safeguarded and was returned, but the episode showed how quickly access can disappear.

UK e-money administrations

Several small UK e-money firms have collapsed in recent years. In some, the administrator found that money had not been properly safeguarded, and customers waited months or years and received less than they were owed after costs. Partly as a result, the FCA introduced stricter safeguarding rules for payment and e-money firms from 7 May 2026, with more reconciliation, monthly reporting to the regulator and annual audits.

Crypto exchanges

The biggest losses by far have come from crypto platforms. When FTX collapsed in November 2022, customers' accounts were frozen and it emerged that their deposits had been used by an affiliated trading firm. Lenders such as Celsius and Voyager failed the same year. Customers did eventually get much of their money back through bankruptcy proceedings, in FTX's case years later and valued at November 2022 prices, missing the subsequent rise in crypto. No deposit insurance applies to crypto, whatever an exchange's marketing implies.

Investment Accounts and Brokerages

Investment platforms have their own protection schemes, which cover something different: the risk that the firm loses or misuses your investments, not the risk that the investments fall in value.

  • United States: the Securities Investor Protection Corporation (SIPC) covers up to $500,000 per customer at a failed brokerage, including up to $250,000 in cash. Shares and funds held for you are usually kept separate from the firm's own assets, so most customers simply have their accounts moved to another broker.
  • United Kingdom: the FSCS covers up to £85,000 if an investment firm fails and cannot return your assets.
  • European Union: investor compensation schemes cover at least €20,000.

Cash held in brokerage accounts is sometimes "swept" into partner banks, where it may have deposit insurance, or into money market funds, which do not. If you keep a large cash balance at a broker, check where it actually sits.

Payment App Balances

Money sitting in a payment app between transactions is often the least protected money people have. In the US, balances in apps like PayPal, Venmo and Cash App are generally not FDIC insured unless the app holds them at a partner bank under specific conditions, for example if you have a linked debit card or direct deposit. The rules change from time to time and are set out in each app's terms.

In the UK and EU, balances in e-money accounts, including those of large, well-known companies, are safeguarded rather than insured. That is a real protection, but it is not the same as a government guarantee of a fixed amount within seven days.

The simple habit that removes most of the risk is to move money out of payment apps into a bank account rather than letting balances build up.

How to Check Whether Your Money Is Protected

  1. Look for the word "bank". Is the company itself a licensed bank, or a partner of one? This should be stated clearly in the app or on its website, often at the bottom of the page.
  2. Check the regulator's register. In the US, the FDIC's BankFind tool shows whether a bank is insured. In the UK, the FCA register shows whether a firm is a bank, an e-money institution or a payment firm, and the FSCS website has a checker. Most countries have equivalents.
  3. Read the protection statement. Phrases such as "safeguarded", "held with our partner banks" or "eligible for pass-through insurance" mean something different from "covered by deposit insurance".
  4. Check which licence a brand uses if you have money with more than one brand from the same group.

How to Protect Yourself

Stay under the limit at each bank

If your savings exceed the limit, spread them across banks with separate licences. Some US banks offer services that automatically spread large deposits across a network of banks so that each piece is fully insured, and some UK and European savings platforms do the same.

Use ownership categories where they exist

In the US, joint accounts, retirement accounts and trust accounts can increase the amount insured at one bank. The FDIC's online calculator, EDIE, works out your coverage.

Treat apps as wallets

Keep in payment apps and e-money accounts only the money you need for spending in the near future. Move savings to an insured bank account.

Be wary of "too good" rates

A savings rate far above what banks offer usually means something is different: the money may be lent out, invested or held in a less protected way. Yotta, for example, attracted customers by offering prize draws in place of interest, through a structure few understood.

Keep records

Save statements, or screenshots of your balances, regularly. If a firm fails and its records are poor, your own evidence can matter.

Have more than one way to pay

Even a short freeze can be a problem if all your money is in one place. Keep a second bank account and card with a different provider, so that you can still pay rent and buy food while a problem is sorted out.

Warning Signs That a Provider Is in Trouble

  • Withdrawals or transfers that suddenly take days instead of minutes.
  • Unexpected changes to terms, especially moving your money to a "new partner".
  • News of a partner bank under regulatory action, or of the company laying off large numbers of staff.
  • A sudden, unusually generous offer to attract deposits.
  • Support lines that stop answering.

None of these proves a firm will fail, but if you see several together, move money you cannot afford to have frozen.

What to Do If Your Bank or App Fails

  1. Do not panic or pay for help. Deposit insurance payouts are automatic in most countries. Scammers often target customers of failed firms, offering to recover money for a fee.
  2. Find the official source. The regulator (for example the FDIC or FSCS) or the appointed administrator will publish instructions. Use only those channels.
  3. Keep evidence of your balance and transactions.
  4. Redirect income and payments. Change where your salary goes and update direct debits if your account is frozen for long.
  5. Make a claim in an administration if asked to, and keep copies of everything you send.

Frequently Asked Questions: What Happens If a Bank Fails

Is my money safe in a digital bank?

If the digital bank holds a full banking licence, yes, up to the deposit insurance limit, exactly like a traditional bank. If it is an e-money or payment company, your money is safeguarded but not insured. Check which it is.

What happens to a loan or mortgage if my bank fails?

You still owe it. Your loan is usually sold to another lender, and you continue paying on the same terms. Payments owed to a failed bank are never cancelled.

Is money in PayPal or Wise protected?

Generally, these are payment or e-money firms rather than banks in most countries, so balances are safeguarded rather than covered by deposit insurance. In the US, some balances may qualify for pass-through FDIC cover under specific conditions. Check the protection statement for your country.

Are joint accounts covered twice?

In many countries, yes: each account holder gets their own limit for their share. In the UK, a joint account with two people is covered up to £240,000. In the US, joint accounts are a separate category, insured up to $250,000 per co-owner.

Is foreign currency in my account covered?

In most schemes, yes, converted at the exchange rate on the day the bank fails. A few countries exclude some currencies, so check the rules if you hold large foreign balances.

Is crypto on an exchange insured?

No. Deposit insurance does not cover crypto anywhere. Some exchanges carry private insurance against hacks, but that rarely covers the exchange itself failing.

How long does it take to get money back?

From a failed bank, usually a few working days for insured amounts. From a failed e-money firm or app, it can be weeks to years, depending on the state of its records.

Final Thoughts

Bank failures are rarely a disaster for ordinary savers in countries with deposit insurance, provided they stay within the limits. What has changed is that much of our money no longer sits in banks. It sits in apps that look and feel like banks but play by different rules, and the difference only becomes visible when something goes wrong.

The fix is not to avoid fintech, which often offers better service and cheaper payments. It is to know which of your balances are insured, keep your savings where they are, and use the rest as what it is: a convenient place to spend from, not a safe place to store.
