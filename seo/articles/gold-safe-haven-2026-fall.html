Updated 24 October 2026

Quick answer on gold price fall: Yes, gold is still a safe haven, but not a safe investment in the sense of one that never falls. After its best year since 1979 in 2025, when it rose about 64%, gold hit a record of around $5,595 an ounce on 29 January 2026, then fell to about $4,027 by 23 October, a drop of roughly 28%. The main reasons were rising interest rates and bond yields, which make an asset that pays no income less attractive, and a stronger dollar, along with speculative traders selling. Yet central banks bought a record 289 tonnes in the second quarter, and gold funds attracted record inflows of about $31 billion in the third quarter. Gold's value as protection against crises, currency debasement and inflation over long periods remains intact; what 2026 showed is that it can be volatile in the short term. A modest allocation, often 5% to 10% of a portfolio, held for the long run, is how most professionals use it.

Gold has a reputation that no other asset quite matches. It has been money, jewellery and a store of wealth for thousands of years. In a crisis, it is what people buy. When governments print money, it is what people say they should have bought. And in 2025, amid trade wars, wars, record government debts and worries about the dollar, it delivered returns that left almost everything else behind.

Then, in the first ten months of 2026, it fell by more than a quarter. For people who bought near the top, often precisely because they wanted safety, that was a shock. This guide explains what drove the surge and the fall, what "safe haven" actually means, what gold does well and badly, how the picture looks in India and other big gold-buying countries, and how much gold, if any, belongs in a sensible portfolio.

The Great Gold Rally of 2025

Gold began 2025 at about $2,625 an ounce and ended it at around $4,300, a gain of about 64% and its best calendar year since 1979. Several forces combined:

  • Central bank buying. Since Western countries froze Russia's foreign currency reserves in 2022, central banks in China, Poland, Turkey, India and elsewhere have been buying gold at the fastest pace in decades, partly to reduce their reliance on the US dollar.
  • Trade wars and geopolitics. New US tariffs, conflict in the Middle East and tensions between major powers drove investors towards assets outside the financial system.
  • Worries about government debt and the dollar. Large US deficits and questions about the independence of central banks made some investors want an asset no government can print.
  • Interest rate cuts. Central banks were cutting rates through 2025, which lowers the cost of holding an asset that pays no interest.
  • Momentum. Rising prices attracted funds that follow trends, and new investors who did not want to miss out.

By late January 2026, the price had climbed to around $5,595 an ounce.

Why Gold Fell in 2026

The same forces that lifted gold began to reverse.

  • Rising bond yields. Gold pays no interest. When safe government bonds pay more, the opportunity cost of holding gold rises. The US 10-year Treasury yield rose above 5% in September 2026 for the first time since 2023 and reached about 5.35% in October, its highest since 2002.
  • Interest rate rises. With inflation picking up again on higher energy prices, the US Federal Reserve raised rates in September 2026 for the first time since 2023, and other central banks followed. Real interest rates, after inflation, rose.
  • A firmer dollar. Gold is priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar makes it more expensive for buyers in other currencies and tends to push the dollar price down.
  • Speculators selling. Much of the 2025 rally was amplified by short-term traders betting on further gains and on rate cuts. When the outlook for rates flipped, they sold quickly, magnifying the fall.

By 23 October 2026, gold was trading at about $4,027 an ounce, around 28% below its January record, though still more than 50% higher than at the start of 2025.

The Puzzle: Who Kept Buying?

The fall in price happened while some of the biggest buyers in the market were buying more than ever.

  • Central banks bought a record 289 tonnes in the second quarter of 2026, according to the World Gold Council, five times as much as in the first quarter, when Turkey, Russia and others sold. Poland was the biggest buyer, adding 51 tonnes, followed by China with 33 tonnes. Goldman Sachs estimated that central banks bought about 79 tonnes in August alone, four times the monthly average before 2022.
  • Gold ETFs, funds that hold physical gold for investors, attracted about $31 billion in the third quarter of 2026, a record, taking global holdings to a record 4,256 tonnes in September.

How can prices fall while buying hits records? The answer is that gold's price is set at the margin by the most active traders. Central banks buy slowly and steadily for long-term strategic reasons, largely regardless of price. Futures traders and hedge funds move far larger amounts in the short term, and their selling as rate expectations rose outweighed the steady buying. Some analysts see that gap as a sign that the long-term case for gold is intact even as its price falls; others see it as evidence that the 2025 rally simply ran too far.

What "Safe Haven" Really Means

A safe haven is an asset that tends to hold its value, or rise, when other assets are falling sharply, especially in a crisis. It does not mean an asset that never falls.

By that definition, gold has a good record:

  • The 2008 financial crisis: gold ended 2008 with a small gain while global shares fell by more than 40%.
  • The 2020 pandemic: after a brief drop in the March panic, gold rose to a then-record above $2,000 in August.
  • Currency crises: for households in countries where the local currency collapses, from Turkey to Argentina, gold has preserved wealth when savings in banks did not.

But gold has also had long, painful periods. After peaking at about $850 in January 1980, it took more than 25 years to regain that level in nominal terms. After a peak of around $1,900 in 2011, it fell by more than 40% and did not recover fully until 2020. In 2026, it has fallen by more than a quarter in nine months.

The lesson is that gold protects against specific kinds of disaster, financial panic, currency debasement and very high inflation, over long periods. It does not protect against everything, and it is not stable from year to year.

A Short History of Gold as Money

For most of modern history, gold was not an investment alongside money; it was the anchor of money itself. Under the classical gold standard of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, currencies such as the British pound and the US dollar could be exchanged for a fixed weight of gold. After the Second World War, the Bretton Woods system tied the dollar to gold at $35 an ounce, and other currencies to the dollar.

That link ended in August 1971, when President Richard Nixon suspended the convertibility of dollars into gold. Since then, gold has floated freely. Its price soared during the inflation of the 1970s, to about $850 in January 1980, then spent two decades in decline as inflation was tamed and interest rates stayed high. In 1999, at around $250 an ounce, it was widely written off; Britain sold about half its gold reserves at prices near the bottom.

The 2000s brought a long bull market, helped by the arrival of gold ETFs in 2003 and 2004, which made gold easy to buy for ordinary investors, and by the financial crisis of 2008. After the 2011 peak and a long slump, the 2020s brought a new surge, driven this time largely by central banks. The pattern across fifty years is of long cycles, measured in decades rather than months, that reflect inflation, interest rates and trust in paper money.

Gold Versus Shares Over the Long Run

Since gold began trading freely in 1971 at $35 an ounce, its price has risen to around $4,000, an average gain of roughly 9% a year. Over the same period, US shares, with dividends reinvested, have returned somewhat more, around 10% to 11% a year. The difference in the final sums is large: over half a century, a gap of one or two percentage points a year compounds into a portfolio several times bigger.

The bigger difference is in the journey. Gold's returns came in bursts, with two slumps of more than a decade, from 1980 to 2001 and from 2011 to 2019, when it made no progress or lost value. Shares had their own deep crashes, but they recovered faster on average, because companies keep earning and paying dividends. That is why most long-term investors use shares to build wealth and hold gold, if at all, as a smaller insurance policy.

What Gold Does Well, and Badly

Strengths

  • No default risk: physical gold is nobody's liability. It cannot go bankrupt.
  • Scarcity: new mining adds only about 1.5% to 2% to the world's stock of gold each year.
  • Diversification: over long periods, gold's price has moved fairly independently of shares and bonds, which can smooth a portfolio's returns.
  • Crisis protection: as above, it has often risen when everything else falls.
  • Universal acceptance: it can be bought and sold almost anywhere in the world.

Weaknesses

  • No income: gold pays no dividends, interest or rent. Its only return is a change in price.
  • Volatility: it can fall 20% to 40% and stay down for years.
  • Long-run returns below shares: over most long periods, a diversified share portfolio has beaten gold by a wide margin, because companies grow and pay dividends.
  • Costs: physical gold involves dealer spreads, storage and insurance; jewellery carries making charges that are lost when you sell.
  • Sensitivity to interest rates: as 2026 showed, rising real rates can hit gold hard.

Gold in India and Other Big Gold-Buying Countries

India and China are the world's two largest consumers of gold. Indian households are estimated to hold around 25,000 tonnes, more than the official reserves of the world's top ten central banks combined, mostly as jewellery. Gold is bought for weddings and festivals such as Diwali and Akshaya Tritiya, given as gifts and treated as a family's emergency savings.

For Indian investors, two things are different from investors in dollars:

  • The rupee: because gold is priced in dollars, a weaker rupee raises its price in India. Over decades, that has helped gold's returns in rupee terms, which have often been higher than in dollars.
  • How to own it: jewellery carries making charges and GST, and resale value is usually below the purchase price. More efficient ways to invest include gold ETFs and gold mutual funds, digital gold from regulated providers, and existing Sovereign Gold Bonds, which also paid 2.5% interest a year; the government has stopped issuing new tranches. Taxes on gains differ by product and holding period, so check the current rules.

In China, gold buying by households and the central bank has been a major force in the market. In the Middle East and Turkey, gold is widely held as savings and jewellery. In Western countries, most investors own gold through ETFs, and sometimes through coins and bars.

Ways to Own Gold

  • Gold ETFs: funds listed on stock exchanges that hold physical gold. Low cost, easy to buy and sell, no storage worries. The most practical choice for most investors.
  • Coins and bars: direct ownership, but you pay a dealer premium when buying, accept a lower price when selling, and must store and insure them safely.
  • Jewellery: meaningful for personal and cultural reasons, but an expensive way to invest because of making charges.
  • Gold mining shares: companies whose profits rise and fall with gold, often by more than the metal itself. They add company and operational risks.
  • Digital gold and gold savings schemes: convenient for small amounts; check that the provider is regulated and that your gold is held securely in your name.
  • Government gold bonds, where available: in India, existing Sovereign Gold Bonds trade on exchanges; elsewhere, check what local governments offer.

How Much Gold Should You Own?

There is no correct figure, but most financial planners who recommend gold suggest a modest share of a long-term portfolio, often 5% to 10%. At that level, gold can cushion a crisis without dragging down returns too much in calm years. The calculator above shows the trade-off.

A few principles help:

  • Treat it as insurance, not a growth engine. Its main job is to hold up when other things fall.
  • Buy gradually. Regular purchases avoid putting everything in at a peak, as many investors did in January 2026.
  • Rebalance. When gold has risen strongly, as in 2025, trim it back to your target share; when it has fallen, as in 2026, top it up. That forces you to sell high and buy low.
  • Keep costs low. ETFs and funds are usually cheaper than jewellery or small bars.
  • Do not count gold as your emergency fund. Its price can be 25% lower exactly when you need to sell. Keep emergency money in cash; see our guide to [[post:articles/emergency-fund-guide]].

Common Mistakes With Gold

  • Buying after a big rally. Many investors piled in near the January 2026 peak, just as they did in 1980 and 2011. Buying gradually avoids the worst of this.
  • Treating gold as a guaranteed safe asset. It is a safe haven against particular crises, not a guarantee against losses.
  • Holding too much. A portfolio that is mostly gold misses the long-term growth that shares provide.
  • Paying too much to own it. High making charges, dealer premiums and storage fees eat into returns.
  • Buying from unregulated sellers or schemes promising guaranteed returns on gold, which are a common route for fraud.

What Could Happen Next

Gold's next move depends mostly on interest rates, the dollar and the level of fear in the world.

  • Gold could recover if inflation eases and central banks start cutting rates again, if the dollar weakens, if a recession or financial crisis sends investors looking for safety, or if central bank buying continues at its recent pace.
  • Gold could fall further if bond yields keep rising, if inflation stays high enough to force more rate rises, or if speculators who are still holding positions from 2025 continue to sell.

Forecasts from major banks have varied widely and have been revised frequently in 2026. A sensible approach is to hold a modest allocation for its long-term role, rather than trying to predict the next 20% move.

Frequently Asked Questions: Gold Price Fall

When will the gold price fall?

No one can time it reliably. Gold tends to weaken when real interest rates rise, the US dollar strengthens, central banks slow their buying, or fear in markets fades; it tends to rise in the opposite conditions. That is why most guides suggest holding a small, fixed share of gold rather than trading it.

How is the price of gold determined?

Gold trades around the clock in global markets, and its price is set by supply and demand: central bank buying, investment demand through ETFs, jewellery demand, interest rates and the US dollar. The LBMA gold price set twice a day in London is the main benchmark.

Why can the gold price fall?

Higher real interest rates make gold, which pays no interest, less attractive; a stronger US dollar makes it dearer for other buyers; and when fear eases, investors sell safe havens. Profit-taking after a big rally can also cause sharp falls.

Why did gold fall in 2026?

Mainly because interest rates and bond yields rose, which makes gold, which pays no interest, less attractive; because the dollar strengthened; and because short-term traders who had bet on further gains and rate cuts sold. Central banks and gold ETF investors kept buying, but not enough to offset that selling.

Is gold still a good investment?

As a modest part of a diversified portfolio, it can be, mainly as protection against crises and currency debasement. As a main investment, it has historically lagged shares over long periods and can fall sharply, as 2026 showed.

Is gold a good hedge against inflation?

Over very long periods, gold has broadly kept pace with inflation, and it did well in the high-inflation 1970s. Over shorter periods, the link is weak: gold can fall even when inflation rises, if interest rates rise faster.

Should I buy gold now that it has fallen?

If you want gold for the long term and have little or none, buying gradually after a fall is more sensible than buying after a surge. Avoid putting a large share of your savings in at once, and decide on a target allocation first.

Which is better, gold or Bitcoin?

They behave differently. Gold has thousands of years of history, is held by central banks and has often risen in crises. Bitcoin is far more volatile and has tended to fall with shares when rates rise. In 2026, both fell sharply from their peaks. See our article on [[post:articles/bitcoin-losing-its-hype]].

Why do central banks buy gold?

To diversify their reserves away from the US dollar and other currencies, to hold an asset that cannot be frozen or defaulted on by another government, and as insurance against financial crises. Purchases accelerated after Russia's dollar reserves were frozen in 2022.

Is buying gold jewellery a good investment?

Usually not, from a purely financial view, because making charges and taxes are lost when you sell. For investment, gold ETFs, funds or government gold bonds are more efficient. Jewellery has its own personal and cultural value.

Final Thoughts

Gold's journey from about $2,600 to nearly $5,600 and back to about $4,000 in less than two years is a reminder that even the oldest safe haven is not a safe bet in the short term. It rose on fear, falling rates and a rush of momentum buying, and it fell when rates turned and the momentum reversed.

None of that changes what gold is good for. It remains the asset of last resort, held by central banks and families alike as protection against the things that can go badly wrong. The way to benefit from that protection is to own a modest amount, buy it steadily rather than in a rush, and hold it for decades, not months. Bought that way, a 28% fall becomes an opportunity to rebalance, not a reason to panic.
