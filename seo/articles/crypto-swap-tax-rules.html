Quick answer: In most countries, yes. Swapping one cryptocurrency for another, for example Bitcoin for Ether or Ether for a stablecoin, is treated as selling the first coin at its market value and buying the second, so any gain since you bought the first coin is taxable at the moment of the swap, even though you never received cash. That is the rule in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Germany, Japan and India, among others. A few countries are exceptions: France and Portugal tax crypto only when you turn it into ordinary money or spend it, and Singapore and the United Arab Emirates do not tax capital gains for most individuals. Since January 2026, exchanges in the UK, the EU and dozens of other countries have been collecting customer data to send to tax authorities under a new international framework, and US exchanges now report sales on Form 1099-DA, so swaps are far more visible than they used to be. Keep a record of every swap: the date, the coins, the amounts and their value in your own currency.

Many people who trade crypto assume that tax only matters when they "cash out" into dollars, pounds or euros. It is an understandable belief. Nothing seems to have happened when you swap one token for another inside the same app: no money has reached your bank account, and your balance may even have fallen. Yet in most tax systems, that swap is exactly as taxable as selling for cash.

This guide explains why tax authorities see a swap as a sale, how the rules work in the major countries, the exceptions, how to calculate the gain, how stablecoins, DeFi and bridges are treated, the new reporting rules, and how to stay out of trouble without paying more than you owe.

Why a Swap Counts as a Sale

Tax authorities in most countries treat crypto as property or as an asset, not as currency. When you exchange one asset for another, you have disposed of the first one. The price you received is the market value of whatever you got in return.

Imagine you bought 1 Ether for $2,000 and, a year later, swapped it for Solana tokens worth $3,500. In the eyes of the US Internal Revenue Service, HM Revenue & Customs in the UK and most other authorities, you sold your Ether for $3,500 and made a gain of $1,500, and you then bought Solana for $3,500. That $3,500 becomes the cost of your Solana for working out tax when you dispose of it later.

The logic is the same as swapping a painting for a car. You would not escape tax on the painting's rise in value just because you were paid in a car rather than in cash.

The United States

Since a 2014 notice, the IRS has treated cryptocurrency as property. Every disposal, including a crypto-to-crypto swap, using crypto to pay for something and converting it to dollars, is a taxable event. The gain or loss is the US dollar value of what you received minus your cost basis, which is what you paid for the coin, including fees.

Rates

  • Short-term gains, on coins held a year or less, are taxed as ordinary income, at federal rates from 10% to 37%.
  • Long-term gains, on coins held more than a year, are taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%, depending on income.
  • Higher earners may also pay the 3.8% net investment income tax, and many states add their own income tax.

No like-kind exchange

Some traders once argued that crypto swaps could qualify as "like-kind exchanges", which let people swap one property for a similar one without paying tax. Since 2018, US law has limited like-kind treatment to real estate, and the IRS has said it did not apply to Bitcoin, Ether or Litecoin swaps even before that.

Losses

Capital losses on crypto can offset other capital gains, and up to $3,000 a year of net losses can be deducted against ordinary income, with the rest carried forward. As of 2026, the "wash sale" rule, which stops investors from selling shares at a loss and buying them straight back to claim the loss, does not apply to crypto, although Congress has considered changing that.

New reporting

US crypto brokers now send customers, and the IRS, a Form 1099-DA. For the 2025 tax year, they reported the gross proceeds of sales and swaps; from 2026, they must also report cost basis for coins bought on that platform from 1 January 2026. Coins moved in from another wallet or exchange may show proceeds with no cost, and the IRS will treat a missing cost as zero unless you provide your own records.

The United Kingdom

HMRC treats most individuals' crypto as a capital asset, so disposals are subject to capital gains tax. A swap of one token for another is a disposal, valued in pounds at the time.

  • Allowance: the annual exempt amount is £3,000 for 2026/27, down from £12,300 a few years ago.
  • Rates: 18% on gains within the basic-rate income band and 24% above it.
  • Pooling: UK rules do not track each coin separately. Each type of token goes into a "section 104 pool" with an average cost. Special rules match disposals with purchases on the same day or in the following 30 days, to stop people selling and buying straight back to create a loss.
  • Reporting: you must report disposals if your total gains are above the allowance, or if your total proceeds from disposals are more than £50,000, even if you made no gain.

If HMRC considers that you are trading as a business, which is rare for individuals, profits are taxed as income instead.

Canada

The Canada Revenue Agency treats a crypto-to-crypto trade as a barter transaction: you have disposed of one asset and acquired another at its fair market value. For most individuals, the gain is a capital gain, and half of it is added to taxable income. A proposed increase in that "inclusion rate" to two-thirds for larger gains was cancelled in 2025. If you trade frequently enough to be considered in business, the full profit is taxed as business income. Canada's "superficial loss" rule disallows a loss if you buy the same coin back within 30 days.

Australia

The Australian Taxation Office treats a crypto-to-crypto swap as a capital gains tax event. Gains are added to your income and taxed at your marginal rate, but if you held the coin for more than 12 months, only half of the gain is taxed. There is a narrow exemption for "personal use assets" costing under A$10,000, but it applies only to crypto bought to spend on personal items shortly afterwards, not to investments.

Germany and the One-Year Rule

Germany is unusual and, for long-term holders, generous. Crypto held privately for more than one year can be sold or swapped completely tax-free. Gains on coins held for a year or less are taxed as income at your personal rate, unless your total gains from such private sales in a year are below €1,000, in which case they are tax-free. That threshold is a cliff, not an allowance: at €1,001, the whole amount becomes taxable.

A swap counts as a disposal in Germany too, so swapping a coin before its first anniversary triggers tax, and the new coin starts a new one-year clock. Staking or lending coins no longer extends the holding period, after the finance ministry clarified the rules in 2022.

Countries Where Swaps Are Not Taxed

France

France taxes individuals' crypto gains, normally at a flat rate of about 30%, only when crypto is exchanged for ordinary money, goods or services. Exchanges between crypto assets, including stablecoins, are not taxed at the time; the gain is deferred until you eventually cash out. Professional traders are taxed differently.

Portugal

Portugal, once known as a crypto tax haven, introduced tax on crypto in 2023. Gains on coins held less than 365 days are taxed at 28%, while longer-held coins are generally exempt. Swaps between crypto assets are not taxed; the original cost carries over to the new coin.

Singapore and the UAE

Neither country taxes capital gains for individuals investing their own money, so swaps and sales are generally tax-free. People trading as a business can be taxed on profits in Singapore. If you live in either country but are a citizen of the US, you remain subject to US tax on worldwide income.

Japan and India: Swaps Taxed, at High Rates

Japan

In Japan, crypto gains are treated as "miscellaneous income" and added to your other income, with combined national and local rates of up to about 55%. Swaps are taxable. The government's tax reform plan for 2026 proposes a flat 20% rate on crypto traded through registered exchanges, as for shares, with losses carried forward for three years, but it depends on changes to securities law and is not expected to take effect before 2028.

India

India taxes gains from "virtual digital assets" at a flat 30%, plus surcharge and cess, with no deduction except the cost of purchase. Losses on one coin cannot be set off against gains on another or carried forward. Swaps are taxable, and a 1% tax deducted at source applies to most transfers above small limits.

How to Calculate the Gain on a Swap

The calculation has three parts:

  1. Proceeds: the market value, in your own currency, of the coins you received, at the time of the swap. Most exchanges show this, or you can use a reliable price source at that minute.
  2. Cost: what you paid for the coins you gave up, including purchase fees. If you bought them in several lots, your country's rules decide which cost you use: specific identification or first in, first out in the US; pooled average cost in the UK; first in, first out in Germany.
  3. Fees: transaction or network fees on the swap can usually be deducted, either from the proceeds or added to the cost of the new coin.

Gain = proceeds − cost − fees. The value of the coins you received then becomes their cost for the future.

A common mistake is to think that a swap made at a loss in coin terms has no tax effect. If you swapped 1 Ether worth $3,500 for tokens that later fell to $1,000, you still made the $1,500 gain on the Ether at the moment of the swap. The later fall is a separate loss, which you can only claim when you dispose of those tokens. People who traded heavily in 2021 and saw prices crash in 2022 were sometimes left with tax bills on gains they no longer had.

Stablecoins Are Crypto Too

Swapping Bitcoin for a dollar stablecoin such as USDC or USDT is a disposal of Bitcoin in almost every country that taxes swaps, even though it feels like "going to cash". Swapping one stablecoin for another is also technically a disposal, although the gain or loss is usually tiny because both are worth close to $1.

For people outside the US, there is an extra wrinkle: a dollar stablecoin is still a foreign-currency asset, so its value in pounds, euros or yen changes with exchange rates. In the UK or Germany, holding USDC while the dollar rises against your currency can create a small taxable gain when you swap or spend it.

DeFi, Bridges, Wrapping and Airdrops

Rules for newer activities are less settled, and authorities differ.

  • Wrapping and bridging (for example turning Ether into "wrapped Ether", or moving a coin to another blockchain): some authorities, and many tax software firms, treat these as non-taxable if you keep the same economic ownership, while others treat them as swaps. HMRC's guidance looks at whether you still own the same asset in substance.
  • Liquidity pools: depositing coins into a pool and receiving pool tokens in return may count as a disposal. The UK has consulted on simplifying this, but for now caution is sensible.
  • Staking and lending rewards are usually taxed as income when you receive them, at their value then, and become your cost basis.
  • Airdrops are generally taxed as income in the US when you gain control of the tokens; in the UK, airdrops received without doing anything in return may not be income, but are still subject to capital gains tax when you dispose of them.
  • NFTs bought or sold with crypto involve two disposals: the crypto you spend and, later, the NFT.

The End of Invisible Swaps

For years, many swaps went unreported because exchanges did not report them to tax authorities and individuals did not mention them. That is changing quickly.

  • The Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF), developed by the OECD, requires exchanges and brokers to collect customers' tax details and report their transactions, including swaps, to the authorities, which then share the data between countries. The UK, all EU members except one, and many others began collecting data on 1 January 2026, with the first exchanges of information in 2027. The EU's version is called DAC8. Others, including Singapore, Hong Kong and the UAE, are due to follow from 2028.
  • Form 1099-DA in the US now reports sales and swaps to the IRS.
  • Blockchain analytics firms already help tax authorities trace transactions on public blockchains.

Tax authorities have also been sending "nudge" letters to people they believe owe tax on crypto. In practice, it is now best to assume that your swaps are known to your tax authority, or soon will be.

How to Keep Good Records

For each transaction, keep:

  • the date and time;
  • the coins and amounts given up and received;
  • their value in your own currency at that moment, and the source of the price;
  • any fees;
  • wallet addresses and exchange names;
  • what the transaction was for (a swap, a purchase, a transfer between your own wallets).

Download transaction histories from each exchange at least once a year, because exchanges close, merge and delete old data. Crypto tax software can import these files and calculate gains under your country's rules, but check its results: transfers between your own wallets are often misread as sales.

Legal Ways to Reduce the Tax

  • Hold for the long term where it helps: more than a year in the US (lower rates), Germany (tax-free), Portugal (exempt) and Australia (half the gain taxed).
  • Swap less. Every swap can create a taxable gain. Frequent trading between coins multiplies tax and record-keeping.
  • Use your allowances, such as the UK's £3,000 annual exemption, by spreading disposals across tax years.
  • Harvest losses by realising losses on coins that have fallen to offset gains, within each country's rules on buying back.
  • Give or transfer to a spouse where the rules allow transfers between spouses without tax, as in the UK and US, to use both people's allowances and lower rate bands.

Avoid schemes that promise to make crypto tax disappear, such as moving coins through offshore companies. Many are ineffective, and the new reporting rules make them easier to spot.

What to Do If You Have Not Reported Swaps

If you have made taxable swaps in earlier years and not reported them, it is usually much better to correct this yourself than to wait for a letter. Most tax authorities, including the IRS and HMRC, charge lower penalties, or none, when people come forward voluntarily, and HMRC has a dedicated disclosure service for crypto. Gather your records, work out the gains for each year, and consider getting advice from an accountant who understands crypto if the amounts are significant.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is swapping Bitcoin for a stablecoin taxable?

In most countries, yes. It is a disposal of Bitcoin, and any gain since you bought it is taxable. France and Portugal are among the exceptions, taxing only when you turn crypto into ordinary money.

Is moving crypto between my own wallets taxable?

No. Transferring coins between wallets or exchanges you own is not a disposal. Network fees may be treated as a small disposal in some countries, such as the UK.

What if I swapped at a loss?

Then you have a capital loss, which can usually offset other gains. Make sure you report it, since unreported losses cannot normally be used later.

Do I pay tax if I never cashed out?

In most countries, yes, if you made swaps. Tax is due on the gain at the time of each swap, whether or not you ever converted to cash.

How do I value a swap if the exchange does not show a price?

Use a reasonable, consistent source for the market price at that time, such as a major exchange or price index, and note the source. For obscure tokens, use the value of the coin you gave up if that is easier to establish.

Are crypto-to-crypto swaps taxable in the EU?

It depends on the country. Germany, Spain, Italy and most others tax them; France and Portugal do not tax swaps between crypto assets. EU rules now require exchanges to report transactions, but tax rates are still set nationally.

Is buying crypto with dollars or pounds taxable?

No. Buying crypto with ordinary money is not a disposal. Tax arises when you sell, swap or spend it.

Final Thoughts

The idea that crypto is only taxed when it reaches your bank account is one of the costliest misunderstandings in personal finance. In most major countries, every swap is a sale. For long-term holders who rarely trade, that changes little. For active traders who move between coins dozens of times a year, it can mean a large tax bill and a mountain of records.

The new reporting rules mean that tax authorities will increasingly see the same transactions you do. The practical answer is to understand your country's rules, keep good records from the start, swap only when you have a reason to, and set aside money for tax when you take a profit, even if that profit is still sitting in another token.
