Updated November 2026

Quick answer: A single dental implant in the US usually costs $3,000 to $6,000 all in, for the titanium post, the connector (abutment) and the crown. A bone graft, an extraction or a sinus lift adds to that. Original Medicare doesn't pay for routine implants, and most dental plans cover only part of the cost, up to an annual maximum that's often $1,000 to $2,000. The surest way to know your price is an itemised quote that lists each procedure by its dental code, so you can compare dentists line by line.

What You're Paying For: Implant Cost by Component

An implant is three or four separate procedures, each billed under its own ADA procedure (CDT) code. Ask for every one to be listed:

Typical totals, consistent with what US practices quote in 2026: $3,000 to $6,000 for a single implant, post, abutment and crown. Add about $500 to $2,000 for a bone graft, $150 to $600 for an extraction, and more for a sinus lift. Implant-supported bridges and full arches ("All-on-4"-style) are priced per arch, often five figures. For the full set of options and how to pay, see our dental implant financing guide.

Why Quotes Vary So Much

  • Who places it: specialists (oral surgeons, periodontists) often charge more for the surgery, but a general dentist may refer out the hard cases anyway.
  • Bone and site: front teeth (looks matter more) and upper back teeth (sinus) are often harder and costlier.
  • Materials: implant brand, a custom versus stock abutment, and zirconia versus porcelain-fused-to-metal crowns.
  • Location: big-city practices charge more than rural ones, and dental schools often charge less, with longer timelines.
  • What's included: some quotes leave out the abutment, crown, scan or follow-up visits. Compare the total for the finished tooth.

Worked example: timing treatment around your annual maximum

A $4,500 implant on a plan paying 50% with a $1,500 annual maximum: done in one calendar year, the plan pays $1,500. If the surgery ($2,000) falls in one year and the abutment and crown ($2,500) in the next, the plan pays $2,250, because each year's maximum applies. Implants are naturally staged months apart while the bone heals, so ask your dentist whether the timing can straddle January.

Tax deduction

Dental implants count as medical expenses for the itemized deduction. Only the part of your total medical and dental expenses above 7.5% of adjusted gross income is deductible: with an AGI of $60,000, only spending above $4,500 counts (IRS Publication 502).

Over 15 to 20 years, an implant often costs about the same as a bridge that needs replacing once. Price over the long term, not just today.

How to Pay Less (Safely)

  • Get two or three itemised quotes listing CDT codes, and compare the total for the finished tooth.
  • Ask a dental school or residency program (often lower fees, supervised by specialists).
  • Use HSA or FSA money, and time treatment across plan years.
  • Ask about in-house payment plans before using a medical credit card with deferred interest.
  • Be cautious with very cheap offers or overseas "dental tourism": complications and follow-up care happen at home, and redoing a failed implant costs more than doing it right once.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a single tooth implant cost?

Usually $3,000 to $6,000 for the post, abutment and crown together, plus any graft or extraction.

Does Medicare cover dental implants?

Original Medicare doesn't cover routine dental implants. Some Medicare Advantage plans include dental benefits with limited implant coverage.

Why are dental implants so expensive?

It's surgery plus custom lab work: 3D imaging, a sterile surgical procedure, the implant system, a custom abutment and crown, and several visits over months.

Can I use my HSA for dental implants?

Yes. Implants are a qualified medical expense for HSA and FSA purposes.

How long do dental implants last?

The post can last decades with good care; the crown on top typically needs replacing after 10 to 15 years or more.

Is an implant cheaper than a bridge?

Upfront, usually not. Over 15 to 20 years the costs often even out, and an implant doesn't involve the neighbouring teeth.
