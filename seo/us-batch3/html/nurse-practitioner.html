Quick answer: A nurse practitioner (NP) is a registered nurse with a graduate degree and national certification who can examine patients, diagnose illness, order and read tests, and prescribe medicines. In 27 states and DC they can do all of this without a physician's oversight. More than 461,000 NPs are licensed in the US. The median NP earns $132,300 a year (BLS, May 2025), and the BLS expects NP jobs to grow 41% between 2025 and 2035, among the fastest of any occupation.

What Nurse Practitioners Do

  • Take histories and examine patients, including annual physicals and well-child checks.
  • Diagnose and treat acute illness and injury (infections, sprains, rashes) and manage chronic disease (diabetes, high blood pressure, asthma, depression).
  • Order and interpret lab tests, X-rays and other imaging.
  • Prescribe medicines, including controlled substances, in all 50 states and DC, under each state's rules.
  • Refer patients to specialists and coordinate care, and counsel patients on prevention and lifestyle.

Many patients see an NP as their regular primary care clinician. In 2023, more than 43% of people in traditional Medicare got care from an NP at least once.

How to Become a Nurse Practitioner

  1. Become a registered nurse. Earn a nursing degree (a BSN is usually needed for NP school) and pass the NCLEX-RN.
  2. Get clinical experience. Many programs want one to two years of RN work, and acute-care tracks often want ICU or emergency experience.
  3. Complete an NP program (MSN or DNP) in your chosen population focus, including supervised clinical hours.
  4. Pass the national certification exam for that population.
  5. Apply for an APRN licence with your state board of nursing, and for prescribing and DEA registration.

From the start of a BSN, the route usually takes 6 to 8 years, including RN work. Accelerated and direct-entry programs for people with a bachelor's in another field can shorten it.

Paying for NP school after July 1, 2026

Federal Grad PLUS loans ended for new borrowers on July 1, 2026. Graduate nursing students can now borrow up to $20,500 a year and $100,000 in total in federal unsubsidized loans. Nursing isn't on the list of "professional" degrees that get the higher $50,000 and $200,000 limits. Employer tuition benefits, scholarships, and the National Health Service Corps and Nurse Corps programs (in exchange for service) cover much of the rest for many students.

Where NPs Can Practise on Their Own

State law sets the "practice environment". Under full practice, in 27 states plus DC, NPs evaluate, diagnose, treat and prescribe under the board of nursing alone; several of these states first require a set period of supervised practice. Under reduced or restricted practice, NPs need a collaborative agreement or supervision from a physician for some or all of their work. Check your state board of nursing before choosing where to train and work, because the rules affect which jobs you can take and whether you can open your own practice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a nurse practitioner be your primary care provider?

Yes. NPs serve as the regular primary care clinician for millions of patients and can be listed as your PCP by most health plans.

Is a nurse practitioner a doctor?

Not a medical doctor. An NP with a Doctor of Nursing Practice (DNP) holds a doctorate in nursing, but NPs are licensed as advanced practice registered nurses, not physicians.

Can nurse practitioners prescribe medication?

Yes, in all 50 states and DC, including controlled substances with DEA registration; some states add limits or require a collaborative agreement.

How long does it take to become a nurse practitioner?

Usually 6 to 8 years from starting a BSN: four years for the BSN, one to two years of RN experience, and two to four years of NP school.

What is the difference between an NP and an RN?

An RN carries out care plans and patient care; an NP, with a graduate degree and certification, diagnoses, treats and prescribes. The median NP earns $34,750 more a year.

What is the difference between an NP and a PA?

NPs train in nursing and certify in a patient population; PAs train in a generalist medical model and can move between specialties more easily. Pay is close.

Can nurse practitioners open their own practice?

Yes, in full-practice states. In other states they need a collaborating or supervising physician.
