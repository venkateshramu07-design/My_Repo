Updated November 2026

Quick answer: Compounded tirzepatide is a pharmacy-made copy of the active ingredient in Zepbound and Mounjaro. It isn't FDA-approved. Compounders could make copies legally in large volumes only while tirzepatide was on the FDA shortage list. The FDA declared the shortage over on December 19, 2024, and its grace periods ended on February 18, 2025 for state-licensed pharmacies and March 19, 2025 for outsourcing facilities. Pharmacies may still compound a version that is genuinely different for an individual patient's documented medical need, but routine copies are no longer allowed. As of May 31, 2026 the FDA had received more than 730 adverse event reports linked to compounded tirzepatide. Meanwhile brand-name Zepbound is sold direct to self-pay patients from $299 a month.

Is Compounded Tirzepatide Legal Now?

  • Copies are not. A pharmacy may not compound drugs that are "essentially a copy" of an approved, available drug regularly or in large amounts. FDA's threshold is more than four prescriptions a month of the same copy.
  • Adding vitamins doesn't change that. The FDA has said that combining a GLP-1 with an extra ingredient such as vitamin B12, at strengths within 10% of available products, can still be treated as a copy.
  • Narrow exceptions remain: a 503A pharmacy can compound a version with a change that produces a significant, documented clinical difference for a specific patient, such as a needed removal of an ingredient the patient is allergic to. A different dose alone usually isn't enough.
  • Outsourcing facilities (503B) can't compound tirzepatide from bulk ingredient, because it isn't on the 503B bulks list or the shortage list, and FDA has proposed excluding it permanently.

Telehealth companies and med spas that still advertise cheap "compounded tirzepatide" in 2026 are operating at or past the edge of these rules. FDA has issued warning letters to sellers since 2025.

Safety Concerns the FDA Has Flagged

  • No FDA review: compounded versions aren't checked for safety, effectiveness or quality before sale.
  • Adverse events: as of May 31, 2026, the FDA had received more than 730 reports for compounded tirzepatide and 990 for compounded semaglutide, probably an undercount because ordinary pharmacies don't have to report them.
  • Dosing errors: multi-dose vials measured in units or mL, not mg, have led to overdoses, some needing hospital care.
  • Fraudulent products: labels naming pharmacies that never made the product, and one reported reaction from such a fake.
  • Unverified ingredients: active ingredient imported from unregistered overseas makers. In September 2025 the FDA started a "green list" import alert to let in only ingredients from sources it has checked.
  • "Research use only" peptides sold online aren't compounded medicines at all and have no safety oversight.

If You're Using Compounded Tirzepatide Now

  1. Don't stop abruptly without a plan. Talk to your prescriber about moving to an FDA-approved product.
  2. Check the pharmacy is licensed in your state (your state board of pharmacy has a lookup), and ask whether the product is made for you specifically and why.
  3. Confirm the dose in milligrams, and how many units or mL that is for your vial's concentration. Ask for a demonstration.
  4. Price the brand: self-pay Zepbound may cost about the same and gives you an approved, consistent product.
  5. Report side effects to your prescriber and to FDA MedWatch.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is compounded tirzepatide still available in 2026?

Some sellers still offer it, but routine copies have not been permitted since the FDA's grace periods ended in February and March 2025. Legitimate compounding is limited to individual patients with a documented clinical need.

Is compounded tirzepatide the same as Zepbound?

It's meant to contain the same active ingredient, but it isn't FDA-approved, and its strength, purity and sterility aren't verified the same way.

Why did compounded tirzepatide get banned?

It wasn't banned outright. The legal basis for mass copying was the shortage, and the FDA declared the shortage over in December 2024.

Is compounded tirzepatide safe?

The FDA has received hundreds of adverse event reports and warns about dosing errors, fake products and unverified ingredients. FDA-approved versions are the safer choice.

How much does Zepbound cost without insurance?

From $299 a month for the 2.5 mg starting dose through Lilly's self-pay program, rising to $699 for the highest doses (prices checked November 2026).
