Quick answer: A foreign transaction fee is a charge, usually up to 3% of the purchase, that some credit cards add when you pay in a foreign currency or buy from a merchant based abroad, even from home online. A card with no foreign transaction fee saves $30 on every $1,000 you spend abroad. When the card terminal asks, always choose to pay in the local currency, not U.S. dollars. "Paying in dollars" (dynamic currency conversion) lets the merchant set the exchange rate, often with a markup bigger than the fee you just avoided.

Federal rules treat these fees as a finance charge. The regulators' own example shows how the 3% builds up: 1% charged by the card network plus 2% added by the issuing bank. The fee applies to purchases in a foreign currency, to purchases processed outside the U.S. (some online shops), and to foreign ATM cash advances.

The Bigger Trap: "Pay in Dollars?" (Dynamic Currency Conversion)

Card terminals, hotels and ATMs abroad often offer to charge you in U.S. dollars. That's dynamic currency conversion: the merchant or ATM operator converts the price at its own rate, usually with a markup, and the card issuer doesn't have to show that markup as a fee. Even on a no-fee card, accepting it costs you money. On a $2,500 trip with a 4% conversion markup, you'd lose $100, more than the 3% fee you avoided. Always choose the local currency; your card network then converts at its wholesale-based rate.

Which Cards Usually Have No Foreign Transaction Fee

  • Travel rewards cards (airline, hotel and general travel cards): almost all waive it.
  • Many no-annual-fee cards now waive it too, especially from issuers that market to travellers, and many credit union cards.
  • Cards that often still charge it: many store cards, basic cash-back cards and some student and secured cards. Check the pricing table, not the marketing page.

A card's terms can change. Look in the "Transaction Fees" row of the card's current pricing table, which issuers must show before you apply and in your account agreement.

Online Purchases From Abroad

You can be charged a foreign transaction fee without leaving home if an online shop, app store or subscription is processed outside the U.S., even when the price is shown in dollars. If you often buy from overseas sellers, a no-fee card is worth it even if you rarely travel.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a foreign transaction fee?

A charge, typically up to 3% of the amount, that some card issuers add to purchases made in a foreign currency or processed by a merchant outside the U.S.

Do debit cards charge foreign transaction fees?

Many do, often 1% to 3%, plus a fee for using an out-of-network ATM abroad (and the foreign ATM's own fee). Some checking accounts waive or refund both.

Should I pay in local currency or dollars abroad?

Local currency. Choosing dollars triggers dynamic currency conversion at the merchant's rate, which usually costs more than any card fee.

Are foreign transaction fees charged in Canada or Mexico?

Yes, if your card has them. Any purchase in Canadian dollars or pesos, or processed by a foreign merchant, can incur the fee.

Can I get a foreign transaction fee refunded?

Sometimes. Call the issuer and ask, especially if you've been a customer for a long time. Better, apply for a no-fee card before your trip.

Does a card with no foreign transaction fee need an annual fee?

No. Many no-annual-fee cards also have no foreign transaction fee.
