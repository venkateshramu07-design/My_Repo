Quick answer: A tort is a civil wrong: an act or failure to act that harms someone and lets them sue for compensation. There are three kinds. Intentional torts include battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass and fraud. Negligence means failing to take reasonable care. Strict liability makes you liable for harm caused by defective products, dangerous animals or abnormally dangerous activities even if you were careful. Tort cases are brought by the injured person, not the state, and usually end in money damages.

Tort vs Crime vs Breach of Contract

TortCrimeBreach of contract
Who brings the caseThe injured person (plaintiff)The government (prosecutor)The other party to the contract
PurposeCompensate the victimPunish and deterGive the benefit of the bargain
Standard of proofPreponderance of the evidence (more likely than not)Beyond a reasonable doubtPreponderance of the evidence

One act can be both a tort and a crime. A punch is criminal battery and a tort; the victim can sue even if the attacker is acquitted, because the civil standard of proof is lower.

1. Negligence: The Most Common Tort

A plaintiff must prove five elements:

ElementQuestion the court asks
DutyDid the defendant owe the plaintiff a duty of reasonable care? (Drivers owe it to everyone on the road.)
BreachDid they fall short of what a reasonable person would have done?
Cause in factWould the harm have happened but for the breach?
Proximate causeWas the harm a foreseeable result, close enough to hold them responsible?
DamagesDid the plaintiff suffer actual harm: injury, property damage or financial loss?

How courts weigh "reasonable care": the Hand formula

Judge Learned Hand put it as a comparison. A defendant is negligent if the burden of a precaution (B) is less than the probability of harm (P) times the size of the loss (L). Example: a precaution costs $2,000. Without it there is a 1% chance of a $500,000 injury, an expected loss of $5,000. Because $2,000 is less than $5,000, skipping the precaution looks negligent.

Common negligence claims

  • Car, truck and motorcycle accidents
  • Slip-and-fall and other premises liability
  • Medical malpractice (professional negligence, judged against the standard of care in the field)
  • Negligent hiring or supervision by employers
  • Dog bites (negligence or strict liability, depending on the state)

2. Intentional Torts

TortWhat it is
BatteryIntentional harmful or offensive contact with another person
AssaultIntentionally causing someone to reasonably fear an imminent battery (no contact needed)
False imprisonmentIntentionally confining someone without legal justification
Intentional infliction of emotional distressExtreme and outrageous conduct causing severe emotional distress
Trespass to landIntentionally entering someone's land without permission
Trespass to chattels and conversionInterfering with someone's property; conversion is serious enough to require paying its full value
Fraud (deceit)A knowingly false statement intended to be relied on, which causes loss
DefamationA false statement of fact that harms reputation (libel when written, slander when spoken); public figures must also prove "actual malice"

"Intentional" means the defendant meant to do the act, or knew the result was substantially certain. They need not have meant to cause the injury.

3. Strict Liability

Liability without fault applies in three main areas:

  • Defective products: manufacturers and sellers are liable for injuries caused by manufacturing defects, design defects or inadequate warnings.
  • Abnormally dangerous activities, such as blasting or storing hazardous chemicals.
  • Wild animals, and in many states domestic animals with known dangerous tendencies (including dog-bite statutes).

Defenses

DefenseEffect
Comparative negligence (most states)Damages are reduced by the plaintiff's share of fault. In "modified" states the plaintiff recovers nothing if 50% or 51% or more at fault
Contributory negligence (Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, Virginia and DC)Any fault by the plaintiff, however small, bars recovery
Assumption of riskThe plaintiff knowingly accepted a known risk (for example, by signing a valid waiver)
ConsentA defense to intentional torts (for example, contact sports within the rules)
Self-defense and defense of othersReasonable force in response to a threat
Statute of limitationsThe deadline to sue, often 2 to 3 years for personal injury, varying by state and claim

Damages

  • Compensatory: economic losses (medical bills, lost wages, property damage) plus non-economic losses (pain and suffering, loss of enjoyment of life).
  • Punitive: to punish especially reckless or malicious conduct. The Supreme Court has said ratios to compensatory damages above single digits will rarely be constitutional (State Farm v. Campbell, 2003).
  • Caps: many states limit non-economic or punitive damages, especially in medical malpractice cases.

Famous Tort Cases Worth Knowing

  • Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad (N.Y. 1928): no liability for an unforeseeable plaintiff. It's the classic case on duty and proximate cause.
  • United States v. Carroll Towing (2d Cir. 1947): the origin of the Hand formula.
  • Liebeck v. McDonald's (N.M. 1994): the hot-coffee case. The jury found 20% fault on the plaintiff, and the $2.7 million punitive award was cut to $480,000 before a confidential settlement.
  • Rylands v. Fletcher (England 1868): the root of strict liability for dangerous uses of land.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the three types of torts?

Intentional torts, negligence, and strict liability.

What is an example of a tort?

A driver who runs a red light and injures a pedestrian commits the tort of negligence; a person who punches someone commits the tort of battery.

Is a tort a crime?

No, a tort is a civil wrong. The same act can be both, and lead to a criminal prosecution and a civil lawsuit.

What is tort reform?

Laws that limit lawsuits or damages, such as caps on non-economic or punitive damages, shorter filing deadlines, or limits on lawyers' fees, especially in medical malpractice.

What is the difference between negligence and gross negligence?

Negligence is a failure of reasonable care; gross negligence is an extreme departure from it, showing reckless disregard for others. It can support punitive damages and defeat liability waivers.

How long do you have to file a tort claim?

It depends on the state and claim: commonly 2 to 3 years for personal injury, sometimes much shorter for claims against government bodies.
