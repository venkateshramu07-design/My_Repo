Quick answer: IQ (intelligence quotient) is a score from standardised tests that measure certain mental abilities, such as reasoning, problem-solving, working memory, processing speed and verbal comprehension, compared with others of the same age. Scores are set so the average is 100, and about two-thirds of people score between 85 and 115. IQ predicts some outcomes, like academic performance, reasonably well, but it doesn't capture creativity, emotional intelligence, practical skills, character or motivation.

Few psychological measures are as famous, or as controversial, as IQ. Some people treat an IQ score as a final verdict on a person's potential; others dismiss it as meaningless. The truth lies in between.

This guide explains what IQ tests measure, where they came from, what scores mean, what influences intelligence and the important limits of IQ as a measure of human ability.

What Is IQ?

An IQ score compares your performance on a set of cognitive tasks with a large reference group of people your age. Modern tests are designed so that:

  • The average score is 100.
  • The standard deviation is 15.
  • About 68% of people score between 85 and 115.
  • About 95% score between 70 and 130.

A Brief History of IQ Testing

  • 1905: French psychologist Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon developed a test to identify children who needed extra educational support.
  • 1912: German psychologist William Stern proposed the term "intelligence quotient", calculated as mental age divided by chronological age, multiplied by 100.
  • 1916: Lewis Terman at Stanford adapted Binet's test into the Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scales.
  • 1939 onward: David Wechsler developed tests now widely used, including the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) and the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC).

Unfortunately, IQ tests were also misused in the early 20th century to justify discriminatory immigration policies and forced sterilisation programmes. This history is one reason IQ remains sensitive today.

What IQ Tests Measure

Modern tests such as the WAIS measure several areas:

  • Verbal comprehension: vocabulary, general knowledge and understanding concepts.
  • Perceptual or fluid reasoning: solving new problems and recognising patterns.
  • Working memory: holding and manipulating information in mind.
  • Processing speed: how quickly you can complete simple mental tasks accurately.

Psychologists often refer to a general factor of intelligence, "g", first proposed by Charles Spearman, because people who do well in one area tend to do well in others.

Fluid vs Crystallised Intelligence

Psychologist Raymond Cattell, later joined by John Horn, divided general intelligence into two broad types, a distinction still widely used today:

  • Fluid intelligence is the ability to solve new problems, spot patterns and think logically without relying on prior knowledge. Puzzles like completing a sequence of shapes test fluid intelligence.
  • Crystallised intelligence is the knowledge and skills you've accumulated over time: vocabulary, facts, and know-how in your field.

The two follow different paths through life. Fluid intelligence tends to peak in early adulthood and slowly declines with age, while crystallised intelligence often keeps growing well into middle age and beyond. This helps explain why older people may solve unfamiliar puzzles more slowly yet outperform younger people in areas that depend on experience and judgement.

The Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) model, which builds on this work, describes a hierarchy of abilities with general intelligence at the top and broad abilities such as reasoning, knowledge, memory, visual processing and speed beneath it. Many modern IQ tests are designed around this model.

What Happens in a Professional IQ Test?

If you or your child are referred for an IQ assessment, it helps to know what to expect. A typical assessment:

  • Is carried out one-to-one by a qualified psychologist, usually over one to two hours, sometimes with breaks.
  • Includes a variety of short tasks: defining words, explaining similarities between concepts, arranging blocks to match a pattern, completing visual puzzles, repeating sequences of numbers, and quickly matching symbols.
  • Starts each task with easy items and becomes harder until the person reaches their limit, so nobody is expected to answer everything.
  • Produces an overall score along with separate index scores for areas such as verbal comprehension, reasoning, working memory and processing speed.

The psychologist then writes a report explaining the results in context. The pattern of strengths and weaknesses across index scores is often more useful than the overall number. For example, a child with strong reasoning but slow processing speed may need extra time in exams rather than easier work.

Assessments are commonly used to identify learning difficulties, giftedness, the effects of brain injury, or to support diagnoses alongside other information. They are not meant to rank people's worth.

What Influences IQ?

Genetics

Twin and adoption studies suggest genes play a substantial role in differences in IQ between people, especially in adulthood.

Environment

  • Nutrition, particularly in early childhood.
  • Education and intellectually stimulating environments.
  • Exposure to toxins such as lead.
  • Socioeconomic conditions and stress.

The Flynn Effect

Researcher James Flynn documented that average IQ scores rose substantially in many countries during the 20th century, roughly three points per decade. This was far too fast to be genetic, showing that environmental factors such as better nutrition, education and health matter. In some countries the rise has recently slowed or reversed.

What IQ Predicts, and What It Doesn't

IQ Correlates With

  • School grades and years of education.
  • Performance in many jobs, especially complex ones.
  • Some health and income outcomes, on average.

IQ Does Not Fully Capture

  • Creativity and original thinking.
  • Emotional intelligence: understanding and managing emotions and relationships.
  • Practical intelligence: street smarts and common sense.
  • Motivation, persistence and character, which strongly affect success.
  • Specialised talents in music, sport, art and crafts.

Howard Gardner's theory of multiple intelligences and Robert Sternberg's triarchic theory both argue that intelligence is broader than IQ tests suggest, although these theories are debated among researchers.

IQ Across the Lifespan

Intelligence develops and changes throughout life:

  • Early childhood: scores are less stable, and early measurements are weaker predictors of adult IQ. Nutrition, language exposure, play and responsive caregiving are especially important.
  • School years: IQ becomes increasingly stable, and education contributes to cognitive growth.
  • Adulthood: relative rankings are fairly stable, though individual scores can shift by several points.
  • Older age: processing speed and fluid reasoning usually slow, while knowledge and vocabulary hold up well. Physical activity, social engagement, learning and managing conditions such as high blood pressure are linked with better cognitive health in later life.

Interestingly, studies of identical twins suggest that the influence of genes on IQ differences actually increases from childhood to adulthood, possibly because adults choose environments that match their abilities and interests.

Gifted Children: Signs and Support

Children with very high ability often show some of these signs:

  • Learning to read or calculate early and quickly.
  • Asking deep, persistent questions and showing intense curiosity.
  • An exceptional memory and wide vocabulary.
  • Strong focus on topics they love, sometimes to the exclusion of other things.
  • A strong sense of fairness, sensitivity or perfectionism.

Gifted children still need support. Without enough challenge they may become bored, disengaged or disruptive in class. Some are "twice exceptional", meaning they are gifted and also have a learning difficulty such as dyslexia or ADHD, which can hide both their strengths and their needs. Parents can help by providing stimulating activities, connecting children with peers who share their interests, praising effort rather than intelligence, and talking with the school about enrichment or acceleration options.

Intellectual Disability: An Important Distinction

A low IQ score alone does not mean someone has an intellectual disability. Diagnosis requires three things: significant limitations in intellectual functioning (usually an IQ around 70 or below), significant difficulties with everyday adaptive skills such as communication, self-care and social skills, and onset during the developmental period. People with intellectual disabilities can learn, work, build relationships and live fulfilling lives with the right support.

Common Myths About IQ

Myth: IQ is fixed at birth

Genes matter, but IQ is shaped by environment, especially in childhood. Education, nutrition and health all influence scores, as the Flynn effect clearly shows.

Myth: IQ tests measure everything that matters

They measure important thinking skills, but not creativity, wisdom, emotional skills, character or motivation.

Myth: Online quizzes give you your real IQ

Without proper standardisation, online scores are unreliable and are often designed to flatter.

Myth: Very high IQ people struggle socially

Large studies generally find that most highly intelligent people are as socially and emotionally well-adjusted as others. The stereotype of the lonely genius describes some individuals, not the group.

Myth: We only use 10% of our brains

Not related to IQ directly, but a popular myth. Brain imaging shows we use virtually all of our brain, just not every region at the same time.

IQ, Mensa and High-IQ Societies

Mensa, founded in England in 1946, is the best-known high-IQ society. Membership is open to people who score in the top 2% of the population on an approved, supervised intelligence test, roughly an IQ of 130 or above on most scales. Other societies set even higher thresholds. Members often join for social connection, discussion groups and shared interests. Mensa membership reflects a test score, not achievement, but many members value the community.

Is IQ Testing Fair? The Cultural Bias Debate

Critics have long argued that IQ tests can favour people from particular cultural, linguistic or educational backgrounds. Vocabulary and general knowledge questions, for example, depend on what a person has been exposed to. Test developers respond by norming tests on large, diverse samples, translating and adapting tests carefully for different countries, and including non-verbal reasoning tasks.

Most psychologists agree that tests used properly are useful for the people and purposes they were designed for, but that scores must always be interpreted with awareness of a person's language, schooling, health and circumstances. Group differences in average scores are heavily influenced by environmental factors and say nothing about any individual.

Famous People With Reported High IQs

You'll often see claims about the IQs of famous figures like Albert Einstein, Isaac Newton or Leonardo da Vinci. Most of these are estimates, as these people never took modern IQ tests. Some modern figures, such as mathematician Terence Tao, were identified as exceptionally gifted in childhood. What these individuals share is not only cognitive ability but also curiosity, persistence and years of focused work.

How IQ Scores Are Calculated Today

Early IQ tests used the "ratio" formula: mental age divided by actual age, multiplied by 100. A ten-year-old performing like a typical twelve-year-old would score 120. This worked poorly for adults, because mental abilities don't keep growing steadily with age.

Modern tests use a deviation IQ instead. Test makers give the test to a large, representative sample of people of each age group, called the norm group. Your raw score is then compared with people your own age and converted to a scale where the average is 100 and the standard deviation is 15. A score of 115 means you scored one standard deviation above the average for your age, higher than about 84% of that group.

Because populations change over time, tests are re-normed every so often. A person taking an old, out-of-date version may get an inflated score, which is one practical consequence of the Flynn effect.

Every test also has a margin of error. Professional reports often give a confidence range, such as 104 to 114, rather than a single exact number, because performance varies a little from day to day.

Can You Increase Your IQ?

Research suggests:

  • Education appears to raise IQ scores; studies estimate a gain of a few points per additional year of schooling.
  • Brain-training games usually improve performance on the trained tasks but show limited evidence of broad intelligence gains.
  • Healthy habits, including good sleep, exercise, nutrition and managing stress, help your brain perform at its best.
  • Learning new, challenging skills keeps the mind active and builds knowledge.

Practical Ways to Think More Clearly Every Day

Whatever your IQ, you can use your mind more effectively. These habits don't change your test score overnight, but they improve how well you think, learn and decide:

  1. Protect your sleep. Sleep strengthens memory and restores attention. Even one night of poor sleep noticeably reduces concentration and reasoning.
  2. Move your body. Regular aerobic exercise improves blood flow to the brain and is linked with better memory and executive function.
  3. Read widely. Reading builds vocabulary, knowledge and the ability to follow complex arguments, all parts of crystallised intelligence.
  4. Learn something hard. A new language, a musical instrument or a programming language challenges the brain in ways routine tasks don't.
  5. Reduce multitasking. Switching between tasks costs time and accuracy. Focus on one demanding task at a time.
  6. Write things down. Notes, lists and diagrams free up working memory for actual thinking.
  7. Question your first answer. Many reasoning errors come from going with the first intuitive answer. Pausing to ask "what else could be true?" catches many mistakes.
  8. Stay socially connected. Conversation and debate expose you to new ideas and keep your mind active, especially in later life.

IQ and Success at Work

Research in workplace psychology has found that general mental ability is one of the better single predictors of job performance, especially in complex roles that require learning, problem-solving and handling new information. But it is far from the whole story:

  • Conscientiousness, being organised, reliable and hardworking, also predicts performance across nearly all jobs.
  • Experience and skill matter enormously once people have learned a role.
  • Social and emotional skills are essential in leadership, sales, teaching, healthcare and teamwork.
  • Integrity and motivation determine whether ability is actually used well.

Many employers now use structured interviews, work samples and situational judgement tests alongside, or instead of, cognitive tests to build a fuller picture of candidates.

Grit, Mindset and Other Ingredients of Achievement

Psychologist Angela Duckworth's research on grit, passion and perseverance for long-term goals, suggests that sustained effort is a key ingredient in achievement, sometimes as important as talent. Carol Dweck's work on growth mindset explores how believing abilities can be developed may encourage people to take on challenges and learn from failure. Both ideas have been debated, and their effects in studies are often smaller than popular books suggest, but the core message is sound: ability provides potential, while effort, practice and good habits turn it into results.

Online IQ Tests: A Warning

Free online IQ tests are mostly for entertainment. They are rarely standardised or validated and often inflate scores. Proper IQ assessments are administered by trained psychologists.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good IQ score?

Anything between 90 and 110 is average. Scores above 120 are considered high, and above 130 very high.

Does IQ change over time?

IQ is fairly stable in adulthood, but it can change, especially during childhood, and is affected by education, health and environment.

Is IQ the same as intelligence?

IQ measures some important aspects of intelligence, but not all of it. Creativity, emotional and practical intelligence are also important.

Does a high IQ guarantee success?

No. Long-term studies of gifted people show that motivation, opportunity, personality and relationships play major roles in life outcomes.

What is the average IQ by country?

You may see lists ranking countries by average IQ, but many are based on limited, outdated or poorly sampled data and should be treated with great caution. Differences between countries are strongly linked to education, health and economic conditions, and they change over time.

What is the highest possible IQ score?

Most professionally used tests have a ceiling of around 160. Claims of IQs of 200 or more usually come from old ratio-based calculations in childhood or unverified estimates, not modern standardised testing.

Can stress or anxiety lower my IQ test score?

Yes. Anxiety, tiredness, illness and unfamiliarity with the testing situation can all lower performance on the day. Professional examiners take this into account when interpreting results.

Is there a link between IQ and happiness?

The link is weak. Research suggests relationships, health, purpose and financial security have far more influence on happiness than IQ does.

Do men and women differ in IQ?

Studies generally find little or no difference in average overall IQ between men and women. Some small differences appear on specific tasks, but they are modest, overlap heavily and say nothing about any individual.

Does playing chess or doing puzzles raise IQ?

Puzzles and games like chess are enjoyable mental exercise and build specific skills, but research finds little evidence that they raise general intelligence on their own. They're still worth doing for fun, focus and social connection.

Should I get my child's IQ tested?

Testing is most useful when there's a specific question, such as possible learning difficulties or giftedness affecting school placement. Discuss it first with teachers, a paediatrician or an educational psychologist.

Final Thoughts

IQ is a useful tool for measuring certain thinking skills, and it predicts some outcomes reasonably well. But it's only one part of what makes a person capable. Curiosity, effort, creativity, kindness and resilience don't show up on an IQ test, yet they often matter just as much. Whatever your score, the habit of learning and growing is what truly shapes your potential.
