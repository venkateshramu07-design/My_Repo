Quick answer: The US dollar is the world's main reserve currency because the United States has the world's largest economy and deepest financial markets, the dollar is freely convertible, US Treasury bonds are seen as among the safest and most liquid assets, and the country has relatively strong legal institutions. Historical events, especially the 1944 Bretton Woods agreement and the dollar pricing of oil from the 1970s, cemented its role, and network effects keep it there: because everyone uses dollars, it's convenient for everyone to keep using them. The dollar makes up close to 60% of the world's official foreign exchange reserves. Its dominance faces challenges from geopolitics, sanctions, rising US debt and alternatives such as the euro, the Chinese yuan and gold, but a sudden replacement appears unlikely.

Central banks around the world, from the Reserve Bank of India to the Bank of Japan, hold large reserves of foreign currency to stabilise their own currencies, pay for imports and protect against crises. Most of those reserves are held in US dollars. The dollar is also the most widely used currency in international trade, borrowing and financial markets.

This article explains what a reserve currency is, how the dollar achieved its position, what keeps it there, the benefits and costs for the United States and the world, the challenges to dollar dominance, and what it all means for India.

What Is a Reserve Currency?

A reserve currency is a foreign currency held in significant amounts by central banks and governments as part of their foreign exchange reserves. Reserve currencies are used to:

  • Pay for imports and settle international debts
  • Intervene in currency markets to stabilise the domestic currency
  • Provide confidence to investors and lenders
  • Serve as a store of value in times of crisis
  • Price commodities and international contracts

A Brief History of Global Currencies

The dollar isn't the first dominant global currency. Over the centuries, the Spanish silver dollar, the Dutch guilder and the British pound sterling each played central roles in international trade.

In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, sterling dominated, backed by Britain's vast empire, trade networks and the City of London's financial power. After the First and especially the Second World War, Britain's economic strength declined, while the United States emerged as the world's leading economy and creditor.

Bretton Woods (1944)

At the Bretton Woods conference, Allied nations established a system in which the US dollar was convertible to gold at US$35 an ounce and other currencies were pegged to the dollar. The International Monetary Fund and the World Bank were created at the same time. This formally placed the dollar at the centre of the global monetary system.

The End of Gold Convertibility (1971)

By the late 1960s, the US could no longer guarantee gold convertibility for the growing supply of dollars abroad. In 1971, President Richard Nixon suspended convertibility, and floating exchange rates followed. Many expected the dollar's role to decline, but it didn't.

The Petrodollar Era

In the 1970s, oil came to be priced and traded in dollars, with oil exporters investing surplus dollars in US assets. This created steady global demand for dollars and reinforced the currency's central role.

Why the Dollar Remains Dominant

1. The Size of the US Economy

The United States has the world's largest economy by nominal GDP, giving the dollar a large base of trade and investment.

2. Deep, Liquid Financial Markets

US Treasury markets are the deepest and most liquid government bond markets in the world. Investors can buy and sell enormous amounts quickly with little effect on prices. No other country offers safe assets on the same scale.

3. Convertibility and Open Capital Markets

The dollar can be freely exchanged, and investors can move money in and out of US markets with few restrictions.

4. Rule of Law and Institutions

Strong legal protections for property and contracts, an independent central bank and established financial regulation give investors confidence.

5. Network Effects

Because most trade, debt and commodities are already priced in dollars, it's convenient for businesses and governments to keep using them. Banks, payment systems and contracts are built around the dollar, making switching costly.

6. Safe-Haven Status

During global crises, investors typically buy dollars and US Treasuries, even when the crisis starts in the United States, as happened in 2008.

7. Military and Political Power

US geopolitical influence and security alliances have supported confidence in the dollar and its use by allied countries.

The Dollar by the Numbers

According to the IMF's data on the currency composition of official foreign exchange reserves (COFER), the US dollar has accounted for close to 60% of allocated global reserves in recent years, down from around 70% in 2000. The euro holds roughly 20%, with the Japanese yen, British pound, Chinese yuan and other currencies making up the rest in much smaller shares.

The Bank for International Settlements' triennial surveys of global currency markets have found that the dollar is involved in close to 90% of all foreign exchange transactions. A large share of international trade invoicing and cross-border lending is also in dollars.

The Dollar in Trade and Finance

Beyond reserves, the dollar plays several other global roles:

  • Trade invoicing: research by economist Gita Gopinath and others has highlighted the "dominant currency paradigm": a large share of world trade is invoiced in dollars even when neither the buyer nor seller is American. This means dollar movements affect trade prices worldwide.
  • International borrowing: governments and companies in many countries issue bonds and take loans in dollars to access a wider pool of investors.
  • Commodity pricing: oil, metals and many agricultural products are priced in dollars.
  • Anchor for other currencies: several countries peg their currencies to the dollar, including Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Hong Kong.
  • Central bank swap lines: during crises such as 2008 and 2020, the US Federal Reserve provided dollars to other major central banks through swap lines to calm markets.

Benefits for the United States

  • Lower borrowing costs: strong global demand for Treasuries helps keep US interest rates lower.
  • Ability to run large deficits: the US can finance trade and budget deficits more easily than most countries.
  • Seigniorage: foreigners hold US currency and assets, effectively providing the US with low-cost financing.
  • Reduced currency risk: US companies trade and borrow in their own currency.
  • Financial power: the dollar's central role gives the US the ability to impose financial sanctions with global reach.

Costs and Risks for the United States

  • A stronger dollar can make US exports less competitive and contribute to trade deficits.
  • The Triffin dilemma: economist Robert Triffin argued that a reserve-currency country must supply the world with its currency, often through deficits, which can eventually erode confidence.
  • Responsibility: US monetary policy has global effects, sometimes creating international tension.

Effects on the Rest of the World

  • Exposure to US monetary policy: when the US Federal Reserve raises interest rates, capital can flow out of emerging markets, weakening their currencies.
  • Dollar debt risk: countries and companies that borrow in dollars face higher repayment costs if their currencies fall.
  • Need for reserves: countries hold large dollar reserves as insurance, which can be costly.
  • Stability and convenience: a common international currency reduces transaction costs and simplifies trade.

Dollar Cycles and Emerging Markets

The dollar tends to move in long cycles of strength and weakness, and these swings ripple through emerging economies. When US interest rates rise and the dollar strengthens, global investors often pull money from emerging markets, pushing their currencies down and their borrowing costs up.

India experienced this sharply in 2013 during the "taper tantrum", when signals that the US Federal Reserve would slow its bond purchases triggered capital outflows. The rupee fell to record lows against the dollar that August, and India was grouped by some analysts among the "Fragile Five" economies seen as vulnerable to such shocks. India responded with measures to attract foreign deposits and rebuild reserves, and has since built a much larger reserve buffer. Similar pressures appeared in 2022, when rapid US interest rate rises strengthened the dollar against most currencies, including the rupee.

The Dollar in Everyday Indian Life

  • Students abroad: families paying tuition in the US and many other countries feel every movement in the exchange rate.
  • Remittances: India is the world's largest recipient of remittances, receiving more than US$100 billion a year in recent years, much of it originating in dollars or dollar-linked currencies. A stronger dollar means more rupees for recipient families.
  • IT and services exports: companies earning in dollars benefit when the rupee weakens.
  • Fuel and electronics: prices of imported goods and fuel rise when the rupee falls.
  • Travel: foreign holidays become more expensive when the dollar strengthens.
  • Investments: Indians investing in US stocks gain or lose from currency movements as well as share prices.

Challenges to Dollar Dominance

Geopolitics and Sanctions

The freezing of Russia's central bank reserves after its 2022 invasion of Ukraine prompted some countries to reconsider holding all their reserves in Western currencies.

Rising US Debt

Large and growing US government debt and political disputes over the debt ceiling have raised concerns about long-term fiscal sustainability.

Central Bank Gold Buying

Central banks have been buying gold at historically high levels. According to the World Gold Council, central bank net purchases exceeded 1,000 tonnes a year in 2022, 2023 and 2024, partly reflecting a desire to diversify away from the dollar.

The Chinese Yuan

China has promoted international use of the yuan through trade settlement, currency swap lines and its Cross-Border Interbank Payment System (CIPS). In 2016, the IMF added the yuan to the basket of currencies that make up its Special Drawing Right (SDR), alongside the dollar, euro, yen and pound. But capital controls, limited convertibility and concerns about legal transparency limit the yuan's reserve role.

The Euro

The euro is the second most important reserve currency, but the eurozone lacks a single, unified government bond market equivalent to US Treasuries.

Local Currency Trade and New Payment Systems

Countries are increasingly settling some bilateral trade in local currencies and experimenting with digital payment links and central bank digital currencies.

Lessons From the Decline of Sterling

The British pound's loss of reserve status offers lessons. Sterling remained an important reserve currency for decades after Britain's economic leadership faded, partly because of habit and the sterling area of Commonwealth countries. Its decline was gradual, unfolding over several decades and accelerated by wartime debts, devaluations and the rise of the US economy. Economists such as Barry Eichengreen have noted that the dollar overtook sterling in some international roles earlier than often assumed, in the 1920s, showing that leadership can shift, but usually through long processes rather than sudden events.

Digital Currencies and the Future of Payments

Technology is creating new ways to move money across borders. Many central banks are researching or piloting central bank digital currencies, and some are experimenting with cross-border platforms that link them. India launched pilots of its digital rupee (e-rupee) in 2022. Fast payment systems such as India's UPI are being linked with those of other countries for remittances and payments. Stablecoins, private digital tokens typically pegged to the dollar, have grown rapidly and, paradoxically, may extend the dollar's reach. These developments could reduce friction in non-dollar payments over time, but they don't by themselves replace the need for deep, trusted financial markets.

What It Means for India

  • Foreign exchange reserves: India holds large reserves, a significant share in dollar assets, to stabilise the rupee and cover imports. Reserves reached around US$700 billion at times in 2024 and 2025.
  • Oil and imports: most of India's oil imports are paid in dollars, so the rupee-dollar exchange rate affects inflation and the trade deficit.
  • US interest rates: Federal Reserve decisions influence foreign investment flows into Indian markets and the rupee.
  • Rupee internationalisation: the Reserve Bank of India has introduced arrangements allowing trade to be invoiced and settled in rupees, and India has signed local-currency settlement agreements with some partners, such as the UAE.

How Central Banks Manage Reserves

Central banks aim for three goals when managing reserves: safety, liquidity and return, usually in that order. Reserves must be safe from default, available quickly in a crisis and, where possible, earn some income. That's why much of global reserves sits in government bonds of major economies, deposits with other central banks and the Bank for International Settlements, gold, and IMF reserve positions and SDRs. Diversifying across currencies and assets reduces risk, but most central banks still keep the dollar as their largest holding because of its unmatched liquidity.

Key Takeaways

  • The dollar became the main reserve currency after the Second World War and has kept that role for decades.
  • Its dominance rests on economic size, deep markets, convertibility, institutions and network effects.
  • The dollar's share of global reserves has declined gradually but remains close to 60%.
  • Challenges include sanctions, US debt, gold buying, the euro, the yuan and new payment technologies.
  • For India, the dollar affects oil costs, inflation, capital flows and the rupee.

Possible Futures for the Global Currency System

  • Continued dollar dominance: the dollar remains the leading currency, with gradual erosion of its share.
  • A multipolar system: the dollar shares its role more evenly with the euro, yuan, gold and regional currencies.
  • Regional blocs: trade within regions increasingly settles in local currencies, while the dollar dominates global finance.
  • Technology-driven change: digital currencies and linked payment systems reduce reliance on traditional correspondent banking.

Most economists expect some combination of these, unfolding over decades rather than years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the US dollar the world's reserve currency?

Because of the size of the US economy, its deep financial markets, the dollar's convertibility, strong institutions, historical agreements like Bretton Woods, oil pricing and network effects.

What percentage of global reserves are in dollars?

IMF data shows the dollar accounts for close to 60% of allocated global foreign exchange reserves.

Can the Chinese yuan replace the dollar?

Not in the near future. Capital controls, limited convertibility and less developed financial markets restrict the yuan's reserve role, though its use in trade is growing.

What would happen if the dollar lost its reserve status?

US borrowing costs would likely rise and the dollar might weaken, while the global financial system would need to adjust to a more multipolar arrangement. Such a shift would probably be gradual.

What is the SDR?

The Special Drawing Right is an international reserve asset created by the IMF, valued based on a basket of five currencies: the US dollar, euro, Chinese yuan, Japanese yen and British pound.

Why do central banks buy gold?

Gold has no default risk, isn't tied to any single country's policies and has historically held value in crises. Some central banks have increased gold holdings to diversify away from dollar assets.

Is the rupee becoming an international currency?

India is gradually promoting rupee use in trade, through rupee invoicing and local-currency agreements, but the rupee is not yet fully convertible on the capital account, which limits its international role.

Why does the rupee fall when the US raises interest rates?

Higher US interest rates make dollar assets more attractive, so global investors may move money out of emerging markets like India, increasing demand for dollars and weakening the rupee.

Which countries peg their currency to the dollar?

Several, including Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Hong Kong and Panama, which uses the dollar directly. Pegs provide stability for trade and investment but tie local monetary policy to US decisions.

Does a strong dollar help or hurt India?

Both. It helps exporters and families receiving remittances but raises the cost of oil, imports, foreign education and dollar debt.

What is the "exorbitant privilege"?

A phrase associated with French official Valéry Giscard d'Estaing in the 1960s, describing the advantages the US gains from issuing the world's main reserve currency, such as cheaper borrowing.

How does the US Federal Reserve affect other countries?

Fed interest rate decisions influence global borrowing costs, capital flows and exchange rates, because so much international debt and trade is in dollars. Emerging markets often feel these effects strongly.

Has any currency replaced a reserve currency before?

Yes. The dollar gradually replaced the British pound during the twentieth century, a process that took several decades.

Final Thoughts

The US dollar's role as the global reserve currency rests on economic size, financial depth, institutional strength, history and the powerful habit of global use. These advantages bring the United States significant benefits and the world a convenient common currency, but also create dependencies and tensions. While the dollar's share of reserves has slowly declined and alternatives are growing, its dominance is likely to evolve gradually rather than disappear suddenly. For countries like India, understanding the dollar's role helps explain everything from fuel prices to stock market movements.
