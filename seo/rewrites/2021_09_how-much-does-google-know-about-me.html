Quick answer: Depending on which services you use and your settings, Google may know your name, email, phone number, search and YouTube history, places you've visited, apps you use, contacts, calendar events, photos, purchases found in Gmail receipts and your approximate interests for advertising. You can see most of this in your Google Account under "Data & privacy" and "My Ad Center", download it with Google Takeout, and limit it by turning off or auto-deleting Web & App Activity, Location History (Timeline) and YouTube History.

Google is part of daily life for billions of people. We use it to search, navigate, email, store photos, watch videos and run our phones. In exchange for these free services, Google collects data, much of which helps personalise services and target advertising.

This guide explains what Google may know about you, where it comes from, how it's used and practical steps to see and control it.

Where Google Gets Your Data

  • Things you provide: your name, date of birth, email, phone number and profile photo when you create an account.
  • Things you create: emails in Gmail, files in Drive, photos in Google Photos, calendar events and contacts.
  • Your activity: searches, websites visited in Chrome when synced, YouTube videos watched, apps used on Android and voice commands to Google Assistant.
  • Location: GPS, Wi-Fi and IP address data from your devices and Google Maps.
  • Third-party websites and apps: sites using Google Analytics, Google ads or "Sign in with Google" can share some activity data with Google.

What Google May Know About You

Your Identity and Contacts

Basic profile information, phone number, recovery email and the contacts synced from your phone.

Your Searches and Browsing

If Web & App Activity is on, Google stores your searches, websites visited through Chrome (if synced) and activity in Google apps. This can reveal health concerns, travel plans, financial interests and more.

Your Location

If location features are enabled, Google Maps Timeline can record places you've visited and routes you've travelled. In recent years Google has moved Timeline data to be stored on users' devices by default rather than in the cloud, but settings vary by account and device.

Your Viewing Habits

YouTube watch and search history shapes recommendations and ads.

Your Interests and Ad Profile

Google infers interests, such as travel, fitness or technology, and broad demographics to personalise ads. You can view and adjust these in My Ad Center.

Your Purchases and Reservations

Receipts and booking confirmations in Gmail can be used to show purchases, flights and reservations within your Google Account. Google has stated it doesn't use Gmail content to personalise ads.

Your Voice and Photos

Voice and audio activity may be saved if you opt in. Google Photos can recognise faces, places and objects in your photos to help you search them.

Your Devices and Apps

Information about your phone model, operating system, installed apps and usage, particularly on Android devices.

How Google Uses Your Data

  • Personalising search results, recommendations and maps.
  • Showing relevant ads, which fund most of its free services.
  • Improving products and developing new features, including AI features.
  • Protecting accounts from fraud and suspicious logins.
  • Responding to legal requests from governments, which Google reports on in its Transparency Report.

Why Free Services Need Your Data

Google Search, Gmail, Maps and YouTube are free to use, yet running them costs billions of dollars a year in data centres, engineers and infrastructure. Most of that is paid for by advertising. Advertising has consistently made up the large majority of the revenue of Alphabet, Google's parent company.

Advertisers pay more when they can reach people who are likely to be interested in their products. The more Google understands about what people search for, watch and do, the better it can match ads to them, and the more advertisers are willing to pay. This is why data is so central to Google's business model, and why the saying "if you're not paying for the product, you are the product" is often quoted, although Google stresses that it does not sell personal information.

Understanding this trade-off doesn't mean you must stop using Google services. It means you can make informed choices about what you share in return for convenience.

A Day in Your Data Trail

To see how quickly data builds up, imagine an ordinary day for someone using an Android phone and Google services:

  • 7:00 am: the alarm goes off and the phone checks the weather for your current location.
  • 8:15 am: you use Google Maps to check traffic on your commute; Location History, if enabled, records your route.
  • 9:30 am: at work, you search for a restaurant for a team lunch and open two reviews.
  • 1:00 pm: you pay with Google Pay and receive the receipt in Gmail.
  • 4:00 pm: you watch a YouTube video about running shoes during a break.
  • 8:00 pm: you ask the Assistant to set a reminder and play music.
  • 10:30 pm: you browse news sites in Chrome while signed in, and several of them use Google ad services.

Depending on your settings, that single day may record your home and work locations, your commute, your lunch plans, a purchase, an interest in running, your music tastes and your news reading habits. Over months and years, this builds a very detailed picture.

How to See What Google Knows About You

  1. Go to myaccount.google.com and open Data & privacy.
  2. Open My Activity (myactivity.google.com) to see searches, app activity and YouTube history.
  3. Check Timeline in Google Maps for location history.
  4. Visit My Ad Center to see the topics and brands Google associates with you.
  5. Use Google Takeout (takeout.google.com) to download a copy of your data.

How to Limit What Google Collects

1. Use Activity Controls

  • Pause or turn off Web & App Activity.
  • Turn off Timeline / Location History.
  • Pause YouTube History.

2. Turn On Auto-Delete

Set activity to automatically delete after 3, 18 or 36 months. For newer accounts, Google sets 18-month auto-delete by default for some activity.

3. Run the Privacy Checkup

Google's Privacy Checkup walks you through the most important settings in a few minutes.

4. Manage Ad Personalisation

In My Ad Center, you can turn off personalised ads or remove specific interests.

5. Review App Permissions

On your phone, review which apps can access location, microphone, camera and contacts. Choose "only while using the app" where possible.

6. Use Incognito Mode Wisely

Incognito mode stops Chrome from saving history on your device, but websites, your employer or school and your internet provider may still see your activity.

7. Consider Privacy-Focused Alternatives

Some people use alternatives such as DuckDuckGo for search, Firefox or Brave for browsing, or Proton Mail for email. Each has trade-offs in convenience and features.

8. Secure Your Account

Use a strong password, enable two-step verification or passkeys and run Google's Security Checkup. A hacked account exposes far more than ad data.

Step-by-Step: Download Your Google Data With Takeout

Google Takeout lets you download a copy of much of the data stored in your account. It's a useful way to see the scale of what's stored, and to keep a backup.

  1. Go to takeout.google.com while signed in to your Google Account.
  2. Choose which products to include. By default, everything is selected; you can deselect large items like Google Photos if you only want to explore activity data.
  3. Choose the file type, size and delivery method, such as a download link by email or adding to Google Drive.
  4. Click "Create export". For large accounts, the export can take hours or even days.
  5. Download and open the files. Activity data usually comes as web pages or JSON files you can browse.

Many people are surprised by what they find: years of search history, every YouTube video they've watched, voice recordings, and location records from places they'd long forgotten visiting.

Protecting Children's Privacy

Children under 13 (the age varies by country) can't normally create their own standard Google Account, but parents can create a supervised account through Google Family Link. Family Link lets parents:

  • Approve or block apps from the Play Store.
  • Set screen time limits and bedtimes.
  • Manage privacy and activity settings on the child's account.
  • See the device's location.

YouTube Kids offers a separate, more restricted environment for younger children, and supervised YouTube experiences are available for older children. Talk to children about what personal information they should never share online, such as their address, school or phone number.

Google vs Other Tech Companies

Google is not alone in collecting data. Most large tech companies gather significant information, but their business models differ:

  • Meta (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp): relies heavily on advertising, using data about your interests, connections and activity. WhatsApp messages are end-to-end encrypted, but metadata, such as who you contact and when, may be collected.
  • Amazon: collects detailed shopping, browsing and Alexa voice data, used for recommendations and its growing advertising business.
  • Apple: earns most of its money from hardware and services, and markets privacy as a selling point with features like App Tracking Transparency, although it also runs an advertising business.
  • Microsoft: collects data through Windows, Bing, LinkedIn and Office, used for products, security and advertising.

Whichever services you use, the same principle applies: review your settings and share only what you're comfortable with.

Signs Your Google Account May Be Compromised

Privacy and security go together. If someone else gains access to your account, they can see everything Google stores about you. Watch for:

  • Security alerts about sign-ins from devices or locations you don't recognise.
  • Emails in your Sent folder that you didn't write.
  • Changed recovery phone numbers or email addresses.
  • Unexpected password reset emails.

If you notice any of these, change your password immediately, sign out of all other sessions from the Security page, check recovery details and turn on 2-Step Verification. Consider using a passkey, which is resistant to phishing.

Privacy Laws That Protect You

Depending on where you live, laws give you rights to access, correct and delete personal data, such as the EU's GDPR, California's CCPA/CPRA and India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. These laws have pushed companies, including Google, to provide more transparency and control tools.

How Advertising Profiles Are Built

Google uses signals from your activity to estimate broad interest categories and demographics. You may see categories such as "Travel", "Fitness", "Personal finance" or "Home and garden" in your ad settings, along with an estimated age range. These estimates are drawn from things like:

  • Searches you make while signed in.
  • YouTube videos you watch.
  • Websites and apps you use that partner with Google's advertising services.
  • Your general location, such as your city.
  • Information you've added to your account, such as your age.

Google says it doesn't use sensitive categories, such as health conditions, religion or sexual orientation, to personalise ads, and its policies restrict advertisers from targeting on such topics. You can view, edit or remove interest categories in My Ad Center, or turn off personalised ads entirely. Turning them off doesn't reduce the number of ads you see; it just makes them less tailored.

Browser Choices and Tracking

Your browser plays a big role in how much tracking happens across the web. Things you can do:

  • Review Chrome's privacy settings: look at cookie controls, ad privacy settings and Safe Browsing options.
  • Consider whether to stay signed in to Chrome: signing in syncs history and bookmarks across devices, which is convenient but links more browsing to your account.
  • Try privacy-focused browsers: Firefox, Brave, Safari and DuckDuckGo's browser block many third-party trackers by default.
  • Use content-blocking extensions carefully: reputable tracker blockers can reduce tracking, but install extensions only from trusted developers, since extensions themselves can see a lot of your activity.
  • Clear cookies regularly, or set the browser to clear them when it closes.

The Benefits of Sharing Some Data

Privacy is about choice, not about turning everything off. Some data sharing genuinely improves your experience:

  • Location data gives you accurate traffic updates and helps find a lost phone.
  • Search history improves suggestions and helps you find pages you visited before.
  • Photo face grouping makes it easy to find pictures of family members.
  • Security signals, such as unusual sign-in detection, help protect your account from hackers.

The goal is to keep the features you value and switch off the ones you don't, rather than accepting every default setting without thinking.

Google, Smart Home Devices and Wearables

Data collection doesn't stop at phones and computers. Smart speakers, displays, thermostats, cameras and wearables can collect additional information:

  • Smart speakers and displays: listen for the wake word ("Hey Google") and send audio to Google after it's detected. You can review and delete recordings, and use the physical microphone switch for extra privacy.
  • Home cameras and doorbells: may store video clips in the cloud depending on your subscription and settings.
  • Fitbit and smartwatches: collect health and fitness data such as steps, heart rate and sleep. Google has said Fitbit health data won't be used for Google ads.

When setting up any smart device, take a few minutes to read the privacy options and decide which features you really need.

An Illustrative Example: Looking at My Activity for the First Time

Imagine someone opening My Activity for the first time and finding search history going back more than eight years, including searches for an old job, a medical symptom they'd once worried about and dozens of holiday plans. The Timeline showed visits to places they'd forgotten. Nothing was secret, but seeing it all together felt uncomfortable. In ten minutes, they deleted older history, set auto-delete to 18 months, paused YouTube search history and turned off ad personalisation for sensitive interests. The services kept working, and they felt far more in control.

Privacy Myths and Facts

  • Myth: "I have nothing to hide, so privacy doesn't matter." Fact: privacy isn't about hiding wrongdoing. Detailed data can be misused if accounts are hacked, can reveal sensitive patterns about health or finances, and can shape what information and prices you see.
  • Myth: "Deleting my history removes all trace." Fact: deleting activity removes it from your account, but some information may be kept for limited periods for security, fraud prevention or legal reasons, as Google's privacy policy explains.
  • Myth: "Turning off ad personalisation stops data collection." Fact: it changes how ads are chosen for you, but activity controls determine what's saved to your account. You need to adjust both.
  • Myth: "Privacy settings are too complicated." Fact: Google's Privacy Checkup walks you through the main choices in about ten minutes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Google listen to my conversations?

Google says Assistant only listens for its activation phrase and records after it is activated. You can review and delete voice recordings and turn off audio saving in your account settings.

Can I delete all my Google data?

You can delete activity history and specific items, or delete your entire Google Account. Some data may be retained for a period for legal or security reasons.

Does Google sell my data?

Google states that it does not sell personal information. It uses data to show targeted ads, which advertisers pay for.

Is using Google services safe?

Google invests heavily in security, but privacy depends on your settings. Reviewing them regularly helps you strike the right balance between convenience and privacy.

Does Incognito mode stop Google from tracking me?

Incognito mode stops Chrome from saving your browsing history, cookies and form data on your device after you close the window. Websites, your employer or school, and your internet provider may still see your activity. If you sign in to a Google service in Incognito, that activity can be saved to your account.

Does Google know my location if Location History is off?

Possibly. Your approximate location may still be estimated from your IP address, and some apps and services, such as Search or Maps, may use location when you use them, depending on your Web & App Activity and device settings. Review both your Google Account and phone location settings.

Can Google read my emails?

Google's systems automatically process Gmail content to provide features like spam filtering, smart replies and organising travel bookings. Google stopped using Gmail content to personalise ads in 2017. Human access to email content is limited to specific cases, such as security investigations, with your permission or as required by law.

How long does Google keep my data?

It depends on your settings. Since 2020, new accounts have auto-delete set to 18 months by default for Web & App Activity and Location History. You can choose 3, 18 or 36 months, or keep data until you delete it manually.

Does using a VPN hide my activity from Google?

A VPN hides your real IP address from websites and your internet provider, but if you're signed in to your Google Account, Google can still link your activity to you. A VPN is most useful on public Wi-Fi and for hiding your location from websites.

Does Google share my data with the government?

Google may disclose user data in response to valid legal requests, such as court orders. It publishes a Transparency Report showing how many requests it receives from governments around the world and how it responds.

Final Thoughts

Google can know a great deal about you, but much of it depends on your choices and settings. Take 15 minutes to visit your Google Account, review your activity, turn on auto-delete and adjust ad settings. You'll keep the convenience of Google's services while taking back more control over your personal information.
