Updated 20 October 2026

Quick answer: You can control diabetes by combining a balanced diet, regular physical activity, a healthy weight, good sleep, stress management, not smoking and taking your medication exactly as prescribed. Monitor your blood sugar, have regular check-ups (including HbA1c, eye, kidney and foot checks) and work closely with your doctor. For many people with type 2 diabetes, lifestyle changes can significantly improve blood sugar, and some can even reach remission. Never stop or change medication without medical advice.

Diabetes affects hundreds of millions of people worldwide, and the number keeps rising. It is a long-term condition in which blood glucose (sugar) levels stay too high because the body either doesn't produce enough insulin or can't use it effectively. Over time, high blood sugar can damage the heart, blood vessels, kidneys, eyes and nerves.

The encouraging news is that diabetes can be managed well. People who understand their condition and build healthy daily habits can live long, active lives with a much lower risk of complications. This guide explains how.

This article is for general information and does not replace advice from your doctor or diabetes care team.

Understanding the Types of Diabetes

  • Type 1 diabetes: an autoimmune condition in which the body destroys the cells that make insulin. People with type 1 need insulin every day.
  • Type 2 diabetes: the most common type. The body becomes resistant to insulin and gradually produces less of it. It is strongly linked to weight, inactivity, diet and genetics.
  • Gestational diabetes: develops during pregnancy and usually goes away after birth, but raises the risk of type 2 diabetes later.
  • Prediabetes: blood sugar is higher than normal but not yet in the diabetes range. Lifestyle changes at this stage can often prevent diabetes.

1. Eat a Balanced, Diabetes-Friendly Diet

Food has the most direct effect on blood sugar. There is no single "diabetes diet", but certain principles help almost everyone.

Foods to Eat More Often

  • Non-starchy vegetables: leafy greens, broccoli, cauliflower, beans, okra, cucumbers and tomatoes.
  • Whole grains in sensible portions: oats, brown rice, millets, whole wheat and quinoa.
  • Legumes: lentils, chickpeas and beans, which are rich in fibre and protein.
  • Lean proteins: fish, eggs, chicken, tofu, paneer and Greek yoghurt.
  • Healthy fats: nuts, seeds, olive oil and avocado.
  • Whole fruits: in moderate portions, ideally with fibre-rich skins, rather than juice.

Foods to Limit

  • Sugary drinks, including soft drinks, sweetened tea and packaged juices.
  • Refined carbohydrates such as white bread, white rice in large portions, pastries and biscuits.
  • Sweets and desserts, which are best kept for occasional small portions.
  • Fried foods and processed meats.
  • Excess salt, which raises blood pressure.

Practical Eating Tips

  • Use the plate method: fill half your plate with vegetables, a quarter with protein and a quarter with whole-grain carbohydrates.
  • Eat at regular times and avoid long gaps followed by large meals.
  • Pair carbohydrates with protein, fat or fibre to slow the rise in blood sugar.
  • Read food labels for total carbohydrates and added sugars.
  • Ask for a referral to a dietitian for a personalised plan.

2. Be Physically Active

Exercise helps your muscles use glucose and improves insulin sensitivity for hours afterwards. Health guidelines generally recommend:

  • At least 150 minutes of moderate activity per week, such as brisk walking, cycling or swimming.
  • Strength training two or three times a week.
  • Breaking up long periods of sitting. Even a 10-minute walk after meals can lower blood sugar spikes.

If you take insulin or certain tablets, check with your doctor about avoiding low blood sugar during exercise, and carry a fast-acting sugar source.

3. Reach and Maintain a Healthy Weight

For people with type 2 diabetes who are overweight, losing even 5% to 10% of body weight can noticeably improve blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol. Research such as the UK's DiRECT trial has shown that significant weight loss can put type 2 diabetes into remission for some people. Focus on sustainable habits rather than crash diets, and seek medical guidance for larger weight-loss goals.

4. Take Medication as Prescribed

Medication is not a failure; it is a tool. Depending on your type and needs, your doctor may prescribe metformin, other tablets, injectable medicines such as GLP-1 receptor agonists, or insulin.

  • Take medicines at the right time and dose.
  • Don't stop or change medication without talking to your doctor, even if you feel well.
  • Learn the signs of low blood sugar (shakiness, sweating, confusion, hunger) and how to treat it.
  • Bring all your medicines, including supplements, to appointments.

5. Monitor Your Blood Sugar

Monitoring shows how food, activity, stress and medication affect you. Depending on your treatment, this may be finger-prick testing or a continuous glucose monitor (CGM). Your doctor will also check your HbA1c, which reflects average blood sugar over about three months. Many adults with diabetes aim for an HbA1c around 7% (53 mmol/mol) or below, but targets should be personalised.

6. Manage Stress

Stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline raise blood sugar. Chronic stress also makes it harder to eat well, sleep and exercise.

  • Practise deep breathing, meditation or yoga.
  • Spend time outdoors and with supportive people.
  • Seek help if you feel overwhelmed. "Diabetes distress" is common and treatable.

7. Prioritise Sleep

Poor or short sleep increases insulin resistance and appetite. Aim for seven to nine hours of good-quality sleep. Snoring with pauses in breathing can be a sign of sleep apnoea, which is common in type 2 diabetes and worth discussing with your doctor.

8. Quit Smoking and Limit Alcohol

Smoking increases insulin resistance and greatly raises the risk of heart disease, stroke, kidney damage and foot problems in people with diabetes. Quitting is one of the best things you can do. Alcohol can cause low blood sugar, especially with insulin or some tablets, and adds empty calories. If you drink, do so in moderation and with food.

9. Attend Regular Check-Ups

Regular checks catch complications early, when they are easier to treat:

  • HbA1c every three to six months.
  • Blood pressure at every visit and cholesterol at least yearly.
  • Kidney function (blood and urine tests) yearly.
  • A dilated eye exam or retinal screening yearly.
  • Foot checks at every visit, plus daily self-checks for cuts, blisters or numbness.
  • Dental check-ups, as diabetes raises the risk of gum disease.

Know the Warning Signs and Risk Factors

Many people live with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes for years without knowing it. Recognising the signs and risk factors allows earlier diagnosis and better outcomes.

Common Symptoms of High Blood Sugar

  • Feeling very thirsty and passing urine more often, especially at night.
  • Unusual tiredness or lack of energy.
  • Blurred vision.
  • Cuts and wounds that heal slowly.
  • Frequent infections, such as thrush, urinary or skin infections.
  • Unexplained weight loss, particularly in type 1 diabetes.
  • Tingling or numbness in the hands or feet.

Type 2 diabetes often causes few or no symptoms early on, which is why screening is important.

Risk Factors for Type 2 Diabetes

  • Being overweight, especially carrying fat around the waist.
  • Physical inactivity.
  • A family history of diabetes.
  • Age, with risk rising after 35 to 45, although type 2 diabetes is increasingly seen in younger adults.
  • Ethnicity: people of South Asian, African, Caribbean, Hispanic and some other backgrounds have higher risk, and South Asians tend to develop diabetes at lower body weights.
  • A history of gestational diabetes or polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).
  • High blood pressure or abnormal cholesterol.

If several of these apply to you, ask your doctor about a blood test such as fasting glucose or HbA1c.

Understanding Carbohydrates and the Glycaemic Index

Carbohydrates have the biggest direct effect on blood sugar, but not all carbohydrates behave the same way.

  • Glycaemic index (GI) ranks foods by how quickly they raise blood sugar compared with pure glucose. Low-GI foods, such as most legumes, whole oats, many fruits and whole grains, release sugar more slowly.
  • Glycaemic load (GL) considers both GI and the amount of carbohydrate in a portion, which makes it more useful in practice. A small portion of a high-GI food may affect blood sugar less than a large portion of a medium-GI food.
  • Fibre slows digestion and absorption of sugar. Vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts and seeds are good sources.
  • Processing matters: whole oats raise blood sugar more slowly than instant oats, and whole fruit more slowly than juice.
  • Cooking and cooling: cooked and cooled rice, potatoes and pasta contain more resistant starch, which may modestly reduce their effect on blood sugar.

Carbohydrate Counting

People who use insulin, especially those with type 1 diabetes, are often taught to count the grams of carbohydrate in their meals and match their insulin dose. Structured education programmes, such as DAFNE in the UK, teach this skill. Even if you don't use insulin, learning roughly how much carbohydrate is in your usual meals helps you make better choices.

A Sample Day of Diabetes-Friendly Eating

Here is an example of balanced meals using common foods. Portions should be adjusted for your needs, and a dietitian can personalise this for your culture, preferences and medication.

  • Breakfast: vegetable omelette or besan chilla with a slice of whole-grain toast, or a bowl of plain oats with nuts, seeds and a few berries. Tea or coffee without sugar.
  • Mid-morning: a handful of unsalted nuts or a small piece of fruit with plain yoghurt.
  • Lunch: a plate with half vegetables (such as salad and a cooked vegetable curry), a quarter dal, chicken, fish or paneer, and a quarter brown rice or one or two whole-wheat or millet rotis.
  • Afternoon: roasted chana, cucumber and carrot sticks with hummus, or buttermilk.
  • Dinner: grilled fish or tofu with stir-fried vegetables and a small portion of quinoa, or a lentil and vegetable soup with a salad.
  • Drinks: water, unsweetened tea, coffee or herbal teas throughout the day.

Exercise Ideas for People With Diabetes

  • Walking: the easiest way to start. Aim to build up to 30 minutes on most days, and try a 10 to 15-minute walk after meals.
  • Cycling or swimming: gentle on the joints and good for heart health.
  • Strength training: using weights, resistance bands or bodyweight exercises like squats and push-ups helps muscles use glucose.
  • Yoga and tai chi: improve flexibility, balance and stress levels.
  • Everyday activity: taking stairs, gardening, housework and standing during phone calls all add up.

Exercise Safety Tips

  • Check blood sugar before and after exercise if you use insulin or medicines that can cause low blood sugar.
  • Carry glucose tablets or a sugary drink.
  • Wear well-fitting shoes and check your feet afterwards.
  • Stay hydrated.
  • Talk to your doctor before starting vigorous exercise, especially if you have heart, eye or nerve complications.

Preventing Diabetes Complications

High blood sugar over many years can damage blood vessels and nerves. Good control significantly lowers the risk of complications. Major studies, including the UK Prospective Diabetes Study, showed that better blood sugar and blood pressure control reduce eye, kidney and nerve damage.

  • Heart and blood vessels: people with diabetes have a higher risk of heart attack and stroke. Control blood pressure and cholesterol, don't smoke and stay active.
  • Eyes (retinopathy): regular screening catches changes early, when treatment works best.
  • Kidneys (nephropathy): yearly urine and blood tests detect early kidney damage. Some diabetes medicines also protect the kidneys.
  • Nerves (neuropathy): numbness, tingling or pain in the feet. Check feet daily and never walk barefoot if you have reduced sensation.
  • Feet: small injuries can become serious infections. Report any cut, blister, colour change or swelling that doesn't heal.

Managing Diabetes During Special Situations

When You're Sick

Illness and infection can raise blood sugar. Keep taking your medication unless your doctor advises otherwise, check your blood sugar more often, drink plenty of fluids and ask your healthcare team for a "sick day plan" in advance.

During Festivals and Celebrations

Celebrations often involve sweets and rich food. Eat a balanced meal before going out, choose small portions of your favourites, increase activity that day and avoid sugary drinks.

While Fasting

Religious fasting can affect blood sugar and medication needs. Talk to your doctor before fasting to adjust medicines and timing, and know when you must break the fast for safety.

When Travelling

Carry medicines in your hand luggage with a doctor's letter, pack extra supplies, keep snacks handy and adjust medication timing across time zones with medical advice.

Diabetes and Mental Health

Living with a condition that needs daily attention can be emotionally exhausting. People with diabetes are more likely to experience depression and anxiety, and "diabetes distress", the frustration, worry and burnout that come from constant self-management, is very common.

  • Talk openly with your healthcare team about how you're feeling, not just your numbers.
  • Join a diabetes support group, in person or online, to share experiences with others who understand.
  • Set realistic goals and celebrate progress rather than aiming for perfection.
  • Ask family and friends for practical support, such as cooking healthy meals together or walking with you.
  • Seek professional help if low mood or anxiety persists. Treating depression often improves diabetes control too.

Building Your Diabetes Care Team

Good diabetes care is a team effort. Depending on your needs, your team may include:

  • Your GP or primary care doctor: for regular reviews, prescriptions and referrals.
  • An endocrinologist or diabetologist: a specialist for complex cases, type 1 diabetes or insulin management.
  • A diabetes nurse or educator: to teach monitoring, injections and self-management skills.
  • A dietitian: for personalised eating plans.
  • An eye specialist: for retinal screening.
  • A podiatrist: for foot care.
  • A pharmacist: to answer questions about medicines and interactions.

Bring a list of questions and your blood sugar records to appointments, and don't hesitate to ask for explanations in plain language.

Myths About Diabetes

  • "Eating sugar causes diabetes." Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune and unrelated to diet. Type 2 is linked to excess weight, inactivity and genetics; a diet high in sugary drinks contributes to weight gain and risk, but sugar alone isn't the single cause.
  • "People with diabetes can never eat sweets." Small portions can fit into a balanced plan, especially as part of a meal.
  • "Insulin means you've failed." Type 2 diabetes often progresses over time, and many people eventually need insulin. It is a highly effective treatment, not a punishment.
  • "Diabetes isn't serious if you feel fine." High blood sugar damages the body silently, which is why monitoring and check-ups matter.
  • "Thin people don't get type 2 diabetes." Weight is a major risk factor, but genetics and fat stored around organs mean people of normal weight can develop it too, particularly in South Asian populations.

Common Mistakes in Diabetes Management

  • Skipping medication when blood sugar seems fine.
  • Replacing sugar with large portions of "sugar-free" processed snacks.
  • Cutting out all carbohydrates without guidance, then overeating later.
  • Relying on unproven remedies instead of prescribed treatment.
  • Ignoring foot injuries or vision changes.

When to Seek Urgent Medical Help

Get immediate help for very high blood sugar with vomiting, deep breathing, confusion or drowsiness (possible diabetic ketoacidosis), or for low blood sugar that does not improve with treatment or causes unconsciousness.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can diabetes be cured?

Type 1 diabetes currently has no cure. Type 2 diabetes cannot usually be cured, but some people achieve remission, with normal blood sugar without medication, through significant weight loss and lifestyle change.

Can people with diabetes eat fruit?

Yes. Whole fruits contain fibre, vitamins and antioxidants. Watch portion sizes and choose whole fruit over juice.

Is rice bad for diabetes?

Rice is not forbidden. Smaller portions, brown or mixed rice and pairing it with vegetables and protein can reduce blood sugar spikes.

What is a normal fasting blood sugar level?

For people without diabetes, fasting blood sugar is usually below 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L). A fasting level of 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher on two tests suggests diabetes. Your doctor will set your personal targets.

Do herbal remedies like bitter gourd or cinnamon help?

Some foods may have small effects, but they cannot replace medication. Talk to your doctor before trying supplements, as some interact with diabetes medicines.

Can I drink tea or coffee if I have diabetes?

Yes. Plain tea and coffee are fine for most people. The problem is usually added sugar, sweetened syrups and large amounts of full-fat milk or cream. Try reducing sugar gradually until you get used to the taste.

Is intermittent fasting safe for diabetes?

Some people with type 2 diabetes use time-restricted eating to lose weight and improve blood sugar, but fasting can cause dangerous lows for those on insulin or certain tablets. Only try it under medical supervision with adjusted medication.

How often should I check my blood sugar?

It depends on your treatment. People on insulin may test several times a day or use a continuous monitor, while those managing with diet or metformin alone may test less often. Your doctor will advise what's right for you.

Final Thoughts

Living well with diabetes is built on everyday choices: balanced meals, regular movement, good sleep, managing stress, taking medicines correctly and keeping up with check-ups. You don't have to change everything at once. Start with one or two habits, track how your body responds and keep working with your healthcare team. Small, steady improvements add up to better blood sugar and a healthier future.
