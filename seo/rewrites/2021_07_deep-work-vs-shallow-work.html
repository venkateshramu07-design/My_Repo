Quick answer: Deep work is focused, distraction-free work on cognitively demanding tasks that create real value, such as writing, coding, designing, analysing or learning hard skills. Shallow work is logistical, easy-to-repeat work often done while distracted, such as email, routine meetings and admin. The idea was popularised by Cal Newport in his 2016 book Deep Work. To do more deep work, schedule protected focus blocks, remove distractions, batch shallow tasks and build the habit gradually.

Most knowledge workers spend their days answering messages, attending meetings and switching between tabs. At the end of the day they feel busy but not productive. The problem isn't laziness; it's that the work that really matters often requires long, uninterrupted concentration, which modern workplaces rarely allow.

This guide explains the difference between deep and shallow work, why deep work is so valuable, and practical strategies to do more of it.

What Is Deep Work?

Cal Newport, a computer science professor at Georgetown University, defines deep work as "professional activities performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that push your cognitive capabilities to their limit." These efforts create new value, improve your skills and are hard to replicate.

Examples include:

  • Writing a report, article or book chapter.
  • Programming a complex feature.
  • Designing a product or strategy.
  • Analysing data to solve a difficult problem.
  • Studying a challenging subject.

What Is Shallow Work?

Newport defines shallow work as "non-cognitively demanding, logistical-style tasks, often performed while distracted." These tasks are often necessary, but they don't create much new value and are easy for others to do.

Examples include:

  • Checking and replying to emails and chat messages.
  • Scheduling meetings.
  • Filling in routine forms or reports.
  • Status meetings with little decision-making.
  • Browsing the web for work-related news.

Why Deep Work Matters

It Produces Better Results

Complex problems require holding many ideas in mind at once. Constant interruptions break that mental model, so quality suffers.

It Helps You Learn Faster

Mastering difficult skills requires focused, deliberate practice, which is only possible in deep work.

It Makes You Valuable

In a world of automation and AI, routine tasks are increasingly done by software. People who can master hard skills quickly and produce high-quality work are increasingly valuable.

It Is More Satisfying

Psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi found that people are often happiest in a state of "flow", deep absorption in a challenging task. Deep work creates the conditions for flow.

The Cost of Constant Distraction

  • Attention residue: research by Sophie Leroy shows that when you switch tasks, part of your attention stays on the previous task, reducing performance on the next one.
  • Recovery time: studies of office workers, including those by Gloria Mark at the University of California, Irvine, found it can take more than 20 minutes to return fully to a task after an interruption.
  • Stress: constant notifications keep the brain in a reactive state, increasing stress and fatigue.

Strategies to Do More Deep Work

1. Choose a Deep Work Style

Newport describes several approaches:

  • Monastic: eliminate most shallow obligations entirely. Suits some writers and researchers.
  • Bimodal: dedicate whole days or weeks to deep work, and the rest to other duties.
  • Rhythmic: do deep work at the same time every day, such as 8 to 10 am. Easiest for most people.
  • Journalistic: fit deep work in whenever you get a gap. Requires experience.

2. Schedule Focus Blocks

Put 60 to 120-minute deep work blocks on your calendar and treat them like important meetings. Many people do their best focused work in the morning, before messages pile up.

3. Remove Distractions

  • Turn off notifications and close email and chat apps.
  • Put your phone in another room or use focus modes.
  • Use website blockers if needed.
  • Tell colleagues when you're unavailable, or use a status message.
  • Use noise-cancelling headphones in open offices.

4. Define a Clear Goal

Before each session, decide exactly what you want to achieve, such as "draft the introduction and first two sections". Clear goals reduce procrastination.

5. Batch Shallow Work

Group emails, messages and admin into set times, such as late morning and late afternoon. This limits constant switching.

6. Reduce Unnecessary Meetings

Ask whether each meeting needs to happen, whether you need to attend and whether it could be shorter or replaced by a written update.

7. Train Your Focus Gradually

Concentration is like a muscle. If you're used to constant distraction, start with 25 to 45-minute sessions (the Pomodoro technique can help) and increase over time. Newport also suggests practising being bored instead of reaching for your phone at every pause.

8. Build a Shutdown Ritual

At the end of the workday, review tasks, plan tomorrow and say a phrase like "shutdown complete". This helps your mind rest so you have energy for focus the next day.

9. Design Your Environment

Your surroundings strongly influence your focus.

  • A dedicated place: work in a specific spot reserved for deep work, such as a desk, library corner or quiet room, so your brain associates it with concentration.
  • Tidy desk: keep only what you need for the task in front of you.
  • Single screen, single task: close unrelated tabs and apps; full-screen mode helps.
  • Ambient sound: some people focus better with silence, others with instrumental music, white noise or background café sounds.
  • Grand gestures: Newport describes how authors sometimes book a hotel room or travel somewhere new to finish important work. A change of environment can signal that this work matters.

10. Track Your Deep Work Hours

What gets measured gets improved. Keep a simple tally of deep work hours each day, on paper or in an app. Seeing the numbers helps you notice which days and habits support focus, and motivates you to protect your time. Many people are surprised how little genuine deep work they do at first, often less than an hour a day.

11. Protect Your Energy

Deep work requires mental energy. Sleep, exercise, regular meals and breaks all support concentration. Schedule your most demanding work for your peak energy time, which for many people is the morning, and leave lower-energy periods for shallow tasks.

12. Embrace Boredom

If you reach for your phone every time you wait in a queue or feel slightly bored, your brain becomes trained to expect constant stimulation, which makes sustained focus harder. Practise tolerating small moments of boredom: wait without your phone, walk without headphones, or simply think. This builds your capacity to stay with difficult work.

The Science of Attention

Attention is a limited resource. The brain's prefrontal cortex, which handles planning, reasoning and self-control, can only fully engage with one demanding task at a time. When we try to multitask, we're actually switching rapidly between tasks, and each switch carries a cost in time and accuracy. Research by the American Psychological Association and others has estimated that task-switching can cost a significant share of productive time.

Studies have also found that heavy media multitaskers, people who regularly juggle many streams of information, can perform worse on tests of attention and filtering out irrelevant information. Meanwhile, research on "flow" shows that people often need around 15 to 20 minutes of uninterrupted focus before they reach their most productive, absorbed state. Every interruption resets that clock.

The practical lesson is simple: long, uninterrupted blocks aren't a luxury; they're how the brain does its best work.

Rest Is Part of Deep Work

Deep work doesn't mean working longer hours. In fact, rest makes deep focus possible.

  • Breaks restore attention: short walks, especially in nature, have been shown to help restore the ability to concentrate.
  • Downtime fuels insight: many creative breakthroughs come during showers, walks or before sleep, when the mind wanders freely after a period of focused effort.
  • Sleep consolidates learning: the brain processes and stores what you've worked on during sleep.
  • Evenings off matter: Newport argues that a clean shutdown at the end of the workday, without checking email at night, recharges the attention you'll need tomorrow.

How to Calculate the Depth of Your Tasks

Newport offers a useful question for judging how deep a task is: "How long would it take, in months, to train a smart recent college graduate with no specialised training in my field to complete this task?"

  • If the answer is a few weeks or less, such as scheduling meetings or formatting a report, the task is shallow.
  • If it would take many months or years, such as designing a system architecture, writing a legal strategy or analysing complex data, the task is deep.

Rating your weekly tasks this way often reveals how much of your time goes to work that adds little unique value, and helps you decide what to delegate, automate or reduce.

Deep Work and Career Growth

Newport argues that two core abilities are increasingly valuable in the modern economy: the ability to quickly master hard things, and the ability to produce at an elite level in both quality and speed. Both depend on deep work. People who can concentrate deeply learn new tools faster, produce better work and become known as the person who can solve difficult problems. Over a career, that reputation compounds into promotions, opportunities and independence.

Digital Minimalism: Reducing the Pull of Your Phone

In his book Digital Minimalism, Newport suggests a "digital declutter": for 30 days, step away from optional technologies like social media, news apps and streaming, then reintroduce only those that genuinely support your values. Other practical steps include:

  • Removing social media apps from your phone and using them only on a computer.
  • Turning your phone to greyscale to make it less appealing.
  • Using app time limits.
  • Keeping your phone out of the bedroom.
  • Replacing scrolling with high-quality leisure, such as reading, hobbies, sport or time with friends.

Signs You're Stuck in Shallow Work

  • You end most days exhausted but can't name a meaningful accomplishment.
  • You check email or chat within minutes of waking up and dozens of times a day.
  • Your calendar is full of meetings with no time blocked for focused work.
  • Important projects keep getting pushed to "next week".
  • You struggle to read a book or long article without checking your phone.
  • You do your most important thinking late at night or at weekends, because that's the only quiet time.

If several of these sound familiar, your workday is likely dominated by shallow work.

Famous Examples of Deep Work

  • Carl Jung built a stone tower in Bollingen, Switzerland, as a retreat for uninterrupted thinking and writing.
  • J.K. Rowling checked into a hotel suite to finish the final Harry Potter book away from household distractions.
  • Bill Gates famously took "Think Weeks", spending several days alone reading and thinking about the future of technology.
  • Charles Darwin followed a strict daily routine with long, uninterrupted morning work sessions and thinking walks along his "Sandwalk" path.
  • Maya Angelou rented a small hotel room to write, removing pictures from the walls to avoid distraction.

The common thread: each created conditions where deep concentration was possible, then protected them fiercely.

A 30-Day Deep Work Challenge

  • Week 1: track how you spend your time and count genuine deep work minutes each day. Turn off non-essential notifications.
  • Week 2: schedule one 60-minute deep work block every weekday, at the same time. Batch email into two or three set times.
  • Week 3: extend to 90 minutes, add a shutdown ritual and practise tolerating boredom during breaks.
  • Week 4: add a second deep work block on at least three days, review your progress and plan how to protect the habit long term.

A Sample Deep Work Day

  • 7:30 am: wake, exercise, breakfast; no email yet.
  • 9:00 to 11:00 am: deep work block 1 on the most important project. Phone in another room, notifications off.
  • 11:00 to 11:15 am: short break and a walk.
  • 11:15 am to 12:30 pm: shallow work: email, messages and quick admin.
  • 12:30 to 1:30 pm: lunch away from the desk.
  • 1:30 to 3:00 pm: meetings and collaboration.
  • 3:00 to 4:30 pm: deep work block 2, for writing, analysis or learning.
  • 4:30 to 5:30 pm: shallow work, then a shutdown ritual: review tasks, plan tomorrow's deep work and close the laptop.

This schedule creates about three and a half hours of deep work while still handling communication and meetings.

How Managers and Teams Can Support Deep Work

Individuals can only do so much if their workplace expects instant replies. Leaders can make deep work possible for everyone:

  • Set communication norms: agree which matters need an immediate reply and which can wait a few hours.
  • Introduce focus time: some companies block meeting-free mornings or "no-meeting Wednesdays".
  • Prefer written updates: replace some status meetings with short written summaries.
  • Reduce chat overload: limit channels and encourage people to batch messages.
  • Measure outcomes, not activity: judge people on results rather than on how quickly they respond.
  • Model the behaviour: when leaders protect their own focus time, teams feel permission to do the same.

Deep Work for Students

Students face many of the same distractions as professionals. Deep work techniques help with exam preparation, essays and projects:

  • Study in 45 to 90-minute focused blocks with short breaks.
  • Keep phones out of the study room.
  • Use active techniques such as practice questions and self-testing rather than rereading notes.
  • Plan your week so the hardest subjects get your best focus hours.
  • Join or form quiet study groups where everyone works silently together.

Deep Work in the Age of AI

AI tools can now handle many shallow tasks, such as drafting routine emails, summarising documents, scheduling meetings and transcribing notes. Used well, they can free more time for deep work. But AI can also become a new source of distraction, or tempt people to skip the hard thinking that builds real expertise. The most valuable professionals will use AI to reduce shallow work while protecting time for the deep thinking, judgement and creativity that machines can't fully replace.

Is Shallow Work Bad?

No. Shallow work is part of most jobs: replying to customers, coordinating with teammates and handling admin keep organisations running. The goal isn't to eliminate it but to keep it from crowding out the work that creates the most value. Many people find that a ratio of more deep work and less shallow work leads to better results and less stress.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many hours of deep work can a person do per day?

Newport suggests that around four hours is a realistic upper limit for most people, with beginners often managing about an hour.

Is multitasking compatible with deep work?

No. Deep work requires single-tasking. Multitasking usually means rapid switching, which reduces quality and speed.

How do I do deep work in an open office?

Use headphones, book a quiet room, work from home on focus days if possible, and agree on "do not disturb" signals with colleagues.

Can meetings ever be deep work?

Some can, such as intense problem-solving or design sessions with a few people. Most routine status meetings are shallow.

Can I do deep work at home with children or family around?

It's harder but possible. Try working early in the morning or after children sleep, agree on "focus hours" with your family, use a door sign or headphones, and accept shorter but regular sessions rather than waiting for perfect conditions.

Are open-plan offices bad for deep work?

Often, yes. A 2018 Harvard study by Ethan Bernstein and Stephen Turban found that moving to open-plan offices reduced face-to-face interaction and increased electronic messaging, while noise and visibility make sustained concentration harder. Quiet rooms, focus days at home and headphones can help.

What if my job is mostly shallow work?

Some roles, such as customer support or coordination, involve a lot of shallow work by design. Even then, protecting a small amount of time each week for deeper learning, process improvement or skill-building can significantly improve your performance and career prospects.

Does listening to music help deep work?

It depends on the person and the task. Instrumental music or ambient noise helps some people focus, while songs with lyrics can interfere with reading and writing. Experiment and notice what works for you.

Is deep work only for knowledge workers?

No. Anyone who does complex, skilled work, such as students, craftspeople, doctors, musicians or athletes, benefits from distraction-free, focused practice.

How do I know if I'm doing deep work?

Ask whether the task is cognitively demanding, creates real value, would be hard for someone else to replicate quickly and has your full, uninterrupted attention. If all four are true, it's deep work.

Final Thoughts

In a world full of pings and pop-ups, the ability to focus deeply is becoming rare, and that is exactly what makes it so valuable. Protect a few hours each week for uninterrupted work on what matters most, batch the small stuff and train your attention gradually. You'll produce better work, learn faster and end your days with a real sense of accomplishment.
