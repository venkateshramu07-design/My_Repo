Quick answer: Deep work is focused, distraction-free work on cognitively demanding tasks that create real value, such as writing, coding, designing, analysing or learning hard skills. Shallow work is logistical, easy-to-repeat work often done while distracted, such as email, routine meetings and admin. The idea was popularised by Cal Newport in his 2016 book Deep Work. To do more deep work, schedule protected focus blocks, remove distractions, batch shallow tasks and build the habit gradually.

Most knowledge workers spend their days answering messages, attending meetings and switching between tabs. At the end of the day they feel busy but not productive. The problem isn't laziness; it's that the work that really matters often requires long, uninterrupted concentration, which modern workplaces rarely allow.

This guide explains the difference between deep and shallow work, why deep work is so valuable, and practical strategies to do more of it.

What Is Deep Work?

Cal Newport, a computer science professor at Georgetown University, defines deep work as "professional activities performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that push your cognitive capabilities to their limit." These efforts create new value, improve your skills and are hard to replicate.

Examples include:

  • Writing a report, article or book chapter.
  • Programming a complex feature.
  • Designing a product or strategy.
  • Analysing data to solve a difficult problem.
  • Studying a challenging subject.

What Is Shallow Work?

Newport defines shallow work as "non-cognitively demanding, logistical-style tasks, often performed while distracted." These tasks are often necessary, but they don't create much new value and are easy for others to do.

Examples include:

  • Checking and replying to emails and chat messages.
  • Scheduling meetings.
  • Filling in routine forms or reports.
  • Status meetings with little decision-making.
  • Browsing the web for work-related news.

Why Deep Work Matters

It Produces Better Results

Complex problems require holding many ideas in mind at once. Constant interruptions break that mental model, so quality suffers.

It Helps You Learn Faster

Mastering difficult skills requires focused, deliberate practice, which is only possible in deep work.

It Makes You Valuable

In a world of automation and AI, routine tasks are increasingly done by software. People who can master hard skills quickly and produce high-quality work are increasingly valuable.

It Is More Satisfying

Psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi found that people are often happiest in a state of "flow", deep absorption in a challenging task. Deep work creates the conditions for flow.

The Cost of Constant Distraction

  • Attention residue: research by Sophie Leroy shows that when you switch tasks, part of your attention stays on the previous task, reducing performance on the next one.
  • Recovery time: studies of office workers, including those by Gloria Mark at the University of California, Irvine, found it can take more than 20 minutes to return fully to a task after an interruption.
  • Stress: constant notifications keep the brain in a reactive state, increasing stress and fatigue.

Strategies to Do More Deep Work

1. Choose a Deep Work Style

Newport describes several approaches:

  • Monastic: eliminate most shallow obligations entirely. Suits some writers and researchers.
  • Bimodal: dedicate whole days or weeks to deep work, and the rest to other duties.
  • Rhythmic: do deep work at the same time every day, such as 8 to 10 am. Easiest for most people.
  • Journalistic: fit deep work in whenever you get a gap. Requires experience.

2. Schedule Focus Blocks

Put 60 to 120-minute deep work blocks on your calendar and treat them like important meetings. Many people do their best focused work in the morning, before messages pile up.

3. Remove Distractions

  • Turn off notifications and close email and chat apps.
  • Put your phone in another room or use focus modes.
  • Use website blockers if needed.
  • Tell colleagues when you're unavailable, or use a status message.
  • Use noise-cancelling headphones in open offices.

4. Define a Clear Goal

Before each session, decide exactly what you want to achieve, such as "draft the introduction and first two sections". Clear goals reduce procrastination.

5. Batch Shallow Work

Group emails, messages and admin into set times, such as late morning and late afternoon. This limits constant switching.

6. Reduce Unnecessary Meetings

Ask whether each meeting needs to happen, whether you need to attend and whether it could be shorter or replaced by a written update.

7. Train Your Focus Gradually

Concentration is like a muscle. If you're used to constant distraction, start with 25 to 45-minute sessions (the Pomodoro technique can help) and increase over time. Newport also suggests practising being bored instead of reaching for your phone at every pause.

8. Build a Shutdown Ritual

At the end of the workday, review tasks, plan tomorrow and say a phrase like "shutdown complete". This helps your mind rest so you have energy for focus the next day.

Is Shallow Work Bad?

No. Shallow work is part of most jobs: replying to customers, coordinating with teammates and handling admin keep organisations running. The goal isn't to eliminate it but to keep it from crowding out the work that creates the most value. Many people find that a ratio of more deep work and less shallow work leads to better results and less stress.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many hours of deep work can a person do per day?

Newport suggests that around four hours is a realistic upper limit for most people, with beginners often managing about an hour.

Is multitasking compatible with deep work?

No. Deep work requires single-tasking. Multitasking usually means rapid switching, which reduces quality and speed.

How do I do deep work in an open office?

Use headphones, book a quiet room, work from home on focus days if possible, and agree on "do not disturb" signals with colleagues.

Can meetings ever be deep work?

Some can, such as intense problem-solving or design sessions with a few people. Most routine status meetings are shallow.

Final Thoughts

In a world full of pings and pop-ups, the ability to focus deeply is becoming rare, and that is exactly what makes it so valuable. Protect a few hours each week for uninterrupted work on what matters most, batch the small stuff and train your attention gradually. You'll produce better work, learn faster and end your days with a real sense of accomplishment.
