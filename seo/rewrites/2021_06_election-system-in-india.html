Quick answer: India is a parliamentary democracy where citizens aged 18 and above elect representatives using the first-past-the-post system: in each constituency, the candidate with the most votes wins. Voters directly elect the Lok Sabha (the lower house of Parliament) and state Legislative Assemblies, usually every five years. The party or coalition with a majority in the Lok Sabha forms the national government and chooses the Prime Minister. The whole process is run by the independent Election Commission of India.

India holds the largest elections in the world. In the 2024 general election, close to a billion people were registered to vote, and voting was spread over several weeks so that security forces and polling staff could reach every corner of the country, from Himalayan villages to remote islands. Despite the scale, results are usually counted and declared within a single day thanks to electronic voting machines.

This guide explains how the system works, from the institutions involved to what happens on polling day and how the government is formed afterwards.

The Structure of Government in India

To understand Indian elections, it helps to know what is being elected. India is a federal parliamentary republic with three main levels of elected government:

  • National (Union) level: Parliament has two houses. The Lok Sabha (House of the People) has 543 elected seats and is directly elected. The Rajya Sabha (Council of States) is mostly elected indirectly by the members of state legislatures.
  • State level: each state, and some union territories, has a Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha) elected directly by voters. Some states also have a Legislative Council.
  • Local level: panchayats in rural areas and municipalities and municipal corporations in towns and cities, elected under the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments.

The Election Commission of India

The Election Commission of India (ECI) is a constitutional body created under Article 324 of the Constitution. It is responsible for conducting elections to Parliament, state legislatures and the offices of President and Vice-President. Local body elections are run by separate State Election Commissions.

What the Election Commission Does

  • Prepares and updates the electoral rolls (voter lists).
  • Announces the election schedule, often in multiple phases.
  • Registers and recognises political parties and allots election symbols.
  • Enforces the Model Code of Conduct, a set of guidelines for parties and candidates once elections are announced.
  • Monitors campaign spending and deploys observers.
  • Manages polling stations, voting machines, counting and the declaration of results.

Who Can Vote in India?

India uses universal adult suffrage. You can vote if you are:

  • A citizen of India.
  • Aged 18 or older on the qualifying date.
  • Registered on the electoral roll of the constituency where you ordinarily live.
  • Not disqualified under law (for example, due to certain criminal convictions or being declared of unsound mind by a court).

Registered voters receive an Electors Photo Identity Card (EPIC), commonly called a voter ID card. Other approved identity documents can also be used at the polling station. You can check your registration and apply for inclusion through the ECI's official voter services portal or app.

How the Voting System Works: First Past the Post

Lok Sabha and Vidhan Sabha elections use the first-past-the-post (FPTP) system. The country or state is divided into single-member constituencies. In each one, the candidate who receives the most votes wins, even if that is less than half of all votes cast.

This system is simple and produces clear local representatives, but it also means that a party's share of seats can differ a lot from its share of votes. A party winning many close contests can gain a large majority of seats with well under half the national vote.

Reserved Constituencies

To ensure representation for historically disadvantaged groups, some constituencies are reserved for candidates from Scheduled Castes (SC) and Scheduled Tribes (ST). Everyone in that constituency can vote, but only SC or ST candidates can stand. A constitutional amendment passed in 2023 also provides for one-third of seats in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies to be reserved for women, to take effect after a future census and delimitation exercise.

The Election Process Step by Step

  1. Announcement: the Election Commission announces the schedule, and the Model Code of Conduct comes into force.
  2. Nominations: candidates file nomination papers, declare their assets, liabilities, education and any criminal cases, and pay a security deposit.
  3. Scrutiny and withdrawal: nominations are checked, and candidates have a short window to withdraw.
  4. Campaigning: parties hold rallies, door-to-door campaigns and media campaigns. Campaigning must stop 48 hours before polling in each area.
  5. Polling day: voters go to their assigned polling station, are identified, have indelible ink applied to a finger and vote on an Electronic Voting Machine (EVM).
  6. Counting: on the scheduled day, EVM votes are counted under the supervision of returning officers and party agents, and results are declared constituency by constituency.

Electronic Voting Machines and VVPAT

India has used EVMs in all constituencies for national and state elections since the early 2000s. An EVM has a ballot unit with buttons next to each candidate's name and symbol, and a control unit operated by polling staff.

Since 2019, every EVM has been paired with a Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail (VVPAT). After you press the button, a small paper slip showing your chosen candidate's name and symbol is visible through a window for about seven seconds before it drops into a sealed box. VVPAT slips from a sample of polling stations in each constituency are counted and matched with EVM results.

The ballot also includes a NOTA (None of the Above) option, introduced in 2013 after a Supreme Court ruling. NOTA votes are counted and published, but they do not change the result; the candidate with the most votes still wins.

How the Government Is Formed

After a general election, the President invites the leader of the party or coalition that commands a majority in the Lok Sabha (at least 272 of 543 seats) to form the government. That leader becomes the Prime Minister, who then selects the Council of Ministers. The government must retain the confidence of the Lok Sabha; if it loses a confidence vote, it falls.

At the state level the same principle applies: the leader of the majority in the Legislative Assembly becomes the Chief Minister, appointed by the state's Governor.

How the President and Rajya Sabha Are Elected

  • President: elected indirectly by an electoral college of elected members of both houses of Parliament and the state legislative assemblies, using a proportional, single-transferable-vote system.
  • Rajya Sabha: most members are elected by state legislators, again using proportional representation. Members serve six-year terms, with one-third retiring every two years.

A Short History of Elections in India

India's first general election was held between October 1951 and February 1952, just a few years after independence. It was one of the boldest democratic experiments in history. Around 173 million people were eligible to vote, the large majority of whom could not read or write. To make voting possible, the Election Commission gave each party a picture symbol, such as a pair of bullocks, a hand or a lamp, so that voters could recognise their choice without reading names. Separate ballot boxes for each candidate were used in many places.

Sukumar Sen, India's first Chief Election Commissioner, organised that election with very limited resources: rolls had to be compiled by hand, polling staff trained from scratch and ballot boxes carried to remote villages by road, river and on foot. The success of that first election established a tradition of regular, competitive elections that has continued ever since.

Key Milestones

  • 1951–52: first general election under universal adult suffrage.
  • 1977: after the Emergency (1975–77), voters removed the ruling Congress government for the first time, showing that power could change hands peacefully at the national level.
  • 1988: the 61st Constitutional Amendment lowered the voting age from 21 to 18.
  • 1990s: under Chief Election Commissioner T. N. Seshan, the Election Commission began enforcing the Model Code of Conduct much more strictly and introduced voter photo identity cards.
  • 1992–93: the 73rd and 74th Amendments gave constitutional status to panchayats and municipalities, with reserved seats for women, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • 2004: EVMs were used in every constituency in a general election for the first time.
  • 2013: NOTA was added to the ballot, and VVPAT trials began.
  • 2019: VVPAT was used with EVMs across all constituencies in a general election.
  • 2023: the Constitution (106th Amendment) Act provided for reserving one-third of Lok Sabha and state assembly seats for women, to be implemented after the next census and delimitation.

How Polling Day Works: What Voters Experience

If you're voting for the first time, knowing what to expect at the polling station removes a lot of anxiety. The process is broadly the same across India.

  1. Find your polling station: before election day, check your name on the electoral roll and your polling station using the official voter services portal, the Voter Helpline app or the voter information slip distributed by booth-level officers.
  2. Carry identification: bring your EPIC (voter ID card) or one of the alternative photo identity documents approved by the Election Commission, such as an Aadhaar card, passport, driving licence or PAN card.
  3. Queue and verification: the first polling officer checks your name on the roll and your identity.
  4. Indelible ink: the second polling officer marks your left forefinger with indelible ink, records your details in a register and asks you to sign or give a thumb impression.
  5. Voter slip: you receive a slip and hand it to the third polling officer, who activates the ballot unit.
  6. Casting your vote: in the voting compartment, press the blue button next to your chosen candidate's name and symbol. A red light glows and a beep sounds.
  7. VVPAT check: look at the VVPAT window to confirm that the printed slip shows your chosen candidate.

Mobile phones are generally not allowed inside the voting compartment, and campaigning is banned within a set distance of polling stations. Facilities such as ramps, wheelchairs, Braille on EVMs and assistance for voters with disabilities are provided, and in recent elections voters aged 85 and above and people with certain disabilities have been offered the option to vote from home in many areas.

How Votes Are Counted and Results Declared

Counting takes place on a single announced day, usually a few days after the final phase of polling. EVMs are stored under armed guard in strong rooms, with candidates' representatives allowed to monitor them.

  • On counting day, strong rooms are opened in the presence of candidates or their agents.
  • Postal ballots, cast by service voters, election staff and certain other groups, are counted first.
  • EVM control units are counted round by round, with results displayed on a public board.
  • VVPAT slips from five randomly selected polling stations per assembly segment are counted and matched against the EVM results.
  • The returning officer declares the winner and issues a certificate of election.

Because of electronic voting, trends usually become clear by mid-morning and most results are declared the same day, even for an electorate of nearly a billion people.

Political Parties in India

India has a multi-party system with hundreds of registered parties. The Election Commission recognises some as national parties and others as state parties based on their vote share and seats won.

  • National parties have a presence across several states. The Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) and the Indian National Congress are the two largest.
  • Regional parties are powerful in particular states and often play a decisive role in coalition governments. Examples include parties that have governed states such as Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Odisha and Punjab.
  • Alliances: national elections are often contested by pre-poll alliances, such as the BJP-led National Democratic Alliance (NDA) and the Congress-led opposition alliance known as INDIA in 2024.

Coalition politics means that even a national election is, in practice, many state-level contests with different local issues, leaders and alliances.

Campaign Finance and Candidate Disclosure

Money plays a large role in Indian elections, and the rules try to bring transparency to it.

  • Spending limits: the Election Commission sets a maximum amount each candidate can spend on their campaign, which varies by state and type of election. There is no cap on spending by political parties as a whole.
  • Affidavits: every candidate must file a sworn affidavit disclosing assets, liabilities, educational qualifications and pending criminal cases. These are published online, allowing voters to compare candidates.
  • Electoral bonds: introduced in 2018 as an anonymous way to donate to parties, they were struck down by the Supreme Court in February 2024 as unconstitutional because they violated voters' right to information.
  • Monitoring: expenditure observers, flying squads and surveillance teams track cash, liquor and gifts used to influence voters, and large seizures are common during election periods.

Delimitation: How Constituency Boundaries Are Drawn

Every Lok Sabha and assembly constituency is meant to represent roughly similar numbers of people within a state. The process of drawing and redrawing these boundaries is called delimitation, and it is carried out by an independent Delimitation Commission whose orders cannot be challenged in court.

The number of Lok Sabha seats allocated to each state has been frozen since 1976, based on the 1971 census, and the freeze was extended until after the first census following 2026. The idea was to avoid penalising states that successfully reduced population growth. Boundaries within states were last redrawn in the 2000s using 2001 census data, without changing the number of seats per state.

The next delimitation is one of the most important upcoming questions in Indian politics. Because population has grown faster in some northern states than in southern states, a purely population-based reallocation could shift seats from south to north. The women's reservation law is also linked to the next delimitation, so the timing affects when one-third of seats will be reserved for women.

Indian Elections in Numbers

The scale of Indian elections is hard to imagine. Figures from the 2024 general election give a sense of it:

  • Close to 970 million registered voters, the largest electorate in the world.
  • Around 640 million votes cast, with turnout of about 66%.
  • More than one million polling stations.
  • About 15 million polling and security personnel deployed.
  • Voting held in seven phases over about six weeks.
  • Thousands of candidates from hundreds of parties, plus independents.

Polling stations are set up so that, as a rule, no voter should have to travel more than two kilometres to vote. Election officials have famously trekked through forests and mountains to set up polling stations for a single voter, reflecting the principle that every vote matters.

How You Can Participate Beyond Voting

Democracy depends on more than one vote every five years. Citizens can also:

  • Help friends and family register, especially first-time voters and people who have moved.
  • Read candidate affidavits to learn about their background before voting.
  • Report violations of the Model Code of Conduct through the Election Commission's cVIGIL app.
  • Volunteer with non-partisan voter awareness groups.
  • Attend local ward or gram sabha meetings, where decisions about your area are discussed.
  • Check facts before sharing political messages on social media.

Strengths and Challenges of the System

Strengths

  • Peaceful transfers of power at national and state levels since independence.
  • Very high participation, including strong turnout among women and rural voters.
  • An independent Election Commission with wide powers during elections.
  • Fast, reliable counting with EVMs.

Challenges

  • High and often opaque campaign spending.
  • Candidates with pending criminal cases contesting and winning.
  • Misinformation spread through social media and messaging apps.
  • Debates over the transparency of political funding and over EVM verification.
  • Gaps between vote share and seat share under first-past-the-post.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often are elections held in India?

Lok Sabha and state assembly elections are normally held every five years, unless a house is dissolved early. Because state election cycles differ, some election is happening somewhere in India almost every year.

Can Indians living abroad vote?

Indian citizens living abroad who have not acquired another citizenship can register as overseas electors, but they currently have to vote in person at their polling station in India.

What is the minimum age to stand for election?

Candidates must be at least 25 for the Lok Sabha and state assemblies, and at least 30 for the Rajya Sabha and state legislative councils.

What happens if no party wins a majority?

Parties may form a coalition or receive outside support to reach a majority. The President invites the leader most likely to win a confidence vote to form the government.

What is the Model Code of Conduct?

It is a set of guidelines that apply from the announcement of elections until results. It restricts governments from announcing new schemes, bans using official resources for campaigning and sets rules on speeches, rallies and polling-day conduct.

What is "One Nation, One Election"?

It is a proposal to hold Lok Sabha and state assembly elections at the same time across the country, as was largely the case until the late 1960s. Supporters say it would reduce costs and the disruption of constant campaigning; critics raise concerns about federalism and what happens if a government falls mid-term. Legislation on the idea has been introduced in Parliament and is being examined.

How can I register as a new voter?

You can apply online through the Election Commission's voter services portal or Voter Helpline app by filling in Form 6, or apply through your booth-level officer. Young people can apply in advance so that they are added to the roll once they turn 18.

Final Thoughts

India's election system takes a simple idea, one person one vote, and applies it on an extraordinary scale. Independent institutions, electronic voting and decades of peaceful transitions have made Indian elections a reference point for democracies worldwide, even as debates about money, misinformation and fairness continue. Knowing how the system works is the first step to taking part in it fully: check your registration, learn about your candidates and cast your vote.
