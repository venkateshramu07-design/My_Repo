Updated 21 October 2026

Quick answer: The Power of Now by Eckhart Tolle, first published in 1997, argues that most human suffering comes from identifying with the constant stream of thoughts in our heads, especially thoughts about the past and the future. Its central message is that peace and clarity are found by bringing full attention to the present moment, observing your thoughts and emotions instead of being controlled by them, and accepting what is happening now rather than resisting it. Key ideas include "you are not your mind", the "pain-body", the difference between clock time and psychological time, and surrender as acceptance, not passivity. Its practical value lies in simple awareness practices that overlap with modern mindfulness; its critics say it is vague, unscientific in places and not a substitute for mental health treatment.

Few spiritual books have reached as many readers as The Power of Now. It has sold millions of copies, been translated into dozens of languages and become a reference point for people interested in mindfulness, meditation and personal growth. Yet many readers find it challenging: its language is unusual, its ideas are abstract and it asks you to question something most of us take for granted, that we are our thoughts.

This article gives a clear overview of the book: who Eckhart Tolle is, the book's central theme and key ideas, how they connect to other traditions and to modern research on mindfulness, practical exercises you can try, the main criticisms, and who is most likely to benefit from reading it.

About the Author: Eckhart Tolle

Eckhart Tolle was born Ulrich Leonard Tölle in Germany in 1948. He spent part of his youth in Spain and later moved to England, where he studied at the University of London and began postgraduate research at the University of Cambridge.

By his own account, Tolle suffered from long periods of anxiety and depression in his twenties. He describes a turning point at the age of 29: one night, overwhelmed by despair, he had the thought "I cannot live with myself any longer." He then wondered who the "I" and the "self" in that sentence were, and whether there might be two of them. He says this question led to a profound inner shift, after which he experienced a deep and lasting sense of peace.

Tolle spent the following years living simply, reflecting and talking with people who sought him out as a spiritual counsellor. He later moved to Vancouver, Canada, where The Power of Now was first published in 1997 by a small publisher, Namaste Publishing. A wider edition by New World Library followed in 1999. The book spread largely through word of mouth, and its popularity grew further after Oprah Winfrey recommended it. Tolle's later book A New Earth (2005) was chosen for Oprah's Book Club in 2008, and a series of online classes with Oprah introduced his ideas to millions more people.

Tolle doesn't belong to any single religion. His teachings draw on themes found in Buddhism, Hinduism (particularly the non-dual Advaita Vedanta tradition), Taoism, Christian mysticism and other traditions, expressed in his own language.

The Central Theme: Presence

The book's core message is simple to state and hard to practise: the present moment is the only time that truly exists, and most of our suffering comes from living somewhere else.

Think about how much of your day is spent replaying past conversations, regretting decisions, worrying about tomorrow, planning, imagining arguments or waiting for life to get better "when" something happens. Tolle argues that this constant mental time travel keeps us anxious, dissatisfied and disconnected from life as it actually is.

The past exists only as memory, and the future only as imagination, both experienced in the present. Life itself always happens now. When you bring your full attention to the present moment, Tolle says, the mind becomes quieter, problems often turn out to be less overwhelming than they seemed, and a deeper sense of peace becomes available.

This idea isn't new. Similar teachings appear in many spiritual traditions. What made The Power of Now popular was its direct, conversational style, written partly in question-and-answer form, and its focus on everyday experience rather than religious belief.

Key Ideas From The Power of Now

1. You Are Not Your Mind

Tolle's starting point is that most people are completely identified with their thinking. The voice in the head comments, judges, worries and compares almost non-stop, and we assume that voice is who we are.

He suggests a different view: you are the awareness in which thoughts appear. Just as you can notice a sound or a sensation, you can notice a thought. The moment you notice a thought as a thought, you're no longer completely lost in it. Tolle calls this "watching the thinker".

He doesn't argue that thinking is bad. The mind is a powerful tool for solving problems and planning. The problem is when the tool takes over and runs constantly, without us realising it, like a radio we can't turn off.

2. The Ego

Tolle uses the word "ego" to describe a false sense of self built from thoughts, memories, roles, possessions, opinions and comparisons with others. The ego, in his view, constantly seeks more: more recognition, more possessions, more being right. Because it's built on thought, it always feels incomplete, and it creates conflict with others.

Recognising the ego at work, for example noticing the urge to win an argument or to feel superior, is, for Tolle, the beginning of freedom from it.

3. The Pain-Body

One of the book's best-known ideas is the "pain-body": an accumulation of old emotional pain that Tolle describes as almost a living energy field within a person. It can lie dormant and then be triggered, by a comment, a situation or a memory, after which it feeds on negative thinking and drama.

If you've ever reacted to something small with surprising anger or sadness, or noticed yourself drawn into the same painful arguments again and again, you may recognise what Tolle is describing. His advice is to notice the pain-body when it arises, observe the emotion in your body without feeding it with stories, and stay present with it. Awareness, he says, gradually weakens its hold.

The pain-body is a metaphor rather than a scientific concept, but many readers find it a useful way to understand recurring emotional patterns.

4. Clock Time and Psychological Time

Tolle is not telling readers to ignore the future or forget the past. He distinguishes between two kinds of time:

  • Clock time: the practical use of time, such as making plans, setting appointments, learning from past mistakes and saving for retirement. This is necessary and healthy.
  • Psychological time: identifying with the past and future so much that you can't be at peace now. This shows up as regret, resentment, anxiety, constant anticipation and the belief that you'll be happy only when some future goal is reached.

The aim is to use clock time when needed and return to the present, rather than living permanently in a mental past or future.

5. Acceptance and Surrender

Tolle argues that much of our suffering comes not from situations themselves but from our resistance to them: the internal "no" to what is happening. Stuck in traffic, we fume. Facing a loss, we replay what should have been.

His alternative is "surrender", which he is careful to say does not mean passivity or giving up. It means accepting the reality of this moment, because it's already here. From that acceptance, you can then take clear action to change what can be changed. In his framing, you have three healthy options in any situation: remove yourself from it, change it, or accept it fully. What causes suffering is staying in a situation while resisting it.

This idea closely resembles the Serenity Prayer, associated with theologian Reinhold Niebuhr, which asks for the serenity to accept what cannot be changed, the courage to change what can, and the wisdom to know the difference.

6. The Inner Body

To anchor attention in the present, Tolle recommends feeling the "inner body": the sense of aliveness in your hands, feet, chest and whole body. Directing attention to physical sensation takes attention away from compulsive thinking. This is similar to body scan practices used in many meditation traditions and in modern mindfulness programmes.

7. Portals Into Stillness

Tolle describes several "portals" into deeper presence: the inner body, silence, space, conscious breathing and surrender. Noticing the silence between sounds, or the space in a room rather than only the objects in it, can help quieten the thinking mind.

8. Relationships as a Spiritual Practice

Tolle devotes a section of the book to relationships. He suggests that many relationships are driven by the ego's need: we look to partners to fill a sense of incompleteness, and when they don't, love turns into blame and conflict.

His advice is to bring presence into relationships: listen fully without planning your reply, notice when you react from old pain, and stop trying to make your partner responsible for your happiness. Being present with another person, he argues, is one of the greatest gifts you can give.

9. Enlightenment

Tolle uses the word "enlightenment" to describe the end of identification with thought and the realisation of a deeper sense of being. He presents this not as something reserved for monks, but as a possibility for anyone, and insists it is found now, not in the future.

How the Book Is Structured

The book is divided into ten chapters, beginning with the idea that you are not your mind and moving through consciousness as the way out of pain, moving deeply into the now, the mind's strategies for avoiding the present, the state of presence, the inner body, portals into the unmanifested, enlightened relationships, the idea that peace is beyond happiness and unhappiness, and finally the meaning of surrender.

Much of the text is written as answers to questions, as if from a teacher to a student. Some readers find this engaging; others find it repetitive. Tolle himself suggests reading slowly and pausing often, treating the book as an experience rather than just information.

Connections With Other Traditions

Readers familiar with other teachings will notice many parallels:

  • Buddhism teaches that suffering arises from craving and attachment, and emphasises mindfulness and non-attachment to thoughts.
  • Zen stresses direct experience of the present moment, often summarised as "when eating, just eat; when walking, just walk". Our introduction to Zen covers these ideas.
  • Advaita Vedanta teaches that the true self is pure awareness, distinct from thoughts and the ego.
  • Stoicism encourages focusing on what is within our control and accepting what isn't, and Marcus Aurelius wrote that each person lives only in the present moment.
  • Christian mysticism includes practices of stillness and contemplative prayer, and Tolle quotes Jesus's teachings in his own interpretation.

Tolle's contribution is not inventing these ideas, but presenting them in plain, modern language for people without a religious background.

What Science Says About Present-Moment Awareness

The Power of Now is a spiritual book, not a scientific one, and Tolle doesn't rely on research. But some of its practical ideas overlap with areas psychologists have studied closely.

  • Mind-wandering: a 2010 Harvard study by Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert used a smartphone app to sample thousands of people's thoughts and moods during the day. It found that people's minds were wandering nearly half the time, and that people were generally less happy when their minds wandered than when they were focused on what they were doing. Its title summed it up: "A wandering mind is an unhappy mind."
  • Mindfulness programmes: Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn at the University of Massachusetts Medical School in 1979, and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT), developed for people with recurrent depression, teach present-moment awareness through meditation. Research reviews have found that these programmes can help reduce stress, anxiety and depressive symptoms for many people, and MBCT is recommended in some clinical guidelines to help prevent relapse in recurrent depression.
  • Rumination: psychologists have found that repetitive dwelling on problems and negative feelings is linked to depression and anxiety. Learning to notice thoughts without getting caught up in them, sometimes called "cognitive defusion" in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), is a recognised therapeutic skill.
  • Acceptance: ACT and other therapies use acceptance of difficult feelings, rather than struggling against them, as a path to psychological flexibility, an idea close to Tolle's "surrender".

Research on mindfulness has limitations, and effects vary from person to person. But the broad idea that training attention on the present can reduce stress has considerable support.

Practical Exercises Inspired by the Book

You don't need to accept every idea in the book to benefit from its practices. Here are some simple exercises you can try.

1. Watch the Thinker

Sit quietly for a few minutes and observe your thoughts as if you were watching cars pass on a road. Don't judge or follow them; just notice "there's a thought". You may notice short gaps between thoughts. Over time, this builds the ability to step back from your mental chatter.

2. Conscious Breathing

Several times a day, take three slow breaths and pay complete attention to the sensation of breathing. This quick reset brings you back to the present and can calm the nervous system.

3. Feel the Inner Body

Close your eyes and feel the aliveness in your hands. Then your feet, arms, legs and whole body. Try doing this while waiting in a queue or listening to someone.

4. Do Ordinary Activities Fully

Choose one daily task, such as washing dishes, walking to the bus or drinking tea, and give it your full attention. Notice the temperature of the water, the sound of your footsteps or the taste of the tea. When your mind wanders, gently bring it back.

5. Notice Resistance

When you feel irritated or upset, ask: "What am I resisting right now?" Notice whether you're arguing with reality. Then decide whether to change the situation, leave it or accept it.

6. Watch the Pain-Body

When strong emotion arises, shift attention from the story ("they always do this to me") to the feeling in your body: the tight chest, the hot face. Breathe and stay with the feeling without feeding it. Often its intensity begins to fade.

7. Listen Fully

In your next conversation, practise listening with complete attention, without planning your reply or judging. Notice how it changes the conversation.

8. Take Presence Breaks

Set a few reminders on your phone during the day. When one goes off, pause for 30 seconds, look around, feel your body and notice what is here now.

Criticisms and Controversies

The book has many admirers, but also critics. It's worth knowing the main criticisms before reading.

  • Vague and abstract language: terms like "Being", "the Unmanifested" and "the pain-body" are not precisely defined, and some readers find the writing repetitive or hard to apply.
  • Lack of scientific evidence: Tolle makes claims about consciousness, the body and spirituality that aren't scientifically tested. Readers should treat these as spiritual perspectives rather than facts.
  • Not original: some critics argue the book repackages older teachings from Buddhism, Hinduism and other traditions without fully acknowledging their depth or context.
  • Risk of "spiritual bypassing": a term coined by psychologist John Welwood for using spiritual ideas to avoid dealing with real emotional problems, injustice or responsibility. A reader could misuse "acceptance" to avoid facing a harmful relationship or a mental health problem.
  • Not a replacement for treatment: people experiencing depression, anxiety, trauma or other mental health conditions may find aspects of the book helpful, but it is not a substitute for professional care. Some intensive meditation practices can occasionally bring up difficult experiences, especially for people with a history of trauma.
  • Commercialisation: some critics are uncomfortable with the large commercial success of Tolle's books and courses, seeing a contrast with the message of letting go of ego.
  • Tolle's personal story: his account of sudden awakening can't be verified, and some readers find it too dramatic to relate to.

Many readers find a balanced approach works best: take the practical insights that help, and leave aside what doesn't resonate.

Who Should Read The Power of Now?

The book may be especially helpful if you:

  • often feel caught up in worry, overthinking or regret;
  • are interested in mindfulness and meditation but want a non-religious introduction;
  • notice recurring emotional reactions you'd like to understand;
  • feel that you're always waiting for life to start "when" something changes.

It may be less suitable if you prefer practical, evidence-based self-help with step-by-step instructions, or if you're looking for help with a serious mental health condition, in which case speaking to a doctor or mental health professional is the right first step.

For readers who find the book too abstract, Tolle's shorter companion, Practicing the Power of Now (2001), collects key passages and exercises. Other accessible books on similar themes include Thich Nhat Hanh's The Miracle of Mindfulness and Jon Kabat-Zinn's Wherever You Go, There You Are.

Tips for Reading the Book

  • Read slowly. Tolle recommends pausing to reflect rather than racing through.
  • Practise as you go. Try each exercise for a few days before moving on.
  • Don't worry about understanding everything. Some passages may make sense only later, or not at all.
  • Keep a journal of insights and experiences.
  • Re-read it. Many readers say the book reads differently at different stages of life.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main message of The Power of Now?

That peace is found by living fully in the present moment, recognising that you are not your thoughts, and accepting what is happening now instead of resisting it.

Is The Power of Now a religious book?

It's spiritual rather than religious. It draws on ideas from many traditions but doesn't require any religious belief.

What is the pain-body?

Tolle's term for accumulated old emotional pain that can be triggered and fuel negative thinking and emotional reactions. He suggests observing it with awareness rather than feeding it.

Does living in the present mean not planning for the future?

No. Tolle distinguishes between practical "clock time", which is useful for planning, and "psychological time", which is getting lost in worry and longing about the future or past.

Is The Power of Now hard to read?

Many readers find it challenging at first because of its abstract language. Reading slowly and trying the practices tends to help.

Can the book help with anxiety?

Some readers find its practices, such as conscious breathing and observing thoughts, helpful for everyday stress and worry. For anxiety disorders, it's best used alongside, not instead of, professional support.

When was The Power of Now published?

It was first published in 1997 in Canada, with a wider edition published in 1999.

How is it different from A New Earth?

The Power of Now focuses on presence and the individual's inner experience. A New Earth (2005) expands on the ego and on how a shift in consciousness could affect relationships and society.

Final Thoughts

The Power of Now asks a simple but radical question: what would change if you stopped living in your head and started living in this moment? Its language is not for everyone, and some of its claims go beyond what science can confirm. But its central practices, noticing your thoughts, coming back to your breath and body, and accepting what is before acting, are simple, free and available to anyone.

You don't have to read the whole book to start. Take three conscious breaths right now, notice what's around you and feel your body. That, in Tolle's view, is where life is always happening.

If this summary helped, share it with someone who's interested in mindfulness or has been meaning to read the book.
