Quick answer: You can't erase someone from your memory, but you can stop them from dominating your thoughts. Let yourself grieve, limit contact (including on social media), remove daily reminders, lean on friends and family, rebuild your routines, reflect on what you learned, and invest in new goals and experiences. Healing usually happens gradually over months, not days. If sadness feels overwhelming or lasts a long time, talking to a counsellor can help.

Whether it's the end of a romantic relationship, a friendship that fell apart or someone who simply drifted away, losing an important person can hurt deeply. Brain imaging studies have found that social rejection activates some of the same brain regions involved in physical pain. That is why a breakup can feel like a physical ache and why "just get over it" is such unhelpful advice.

Moving on is not about pretending the person never mattered. It's about making peace with the past so that it no longer controls your present. This guide offers practical steps that help.

1. Acknowledge and Accept Your Emotions

The first step is letting yourself feel what you feel. Sadness, anger, confusion, relief, guilt and longing can all appear, sometimes in the same hour. Trying to suppress them usually makes them stronger.

  • Allow yourself to cry or be quiet for a while.
  • Name your emotions: "I feel rejected", "I feel lonely", "I miss our routine".
  • Write in a journal to release thoughts that keep circling.
  • Remind yourself that grief after a loss is normal, not a sign of weakness.

2. Create Distance

Every message, photo or update can reopen the wound. Distance gives your mind a chance to heal.

  • Go no-contact if you can: at least for a while, stop calling, texting and checking in.
  • Mute, unfollow or block on social media: you don't owe anyone an explanation for protecting your peace.
  • Put away reminders: store photos, gifts and keepsakes out of sight. You can decide what to keep later.
  • Avoid "accidental" meetings: change routines that take you to places you know they'll be.

If you share children, work or a friend group, keep contact brief, polite and focused on practical matters.

3. Stop Replaying and Idealising the Past

After a loss, the mind often replays the best moments and forgets the problems. This "rose-tinted" memory makes letting go harder.

  • Write a balanced list: what was good, what wasn't, and why it ended.
  • When you catch yourself daydreaming, gently redirect your attention to something in the present.
  • Limit "what if" thinking. You can't rewrite the past, but you can learn from it.

4. Lean on Your Support System

Isolation makes everything heavier. Spend time with people who care about you.

  • Tell a trusted friend or family member how you really feel.
  • Say yes to invitations even if you don't feel like it at first.
  • Join a support group, online or in person, if you'd like to talk to others in a similar situation.

5. Take Care of Your Body

Emotional pain drains energy. Basic self-care keeps you steady.

  • Sleep: keep a regular bedtime and limit late-night scrolling.
  • Movement: walking, running, yoga or the gym release mood-lifting chemicals and reduce stress.
  • Food: regular, nourishing meals help stabilise mood.
  • Limit alcohol: it may numb pain briefly but worsens low mood and sleep.

6. Rebuild Your Routine and Identity

When someone leaves, they often take a piece of your routine with them: the morning messages, the weekend plans, the shared shows. Fill those gaps intentionally.

  • Create new rituals, such as a Sunday walk, a cooking night or a weekly call with a friend.
  • Return to hobbies you neglected or try something completely new.
  • Rediscover who you are outside the relationship: your values, interests and goals.

7. Focus on Personal Growth

Pain can become a turning point. Use this time to invest in yourself.

  • Set a goal for your health, career, learning or finances.
  • Take a course, read, travel or volunteer.
  • Celebrate small wins, like a good day, a new skill or an evening without checking their profile.

8. Reflect and Learn

Once the first wave of pain has eased, reflection can help you move forward wiser rather than bitter.

  • What did this relationship teach you about what you need and what you won't accept?
  • Were there patterns you want to change in future relationships?
  • What are you grateful for, even in the ending?

9. Practise Forgiveness, For Your Sake

Forgiveness doesn't mean excusing hurtful behaviour or reconnecting. It means releasing the anger that keeps you tied to the other person. Forgive yourself too, for mistakes you made or signs you missed. Holding onto resentment keeps the past alive.

10. Be Patient With Yourself

There is no fixed timeline for healing. Some days you'll feel strong, and others will bring a wave of sadness, often triggered by a song, a place or an anniversary. That's normal. Healing isn't a straight line, but over time the bad days become less frequent and less intense.

Common Mistakes That Slow Healing

  • Checking their social media "just once".
  • Staying friends too soon, while feelings are still strong.
  • Jumping into a rebound relationship to avoid the pain.
  • Blaming yourself completely, or blaming them completely.
  • Isolating yourself from friends and family.

When to Seek Professional Help

Consider speaking to a counsellor or therapist if, after several weeks, you're struggling to function at work or home, can't sleep or eat properly, feel hopeless, or keep returning to an unhealthy relationship. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, contact a local emergency number or crisis helpline immediately.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to forget someone?

It varies widely. Many people notice real improvement within a few months, but deep relationships can take longer. You won't forget them entirely, but the memories will lose their emotional sting.

Is it possible to stay friends with an ex?

Sometimes, but usually only after both people have fully moved on. Trying to be friends too early often prolongs the pain.

Why do I keep thinking about someone who hurt me?

Strong emotional experiences, both good and bad, are stored vividly in memory. Unresolved questions also keep the mind returning. Journaling, distance and time help these loops fade.

Should I get rid of everything that reminds me of them?

You don't have to throw everything away. Putting items out of sight for a few months is often enough. Later, you can decide calmly what to keep.

Final Thoughts

Forgetting someone isn't about deleting the past. It's about letting it settle into its proper place, as part of your story but not the whole of it. Feel your emotions, create distance, lean on the people who love you and invest in the life you want to build next. One day you'll realise you went a whole day without thinking about them, and that will be the moment you know you've moved on.
