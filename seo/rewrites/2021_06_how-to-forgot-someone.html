Quick answer: You can't erase someone from your memory, but you can stop them from dominating your thoughts. Let yourself grieve, limit contact (including on social media), remove daily reminders, lean on friends and family, rebuild your routines, reflect on what you learned, and invest in new goals and experiences. Healing usually happens gradually over months, not days. If sadness feels overwhelming or lasts a long time, talking to a counsellor can help.

Whether it's the end of a romantic relationship, a friendship that fell apart or someone who simply drifted away, losing an important person can hurt deeply. Brain imaging studies have found that social rejection activates some of the same brain regions involved in physical pain. That is why a breakup can feel like a physical ache and why "just get over it" is such unhelpful advice.

Moving on is not about pretending the person never mattered. It's about making peace with the past so that it no longer controls your present. This guide offers practical steps that help.

1. Acknowledge and Accept Your Emotions

The first step is letting yourself feel what you feel. Sadness, anger, confusion, relief, guilt and longing can all appear, sometimes in the same hour. Trying to suppress them usually makes them stronger.

  • Allow yourself to cry or be quiet for a while.
  • Name your emotions: "I feel rejected", "I feel lonely", "I miss our routine".
  • Write in a journal to release thoughts that keep circling.
  • Remind yourself that grief after a loss is normal, not a sign of weakness.

2. Create Distance

Every message, photo or update can reopen the wound. Distance gives your mind a chance to heal.

  • Go no-contact if you can: at least for a while, stop calling, texting and checking in.
  • Mute, unfollow or block on social media: you don't owe anyone an explanation for protecting your peace.
  • Put away reminders: store photos, gifts and keepsakes out of sight. You can decide what to keep later.
  • Avoid "accidental" meetings: change routines that take you to places you know they'll be.

If you share children, work or a friend group, keep contact brief, polite and focused on practical matters.

3. Stop Replaying and Idealising the Past

After a loss, the mind often replays the best moments and forgets the problems. This "rose-tinted" memory makes letting go harder.

  • Write a balanced list: what was good, what wasn't, and why it ended.
  • When you catch yourself daydreaming, gently redirect your attention to something in the present.
  • Limit "what if" thinking. You can't rewrite the past, but you can learn from it.

4. Lean on Your Support System

Isolation makes everything heavier. Spend time with people who care about you.

  • Tell a trusted friend or family member how you really feel.
  • Say yes to invitations even if you don't feel like it at first.
  • Join a support group, online or in person, if you'd like to talk to others in a similar situation.

5. Take Care of Your Body

Emotional pain drains energy. Basic self-care keeps you steady.

  • Sleep: keep a regular bedtime and limit late-night scrolling.
  • Movement: walking, running, yoga or the gym release mood-lifting chemicals and reduce stress.
  • Food: regular, nourishing meals help stabilise mood.
  • Limit alcohol: it may numb pain briefly but worsens low mood and sleep.

6. Rebuild Your Routine and Identity

When someone leaves, they often take a piece of your routine with them: the morning messages, the weekend plans, the shared shows. Fill those gaps intentionally.

  • Create new rituals, such as a Sunday walk, a cooking night or a weekly call with a friend.
  • Return to hobbies you neglected or try something completely new.
  • Rediscover who you are outside the relationship: your values, interests and goals.

7. Focus on Personal Growth

Pain can become a turning point. Use this time to invest in yourself.

  • Set a goal for your health, career, learning or finances.
  • Take a course, read, travel or volunteer.
  • Celebrate small wins, like a good day, a new skill or an evening without checking their profile.

8. Reflect and Learn

Once the first wave of pain has eased, reflection can help you move forward wiser rather than bitter.

  • What did this relationship teach you about what you need and what you won't accept?
  • Were there patterns you want to change in future relationships?
  • What are you grateful for, even in the ending?

9. Practise Forgiveness, For Your Sake

Forgiveness doesn't mean excusing hurtful behaviour or reconnecting. It means releasing the anger that keeps you tied to the other person. Forgive yourself too, for mistakes you made or signs you missed. Holding onto resentment keeps the past alive.

10. Be Patient With Yourself

There is no fixed timeline for healing. Some days you'll feel strong, and others will bring a wave of sadness, often triggered by a song, a place or an anniversary. That's normal. Healing isn't a straight line, but over time the bad days become less frequent and less intense.

Why Letting Go Is So Hard: The Science of Heartbreak

Understanding what's happening in your brain can make the pain feel less mysterious and less personal.

  • Love activates the reward system. Research by anthropologist Helen Fisher and colleagues used brain scans of people who had recently been rejected. Looking at photos of their former partner activated brain regions associated with reward, motivation and craving, the same systems involved in addiction. That's why you may feel an almost physical urge to text, call or check their profile.
  • Rejection hurts like pain. Studies by Ethan Kross and others found overlap between brain activity during intense social rejection and physical pain.
  • Stress hormones rise. Breakups can raise cortisol, disrupting sleep, appetite and concentration.
  • Your identity is shaken. Long relationships weave two lives together, through shared friends, routines, plans and even the way you describe yourself. Losing the relationship means rebuilding parts of your identity.
  • Uncertainty keeps the mind busy. If you don't fully understand why things ended, your brain keeps searching for answers, replaying conversations in an attempt to make sense of them.

The encouraging news is that these effects fade with time and distance. Studies of people after breakups generally find that distress decreases significantly over the following months, and many people report personal growth afterwards.

The Stages of Moving On

Healing rarely follows a neat path, but many people recognise these broad stages:

  1. Shock and denial: it doesn't feel real, and you may hope for reconciliation.
  2. Pain and longing: intense sadness, crying, missing them and urges to make contact.
  3. Anger and bargaining: frustration at them, at yourself or at the situation, and "if only" thinking.
  4. Sadness and reflection: a quieter grief as reality sinks in, often with deeper understanding.
  5. Acceptance: the loss is part of your story, and you begin looking forward.
  6. Growth: new routines, relationships and goals take root, and you feel like yourself again, sometimes a stronger version.

You may move back and forth between stages, especially around anniversaries, birthdays or unexpected encounters. That's normal.

Practical Tools to Stop Thinking About Someone

The Five-Minute Rule

When you feel the urge to contact them or check their social media, set a timer for five minutes and do something else: go for a short walk, drink a glass of water or text a friend instead. Urges peak and then fade, and each time you ride one out, the next becomes easier.

Write an Unsent Letter

Write everything you wish you could say: your hurt, anger, gratitude and goodbyes. Don't send it. Many people find this helps them release emotions and feel a sense of closure without needing the other person's response.

The "Reality List"

Keep a note on your phone listing the reasons the relationship didn't work: the arguments, incompatibilities and times you felt unhappy. Read it whenever you catch yourself romanticising the past.

Thought Stopping and Redirection

When an intrusive memory appears, notice it without judgement ("there's that memory again"), then deliberately redirect your attention to something absorbing, like a podcast, puzzle, exercise or conversation.

Schedule Grief Time

Instead of trying to avoid thinking about them all day, give yourself 15 to 20 minutes in the evening to think, journal or cry. If thoughts appear at other times, remind yourself you'll come back to them later. This can make thoughts less intrusive during the day.

Change Your Environment

Rearrange furniture, redecorate a room, try a new café or change your walking route. Small changes reduce constant reminders and signal a fresh chapter.

Moving On From Different Kinds of Loss

After a Breakup You Didn't Want

Being left can damage your self-esteem. Remind yourself that the end of a relationship reflects compatibility, timing and two people's choices, not your worth. Focus on rebuilding confidence through friendships, achievements and self-care.

After Ending It Yourself

Ending a relationship can bring guilt and doubt, even when it was the right decision. Remember why you made the choice, allow yourself to grieve what you hoped the relationship would be, and avoid going back simply to escape loneliness.

After a Friendship Ends

Friendship breakups are often underestimated but can hurt just as much. Give yourself permission to grieve and invest in other friendships and new communities.

After Someone Has Died

When the person has died, the goal isn't to forget them but to find a way to carry their memory while continuing to live. Grief counsellors talk about "continuing bonds": keeping a connection through rituals, memories and honouring their values. Grief after a death often takes longer and deserves extra support.

After an Unrequited Love

Loving someone who doesn't feel the same can be confusing, because there may be no clear ending. Limit contact, avoid holding onto hope based on small signs, and redirect your attention toward people who reciprocate your care.

What Heartbreak Can Teach You

In time, many people look back on a painful ending as a turning point. Psychologists call this post-traumatic growth: positive change that emerges from struggling with difficult life events. After a breakup, people often report:

  • Greater clarity about what they need in a partner.
  • Stronger friendships and family bonds, built during the hard months.
  • More confidence in their ability to cope with loss.
  • New interests, skills or goals discovered while rebuilding.
  • Healthier boundaries in future relationships.

You don't have to feel grateful for the pain, but you can let it shape a wiser, more self-aware version of yourself.

When Is It Okay to Date Again?

There's no fixed timeline. Signs you may be ready include:

  • You can think about your ex without intense pain or anger.
  • You're not hoping to make them jealous or prove something.
  • You feel curious about meeting new people rather than desperate to fill a gap.
  • You've reflected on what you want and don't want in a partner.

If you're dating mainly to avoid feeling sad, it may be worth waiting a little longer.

A 30-Day Plan to Start Moving On

Structure helps when your emotions feel chaotic. Use this plan as a guide, adapting it to your situation.

Week 1: Stabilise

  • Tell two or three trusted people what happened and ask for support.
  • Mute, unfollow or block on social media, and archive message threads.
  • Focus on basics: sleep, regular meals, water and a short walk every day.
  • Let yourself cry and write about how you feel each evening.

Week 2: Create Distance and Routine

  • Box up reminders and put them out of sight.
  • Plan something for every evening and weekend: a call with a friend, a class, a film or exercise.
  • Write your "reality list" about why the relationship didn't work.
  • Start one small daily habit, such as a morning walk or ten minutes of reading.

Week 3: Reconnect With Yourself

  • Return to a hobby you dropped or sign up for something new.
  • Spend time with friends you may have neglected during the relationship.
  • Set one personal goal for the next three months, such as a fitness target, course or savings goal.
  • Reflect on what you learned about your needs and boundaries.

Week 4: Look Forward

  • Plan something to look forward to, such as a trip, event or project.
  • Write a letter to your future self describing the life you want to build.
  • Notice how far you've come since week one.
  • If you're still struggling to function, book an appointment with a counsellor.

Rebuilding Your Self-Esteem

A painful ending can leave you questioning your attractiveness, judgement or value. Rebuilding self-esteem is an important part of moving on.

  • Separate the event from your identity. "This relationship ended" is a fact; "I am unlovable" is a painful interpretation, not the truth.
  • List your strengths. Write down qualities friends appreciate in you and achievements you're proud of. Ask a close friend to add to the list.
  • Keep promises to yourself. Small commitments, like a daily walk or finishing a book, rebuild trust in yourself.
  • Invest in your appearance and health for you, not to impress anyone. A haircut, new clothes or a fitness routine can lift your mood.
  • Limit comparisons. Social media shows others' highlights, not their struggles.
  • Speak to yourself like a friend. Replace "I'm such a fool" with "I trusted someone, and that shows my capacity to love."

What to Do on the Hardest Days

Some days will be harder than others: the anniversary, their birthday, holidays you used to spend together, or the day you hear news about them. Plan for these days in advance.

  • Schedule company, so you're not alone for long stretches.
  • Plan a comforting activity, such as a favourite film, a long walk in nature or cooking a special meal.
  • Stay off social media for the day.
  • Allow yourself to feel sad without judging yourself for "going backwards".
  • Remind yourself that hard days are temporary and tomorrow will likely feel lighter.

Daily Self-Care Checklist During Heartbreak

  • Did I eat at least two proper meals today?
  • Did I move my body for at least 20 minutes?
  • Did I talk to at least one person who cares about me?
  • Did I avoid checking their profile or messages?
  • Did I do one thing just for me?
  • Did I get to bed at a reasonable time?

Ticking even a few of these boxes each day helps keep you steady while your emotions heal.

How to Support a Friend Who Is Trying to Move On

  • Listen more than you advise. They may need to tell the story many times.
  • Avoid harsh judgements of the ex; your friend may still have mixed feelings, and they might reconcile.
  • Offer practical help: bring food, help them move belongings or keep them company on difficult evenings.
  • Invite them out regularly, even if they often say no.
  • Watch for warning signs such as heavy drinking, isolation or hopelessness, and encourage professional support if needed.

Common Mistakes That Slow Healing

  • Checking their social media "just once".
  • Staying friends too soon, while feelings are still strong.
  • Jumping into a rebound relationship to avoid the pain.
  • Blaming yourself completely, or blaming them completely.
  • Isolating yourself from friends and family.

When to Seek Professional Help

Consider speaking to a counsellor or therapist if, after several weeks, you're struggling to function at work or home, can't sleep or eat properly, feel hopeless, or keep returning to an unhealthy relationship. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, contact a local emergency number or crisis helpline immediately.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to forget someone?

It varies widely. Many people notice real improvement within a few months, but deep relationships can take longer. You won't forget them entirely, but the memories will lose their emotional sting.

Is it possible to stay friends with an ex?

Sometimes, but usually only after both people have fully moved on. Trying to be friends too early often prolongs the pain.

Why do I keep thinking about someone who hurt me?

Strong emotional experiences, both good and bad, are stored vividly in memory. Unresolved questions also keep the mind returning. Journaling, distance and time help these loops fade.

Should I get rid of everything that reminds me of them?

You don't have to throw everything away. Putting items out of sight for a few months is often enough. Later, you can decide calmly what to keep.

Should I get back together with my ex?

Some couples do reunite successfully, but only when the original problems are clearly understood and both people have genuinely changed. If you're considering it mainly because you feel lonely or afraid of being single, give yourself more time before deciding.

How do I stop comparing new people to my ex?

Comparison is natural at first. Try to notice each new person as an individual, focus on how you feel in their company, and remember that the comparison is often with an idealised memory, not the real relationship.

Is it normal to dream about my ex?

Yes. Dreams often process strong emotions and memories. Dreaming about an ex doesn't necessarily mean you want them back; it may simply mean your mind is still working through the change.

Why do I miss someone who treated me badly?

The brain becomes attached to familiar people, especially when affection was unpredictable. Intermittent warmth can create a strong emotional bond, sometimes called a trauma bond. Missing someone doesn't mean they were good for you. Distance and support from others help this bond weaken.

Is no contact really necessary?

It's not required in every case, but it speeds healing for most people because it stops the cycle of hope and disappointment. If you must stay in contact for children or work, keep conversations brief, practical and polite.

What if I see them with someone new?

It's normal to feel a sharp pang of hurt. Allow the feeling, remind yourself that their new relationship doesn't define your worth, and lean on a friend. Unfollowing them helps avoid repeated reminders.

Can therapy help me get over someone?

Yes. A therapist can help you process grief, understand relationship patterns, rebuild self-esteem and develop coping strategies, especially if the relationship was long, traumatic or the loss has triggered older pain.

Final Thoughts

Forgetting someone isn't about deleting the past. It's about letting it settle into its proper place, as part of your story but not the whole of it. Feel your emotions, create distance, lean on the people who love you and invest in the life you want to build next. One day you'll realise you went a whole day without thinking about them, and that will be the moment you know you've moved on.
