Quick answer: An annuity is a contract, usually with an insurance company, in which you pay a lump sum or a series of payments and in return receive regular income, often for the rest of your life. Annuities can be immediate or deferred, and fixed, variable or indexed. Their main benefit is protection against outliving your savings. Their main drawbacks are that your money is usually locked in, returns can be modest, fees and surrender charges can be high, and fixed payments lose value to inflation. They can suit retirees who want guaranteed income, ideally for part of their savings rather than all of it.

One of the biggest fears in retirement is running out of money. People are living longer, traditional pensions are less common, and nobody knows exactly how long their savings need to last. Annuities are designed to solve this problem by turning savings into a guaranteed income stream.

But annuities are also among the most misunderstood and sometimes mis-sold financial products. This guide explains how annuities work, the main types, their pros and cons, costs, taxation in India and the US, and how to decide whether one makes sense for you.

What Is an Annuity?

An annuity is a financial contract between you (the annuitant) and an insurance company (the annuity provider). You give the insurer money, either as a single lump sum or through regular payments over time. In exchange, the insurer promises to pay you income at regular intervals, monthly, quarterly, half-yearly or yearly, either for a fixed period or for the rest of your life.

In effect, an annuity is the opposite of life insurance. Life insurance protects your family if you die early; a lifetime annuity protects you if you live a long time.

How Annuities Work: The Two Phases

  1. Accumulation phase: you pay into the annuity, and the money may grow over time. This phase applies only to deferred annuities.
  2. Payout (annuitisation) phase: the insurer starts making regular payments to you. With an immediate annuity, this begins straight away.

The insurer pools money from many annuitants. Some will live shorter lives than expected and some longer. This pooling of longevity risk, sometimes called "mortality credits", is what allows insurers to guarantee lifetime income, something you can't easily do on your own.

Types of Annuities

By When Payments Start

  • Immediate annuity: you pay a lump sum and income starts almost immediately, usually within a month or a year. Common for people at or near retirement.
  • Deferred annuity: you invest now and payments begin at a future date, such as age 60 or 65. The money may grow during the deferral period.

By How Returns Are Determined

  • Fixed annuity: the insurer guarantees a fixed interest rate or payout amount. Predictable and low risk, but returns are modest.
  • Variable annuity: your money is invested in sub-accounts similar to mutual funds. Payments depend on investment performance, so they can rise or fall. Common in the US, often with high fees.
  • Indexed (fixed indexed) annuity: returns are linked to a market index such as the S&P 500, with a minimum guarantee and a cap on maximum gains. Popular in the US but often complex.

By How Long Payments Last

  • Life annuity: payments continue for as long as you live. This gives the strongest protection against outliving your money.
  • Joint life annuity: payments continue as long as either you or your spouse is alive, often at the same or a reduced rate after the first death.
  • Period certain annuity: payments are guaranteed for a fixed number of years, such as 10 or 20, even if you die earlier (in which case your nominee receives the remaining payments).
  • Life with return of purchase price: payments last for life, and when you die, the original purchase amount is returned to your nominee. Payments are lower because of this guarantee.
  • Increasing annuity: payments rise by a fixed percentage each year, such as 3%, to partly offset inflation. Starting payments are lower.

Advantages of Annuities

  • Guaranteed income: a predictable "paycheck" in retirement, regardless of market conditions.
  • Protection against longevity risk: a lifetime annuity ensures you can't outlive your income.
  • Simplicity: once purchased, there's no need to manage investments or decide how much to withdraw.
  • Peace of mind: research on retirees suggests that guaranteed income is linked to greater confidence and less anxiety about money.
  • Spousal protection: joint life options can provide for a surviving spouse.
  • Behavioural benefits: annuity income can prevent overspending or panic selling during market downturns.

Disadvantages of Annuities

  • Loss of access to capital: most immediate annuities can't be surrendered once purchased. Your lump sum is converted permanently into income.
  • Inflation risk: a fixed payment that looks generous today will buy much less in 20 years. At 6% inflation, prices roughly double every 12 years.
  • Modest returns: annuity rates reflect long-term interest rates and life expectancy. After accounting for return of capital, the effective return can be lower than other investments.
  • Fees and charges: particularly with variable and indexed annuities, fees for administration, investment management and optional riders can add up significantly.
  • Surrender charges: deferred annuities in many markets charge penalties if you withdraw early, sometimes for 7 to 10 years.
  • Taxation: annuity income is often fully taxable as ordinary income, depending on the country and product.
  • Insurer risk: your income depends on the insurance company's ability to pay. Regulation reduces this risk, but choosing a financially strong provider matters.
  • Complexity: some products are hard to understand, and commissions can encourage mis-selling.

How Annuity Rates Are Set

The income you receive depends on several factors:

  • Your age when payments start: older buyers receive higher payments because payments are expected to last for fewer years.
  • Interest rates: when long-term interest rates are high, annuity rates are generally better.
  • The annuity option: features like return of purchase price, joint life or annual increases reduce starting income.
  • Gender and health: in some markets, rates differ by gender or offer higher "enhanced" rates for people with health conditions that reduce life expectancy.
  • Payment frequency: yearly payments are usually slightly higher in total than monthly payments.
  • The insurer: rates vary between companies, so comparing quotes matters.

Annuities in India

In India, annuities are offered by life insurance companies regulated by the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI). Common products include immediate annuity plans such as LIC's Jeevan Akshay and deferred annuity plans such as LIC's Jeevan Shanti, along with similar plans from private insurers. IRDAI has also required life insurers to offer a standard immediate annuity product, Saral Pension, with simple, uniform features across companies.

Annuities and the National Pension System (NPS)

Annuities are closely linked to the NPS. At retirement, NPS subscribers have generally been required to use at least 40% of their accumulated corpus to buy an annuity from an empanelled insurer, unless the total corpus is below the threshold that allows a full lump-sum withdrawal. The rest can be withdrawn as a tax-free lump sum within limits. PFRDA has been revising NPS exit and withdrawal rules, so check the current requirements when you approach retirement.

Taxation in India

Annuity income in India is taxed as "income from other sources" at your income tax slab rate. Under the old tax regime, premiums paid for certain pension and annuity plans may qualify for deductions under Section 80CCC within the overall 80C limit. The lump sum returned to a nominee under a return-of-purchase-price option is generally not taxed as income for the nominee. Tax rules change, so verify the current position.

Annuity Rates in India

Annuity rates in India typically range from around 5% to 8% a year of the purchase price, depending on age, the option chosen and prevailing interest rates. The highest rates come from the simplest "life annuity without return of purchase price" option for older buyers; options with return of capital usually offer considerably less. Because income is taxable, the post-tax return can be lower than it appears.

Annuities in the United States

In the US, annuities are widely sold by insurers and regulated at the state level, with variable annuities also regulated as securities by the SEC and FINRA. Key points:

  • Tax deferral: earnings in a deferred annuity grow tax-deferred until withdrawn.
  • Early withdrawal penalty: withdrawals of earnings before age 59½ generally face a 10% IRS penalty in addition to income tax.
  • Qualified vs non-qualified: annuities held in retirement accounts like IRAs are fully taxable on withdrawal; non-qualified annuities bought with after-tax money are taxed only on the earnings portion.
  • QLACs: Qualified Longevity Annuity Contracts allow retirees to use part of their IRA or 401(k) to buy a deferred annuity that starts as late as age 85, delaying required minimum distributions on that amount, up to an IRS limit.
  • State guaranty associations provide some protection if an insurer fails, up to limits that vary by state.

A Worked Example

Ramesh, 60, has retirement savings of 60 lakh rupees. His monthly expenses are 50,000 rupees, and he receives no pension.

  • He uses 25 lakh rupees to buy a joint life annuity with his wife. Assuming an annual rate of around 7%, this provides roughly 14,000 to 15,000 rupees a month for as long as either of them lives.
  • He keeps 5 lakh rupees in a liquid fund and savings account as an emergency reserve.
  • He invests the remaining 30 lakh in a mix of debt funds, senior citizen savings schemes and a modest allocation to equity funds for long-term growth, from which he makes regular withdrawals.

The annuity covers a significant share of essential expenses for life, reducing the risk of running out of money, while the rest of the portfolio provides flexibility and growth to help keep up with inflation. This "partial annuitisation" approach is common among retirement planners. (Figures are illustrative; actual rates vary by insurer, age and option.)

The Impact of Inflation on Annuity Income

Inflation is the biggest weakness of a fixed annuity. Consider a fixed income of 30,000 rupees a month starting at age 60. If inflation averages 6% a year, the purchasing power of that income falls over time:

By age 80, the same payment buys less than a third of what it did at retirement. Ways to manage this include choosing an increasing annuity, buying annuities in stages over several years, and keeping part of your savings in growth investments to supplement income later.

Laddering: Buying Annuities in Stages

Instead of putting a large lump sum into one annuity, some retirees "ladder" purchases, for example buying one annuity at 60, another at 65 and a third at 70. Benefits include:

  • Higher rates on later purchases, because you're older when you buy.
  • The chance to benefit if interest rates rise.
  • Keeping more money accessible in the early years of retirement.
  • Spreading risk across different insurers.

The trade-off is that money not yet annuitised remains exposed to market and interest rate changes, and rates could fall instead of rise.

Understanding the "Annuity Puzzle"

Economists have long noticed that fewer people buy annuities than theory suggests they should, given how valuable longevity insurance is. This is known as the annuity puzzle. Explanations include:

  • Reluctance to hand over a large lump sum permanently.
  • Fear of dying early and "losing" money to the insurer.
  • Wanting to leave money to children.
  • Concerns about inflation and insurer reliability.
  • Complexity and distrust of financial products.
  • Existing pensions or government benefits that already provide some guaranteed income.

Behavioural research suggests that framing matters: people find annuities more attractive when described as "guaranteed income for life" rather than as an investment with a particular return. Thinking of an annuity as insurance, not a bet on how long you'll live, can help you decide more objectively.

How to Buy an Annuity in India: Step by Step

  1. Estimate your essential monthly expenses in retirement and your other guaranteed income.
  2. Decide how much of your savings to annuitise to cover the gap.
  3. Choose the annuity option: single or joint life, with or without return of purchase price, fixed or increasing.
  4. Get quotes from several insurers, including LIC and private insurers, or compare through IRDAI-registered aggregators.
  5. Check the insurer's claim settlement ratio and financial strength.
  6. Read the policy document and benefit illustration carefully.
  7. Complete KYC and the proposal form, and pay the purchase price.
  8. Use the free-look period to review the policy, and keep documents safe with your nominee informed.

Who Should Consider an Annuity?

An annuity may suit you if:

  • You have no or limited pension income and worry about outliving your savings.
  • You're in good health with a family history of longevity.
  • You value predictable income over higher potential returns.
  • You find managing investments stressful or difficult.
  • You want to secure a base level of income for essential expenses.

An annuity may not suit you if:

  • You need access to your money for emergencies or large expenses.
  • You have serious health conditions that reduce life expectancy (unless enhanced rates are available).
  • You already have a large guaranteed pension covering your needs.
  • Your main goal is leaving a large inheritance.
  • You're young and have decades for your investments to grow.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Putting all your savings into an annuity, leaving no emergency fund or growth investments.
  • Ignoring inflation when comparing fixed payouts.
  • Buying complex products you don't understand, especially those with many riders and fees.
  • Not comparing quotes from different insurers.
  • Choosing the wrong option, such as a single life annuity when your spouse depends on the income.
  • Confusing insurance-cum-investment plans with pure annuities, or buying products mainly for tax saving.
  • Buying all at once when rates are low, instead of considering staggering purchases over a few years.
  • Not checking the free-look period, which in India typically gives you 15 to 30 days to cancel a new policy if you change your mind.

Questions to Ask Before Buying

  1. What exactly will I receive, how often and for how long?
  2. What happens to payments when I die, or when my spouse dies?
  3. Is any part of the purchase price returned to my nominee?
  4. What fees, charges or commissions apply?
  5. Can I withdraw or surrender, and what penalties apply?
  6. How will the income be taxed?
  7. How financially strong is the insurer, and what is its claim settlement record?
  8. How does this compare with quotes from other insurers?

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an annuity a good investment?

An annuity is better thought of as insurance against living longer than your savings than as an investment for growth. It can be very valuable for securing essential income, but it usually shouldn't hold all your retirement money.

Can I lose money in an annuity?

With a fixed lifetime annuity, you receive guaranteed payments, but if you die early without a return-of-purchase-price option, you may receive less than you paid in total. Variable annuities can lose value if investments perform poorly.

What is the best age to buy an annuity?

Many people buy at or after retirement, often between 60 and 75. Buying later usually gives higher income but leaves you exposed to market and interest rate changes in the meantime. Some retirees stagger purchases over several years.

Are annuities safe?

Annuities from regulated insurers are generally considered safe, as insurers must meet capital and solvency requirements. Choose financially strong providers and understand any guarantees.

Can I cancel an annuity?

Immediate annuities generally can't be cancelled once the free-look period has passed. Deferred annuities may allow surrender, often with charges. Check the terms before buying.

How are annuity payments different from pension payments?

A pension is usually provided by an employer or government. An annuity is a product you buy yourself, often using retirement savings. In practice, many pensions are paid through annuities.

What is the difference between an annuity and a SWP?

A systematic withdrawal plan (SWP) lets you withdraw a fixed amount regularly from a mutual fund. Unlike an annuity, your money remains invested and accessible, and can grow, but there's no guarantee it will last for life. Many retirees combine both.

Do annuity payments stop when I die?

It depends on the option. Single life annuities stop on death. Joint life annuities continue for the surviving spouse, and return-of-purchase-price options pay the original amount to your nominee.

Can NRIs buy annuities in India?

Many Indian insurers allow NRIs to buy annuity plans, subject to residency, KYC and payment rules. Tax treatment depends on both Indian law and the tax laws of the country where you live, so check before buying.

Final Thoughts

Annuities offer something no other financial product does as reliably: guaranteed income for as long as you live. For retirees worried about running out of money, that security can be priceless. But annuities lock up your capital, may not keep pace with inflation and can come with high fees. The best approach for most people is balance: use an annuity to cover essential expenses, keep an emergency fund, and invest the rest for flexibility and growth. Read the terms carefully, compare quotes, and consider advice from a qualified, independent financial planner before committing.
