Quick answer: Some people call Dubai a "fake city" because of its rapid growth from a small trading port into a futuristic metropolis, its artificial islands and record-breaking buildings, its focus on luxury and tourism, its heavy reliance on foreign workers and its carefully managed image. Critics see this as superficial; supporters see an ambitious, well-planned city that diversified away from oil. The truth is more nuanced: Dubai has a real history as a trading hub and a real, diverse population, alongside its showpiece projects.

Dubai is one of the most talked-about cities in the world. Its skyline includes the Burj Khalifa, the world's tallest building, and its coastline features man-made islands shaped like palm trees. Tens of millions of visitors pass through its airport every year. Yet alongside admiration, the city attracts a common criticism: that it's "fake".

This article looks at why that label exists, what's fair and unfair about it, and what Dubai is really like for residents, students, tourists and businesses.

Dubai's Rapid Transformation

For much of the 20th century, Dubai was a modest trading town on Dubai Creek, known for pearl diving, fishing and commerce with India, Iran and East Africa. Oil was discovered in 1966, and the United Arab Emirates was formed in 1971. Dubai's oil reserves were relatively small, so its rulers invested early in trade, aviation, tourism, finance and real estate.

Major milestones include the Jebel Ali port and free zone, Emirates airline, the Burj Al Arab hotel, Palm Jumeirah, Dubai Mall and the Burj Khalifa, completed in 2010. In a few decades, the city went from desert town to global hub, a pace that can make it feel artificial to some observers.

Why Some People Call Dubai a Fake City

1. Artificial Landscapes

Dubai has built islands in the sea, an indoor ski slope, giant aquariums inside malls and lush green landscapes in the desert, all requiring heavy engineering and resources. To critics, these features symbolise a city created for show rather than shaped naturally over time.

2. Focus on Luxury and Image

Seven-star hotel branding, supercars, gold vending machines and record-breaking attractions give Dubai a reputation for excess. Social media amplifies the glamorous image, which some see as hiding everyday realities.

3. A Population Made Up Mostly of Expatriates

Emirati citizens make up only a small minority of Dubai's residents, roughly 10% or less by most estimates. The majority are foreign residents from South Asia, the Middle East, Africa, Europe and elsewhere. Many live in Dubai on renewable work visas, which leads some to describe the city as transient and lacking deep roots.

4. Concerns About Migrant Workers

Human rights organisations have long raised concerns about the conditions of low-wage migrant workers, including recruitment fees, wage issues and housing. The UAE has introduced labour reforms, such as wage protection systems and midday work bans in summer, but critics argue more is needed. This contrast between luxury and labour fuels the "fake" criticism.

5. Managed Media and Public Image

The UAE has strict laws on speech, media and public behaviour. Critics argue that the polished image presented to the world doesn't allow open discussion of problems.

6. Sustainability Questions

Running a large city in the desert requires desalinated water, extensive air conditioning and energy-intensive infrastructure. Environmentalists question whether this model is sustainable, although Dubai is investing heavily in solar power and has hosted major climate events.

Why the "Fake" Label Is Unfair

  • A genuine history: Dubai's trading heritage is visible in Al Fahidi historical district, the souks and the traditional abra boats on Dubai Creek.
  • A real economy: oil now makes up only a small share of Dubai's economy. Trade, logistics, tourism, finance and real estate drive growth.
  • Real communities: millions of people live, work, raise families and build businesses in Dubai, many for decades.
  • Safety and infrastructure: the city is known for low crime, modern infrastructure and efficient services.
  • Cultural diversity: residents from around 200 nationalities create a genuinely international atmosphere.

Dubai's Megaprojects Explained

Burj Khalifa

Completed in 2010, the Burj Khalifa stands about 828 metres tall with more than 160 storeys, making it the world's tallest building. Its observation decks on the 124th, 125th and 148th floors offer views stretching far across the desert and the Gulf. It was named after Sheikh Khalifa bin Zayed Al Nahyan, President of the UAE and ruler of Abu Dhabi, which provided financial support to Dubai during the 2009 debt crisis.

Palm Jumeirah

Built from millions of cubic metres of sand dredged from the sea floor and rock from Hajar Mountains quarries, Palm Jumeirah added dozens of kilometres of new coastline. It hosts hotels, apartments and villas, and is visible from space. Two other planned palms, Palm Jebel Ali and Palm Deira, were delayed for years, with Palm Jebel Ali later revived.

The World Islands

An archipelago of around 300 artificial islands shaped roughly like a map of the world. Development stalled after the 2008 financial crisis, and many islands remained empty for years, becoming a symbol for critics of Dubai's overreach, though some projects have since restarted.

Dubai Mall and Ski Dubai

Dubai Mall is one of the largest shopping centres in the world by total area and includes an aquarium, an ice rink and hundreds of stores. Ski Dubai, inside Mall of the Emirates, offers real snow and skiing in a desert climate, perhaps the attraction most often cited by people who call the city "fake".

Museum of the Future

Opened in 2022, this torus-shaped building covered in Arabic calligraphy showcases ideas about technology, space, health and the environment. It reflects Dubai's ambition to brand itself as a city of the future.

The 2009 Debt Crisis: A Reality Check

Dubai's rapid growth was heavily financed by debt. When the global financial crisis hit, property prices collapsed by around half from their peak, construction projects were frozen and thousands of expatriates left. In November 2009, the state-owned conglomerate Dubai World asked creditors for a standstill on billions of dollars of debt, shocking global markets. Abu Dhabi provided financial support to stabilise the situation.

Critics saw the crisis as proof that Dubai's model was a bubble. Supporters point out that the city recovered, diversified further and continued to grow, hosting Expo 2020 (held in 2021–22) and attracting record numbers of residents and tourists in the years that followed.

Dubai's Leadership and Vision

Dubai is one of seven emirates that make up the UAE and is ruled by the Al Maktoum family. Sheikh Rashid bin Saeed Al Maktoum, who ruled from 1958 to 1990, invested oil revenues in infrastructure such as Port Rashid, the Jebel Ali port and the World Trade Centre. His son, Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum, the current ruler and Vice President and Prime Minister of the UAE, has driven the city's ambitious tourism, aviation and real estate strategy since the 1990s. Long-term plans, such as the Dubai Economic Agenda D33 and the Dubai 2040 Urban Master Plan, aim to double the size of the economy and make the city more sustainable and liveable.

Daily Life in Dubai: What Residents Experience

  • Diverse neighbourhoods: from luxury areas like Downtown and Dubai Marina to busy, affordable districts like Deira, Karama and Al Barsha, where many residents live.
  • Weekend: since 2022, the UAE's official working week runs Monday to Friday, with a half-day on Friday in government, bringing it closer to international business hours.
  • Transport: the Dubai Metro, buses, trams, water taxis and affordable taxis make getting around relatively easy, though many residents drive.
  • Food: a huge range of cuisines, from inexpensive Indian, Pakistani and Filipino eateries to celebrity-chef restaurants.
  • Community: active cultural associations, sports clubs and religious centres serve the city's many nationalities, including temples, churches and gurdwaras alongside mosques.
  • Summer: from June to September, temperatures often exceed 40°C with high humidity, so life moves indoors to air-conditioned malls, homes and offices.

Education in Dubai

Dubai has a large private school sector offering British, American, Indian, International Baccalaureate and other curricula, regulated by the Knowledge and Human Development Authority (KHDA). Several international universities operate branch campuses in Dubai, attracting students from across the region. Fees can be high, so families should compare options carefully.

Tourism in Dubai

Tourism is a pillar of Dubai's economy. Popular attractions include:

  • Burj Khalifa and Dubai Mall.
  • Palm Jumeirah and the Atlantis resort.
  • Desert safaris and dune drives.
  • Dubai Creek, the gold and spice souks and Al Fahidi district.
  • Museum of the Future and Dubai Frame.
  • Beaches, water parks and year-round events.

The best time to visit is usually November to March, when temperatures are pleasant. Summers are extremely hot and humid.

Business in Dubai

Dubai has positioned itself as a regional business hub. Attractions for companies and entrepreneurs include:

  • Dozens of free zones offering 100% foreign ownership and simplified setup.
  • Recent reforms allowing full foreign ownership of many onshore businesses.
  • No personal income tax, although the UAE introduced a federal corporate tax in 2023 and VAT in 2018.
  • World-class airport and port connections linking Asia, Africa and Europe.
  • Long-term residency options such as the Golden Visa for investors and skilled professionals.

One key difference is citizenship. Unlike London or Singapore, Dubai offers very limited routes to UAE citizenship, so most residents remain long-term expatriates, even if they live there for decades. This contributes to the perception of a "temporary" city.

Cultural Heritage You Might Not Expect

Visitors who explore beyond the malls often discover a different Dubai:

  • Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood: restored traditional houses with wind towers (barjeel), an early form of air conditioning, now home to museums, galleries and cafés.
  • Al Shindagha Museum: tells the story of Dubai Creek, the ruling family and the city's maritime past.
  • Dubai Creek: wooden abra boats still ferry passengers across for a tiny fare, and dhows load cargo bound for Iran, India and East Africa.
  • Souks: the gold, spice and textile souks continue centuries-old trading traditions.
  • Hatta: a mountain enclave with a heritage village, dams and hiking trails.
  • Emirati cuisine: dishes such as machboos (spiced rice with meat), luqaimat (sweet dumplings) and Arabic coffee with dates.
  • Traditions: falconry, camel racing and Bedouin hospitality remain important to Emirati identity.

Rules and Etiquette for Visitors and Residents

  • Dress modestly in public places such as malls, government buildings and religious sites; swimwear is fine at beaches and pools.
  • Public displays of affection beyond holding hands can cause offence and, in some cases, legal problems.
  • Alcohol is available in licensed venues, but public drunkenness is illegal.
  • Drug laws are extremely strict, including for some medications that are legal elsewhere. Check before travelling with prescriptions.
  • During Ramadan, avoid eating, drinking or smoking in public during daylight hours out of respect.
  • Be careful about photographing people, especially women, without permission, and about posting criticism online, as UAE laws on speech and privacy are strict.

Working in Dubai: What to Know

  • Job market: strong demand exists in hospitality, aviation, construction, real estate, finance, healthcare, technology, logistics and retail. Recruiters and online job portals are common routes, and networking is very important.
  • Contracts and visas: most residents hold employment visas sponsored by their employer. Since labour law reforms in 2022, most employees are on fixed-term contracts, and newer visa options like the Green Visa for skilled workers and freelancers and the Golden Visa for investors and professionals offer more independence.
  • Salaries: pay varies enormously by nationality, sector and role. Packages often include housing allowances, annual flights home and health insurance, so compare total compensation, not just salary.
  • End-of-service benefits: employees are usually entitled to a gratuity payment based on length of service when they leave, which partly replaces the lack of a state pension for expatriates.
  • Working hours: standard hours are typically eight per day, with reduced hours during Ramadan.
  • Worker protections: the Wage Protection System requires salaries to be paid through approved channels, and employers are prohibited from holding employees' passports, although enforcement concerns remain for some low-wage workers.

Dubai's Sustainability Efforts

Responding to criticism about its environmental footprint, Dubai has launched several major initiatives:

  • Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Solar Park: one of the largest single-site solar parks in the world, planned to reach several gigawatts of capacity by 2030.
  • Clean energy targets: the Dubai Clean Energy Strategy aims for a large share of the city's power to come from clean sources by mid-century, with a goal of net-zero emissions by 2050.
  • Green buildings: regulations require energy- and water-efficient designs for new buildings.
  • Sustainable City: a residential community designed to produce much of its own energy and recycle water and waste.
  • Public transport expansion: extensions of the Metro, including a new Blue Line, aim to reduce car dependence.

The UAE hosted the COP28 climate conference in 2023 at Expo City Dubai, which brought both attention to these efforts and criticism over the country's role as a major oil producer.

A Three-Day Itinerary for Seeing the "Real" and the "New" Dubai

Day 1: Old Dubai

  • Morning: walk through Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood and visit the Al Shindagha Museum.
  • Afternoon: cross Dubai Creek by abra and explore the spice and gold souks.
  • Evening: dinner at a traditional Emirati restaurant or a dhow cruise on the creek.

Day 2: Modern Dubai

  • Morning: visit the Museum of the Future.
  • Afternoon: Dubai Mall, the aquarium and the Burj Khalifa observation deck at sunset.
  • Evening: watch the Dubai Fountain show.

Day 3: Desert and Coast

  • Morning: relax at Jumeirah Beach or Kite Beach with views of the Burj Al Arab.
  • Afternoon and evening: a desert safari with dune driving, camel rides and dinner under the stars, choosing operators with responsible environmental practices.

Is the "Fake City" Criticism Fair? A Balanced Verdict

The label captures something real: Dubai is unusually focused on spectacle, much of its landscape is engineered, most residents are temporary and the city carefully manages its image. Yet calling it "fake" ignores a long trading history, a functioning and diversified economy, millions of real lives and a growing effort to preserve heritage. Perhaps a fairer description is that Dubai is a deliberately designed city, built with extraordinary speed and ambition, with all the strengths and weaknesses that implies. Whether you love or dislike it often depends on what you value in a city: history and organic growth, or opportunity, convenience and bold ambition.

Living in Dubai: Pros and Cons

Pros

  • Tax-free salaries for most employees.
  • Safety, cleanliness and modern infrastructure.
  • International, multicultural community.
  • Excellent global travel connections.

Cons

  • High rents and school fees.
  • Extreme summer heat.
  • Residency usually tied to employment, though long-term visas are expanding.
  • Strict laws on public behaviour and speech.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Dubai really built on artificial land?

Most of Dubai is on natural land, but it has several artificial islands, including Palm Jumeirah and The World islands, built by reclaiming land from the sea.

How old is Dubai?

Dubai has been a settlement for centuries, and the Al Maktoum family has ruled it since 1833. Its modern skyline, however, mostly dates from the 1990s onward.

Is Dubai a good place to live?

For many expatriates, yes, especially for career opportunities, safety and tax-free income. It suits people comfortable with heat, high living costs and local laws.

Is Dubai's economy dependent on oil?

No longer. Oil contributes only a small share of Dubai's GDP; trade, tourism, logistics, real estate and finance are far more important.

How is Dubai different from Abu Dhabi?

Abu Dhabi is the UAE's capital and wealthiest emirate, with most of the country's oil reserves. It tends to be quieter and more government- and energy-focused, with cultural landmarks like the Sheikh Zayed Grand Mosque and the Louvre Abu Dhabi. Dubai is busier, more commercial and more tourism-driven.

What is the best time to visit Dubai?

November to March offers warm, sunny days and pleasant evenings, ideal for beaches, desert trips and outdoor events. Summer is far cheaper for hotels, but daytime heat makes outdoor sightseeing difficult.

Is Dubai a good place for families?

Many families enjoy Dubai's safety, cleanliness, international schools, parks, beaches and child-friendly attractions. The main challenges are school fees, rent and the summer heat, which keeps children indoors for several months.

Does Dubai have its own culture?

Yes. Emirati culture, rooted in Bedouin traditions, Islam, pearl diving and maritime trade, remains strong in family life, food, dress, poetry, falconry and hospitality, even though Emiratis are a minority of the population.

Can foreigners buy property in Dubai?

Yes. Foreigners can buy freehold property in designated areas, and buying property above a certain value can make investors eligible for long-term residency visas.

Is Dubai expensive?

It can be, especially for rent, international school fees and luxury lifestyles. However, many residents live affordably by choosing more modest neighbourhoods, cooking at home and using public transport.

Why do so many Indians live in Dubai?

Centuries of trade between India and the Gulf, short flight times, job opportunities, tax-free salaries and a large established Indian community make Dubai a natural destination. Indians form the largest expatriate group in the UAE.

Is Dubai safe for women travelling alone?

Dubai is generally considered very safe for solo female travellers, with low crime rates and well-lit public spaces. Respecting local dress codes and customs helps avoid misunderstandings.

Final Thoughts

Dubai's dazzling skyline and artificial islands make it easy to dismiss the city as fake. But behind the spectacle is a real trading city with a long history, a diverse population and an economy that has deliberately moved beyond oil. Like any major city, it has genuine problems alongside its achievements. The best way to judge it is to look past both the glossy marketing and the stereotypes.
