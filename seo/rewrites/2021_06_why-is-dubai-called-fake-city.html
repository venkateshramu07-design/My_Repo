Quick answer: Some people call Dubai a "fake city" because of its rapid growth from a small trading port into a futuristic metropolis, its artificial islands and record-breaking buildings, its focus on luxury and tourism, its heavy reliance on foreign workers and its carefully managed image. Critics see this as superficial; supporters see an ambitious, well-planned city that diversified away from oil. The truth is more nuanced: Dubai has a real history as a trading hub and a real, diverse population, alongside its showpiece projects.

Dubai is one of the most talked-about cities in the world. Its skyline includes the Burj Khalifa, the world's tallest building, and its coastline features man-made islands shaped like palm trees. Tens of millions of visitors pass through its airport every year. Yet alongside admiration, the city attracts a common criticism: that it's "fake".

This article looks at why that label exists, what's fair and unfair about it, and what Dubai is really like for residents, students, tourists and businesses.

Dubai's Rapid Transformation

For much of the 20th century, Dubai was a modest trading town on Dubai Creek, known for pearl diving, fishing and commerce with India, Iran and East Africa. Oil was discovered in 1966, and the United Arab Emirates was formed in 1971. Dubai's oil reserves were relatively small, so its rulers invested early in trade, aviation, tourism, finance and real estate.

Major milestones include the Jebel Ali port and free zone, Emirates airline, the Burj Al Arab hotel, Palm Jumeirah, Dubai Mall and the Burj Khalifa, completed in 2010. In a few decades, the city went from desert town to global hub, a pace that can make it feel artificial to some observers.

Why Some People Call Dubai a Fake City

1. Artificial Landscapes

Dubai has built islands in the sea, an indoor ski slope, giant aquariums inside malls and lush green landscapes in the desert, all requiring heavy engineering and resources. To critics, these features symbolise a city created for show rather than shaped naturally over time.

2. Focus on Luxury and Image

Seven-star hotel branding, supercars, gold vending machines and record-breaking attractions give Dubai a reputation for excess. Social media amplifies the glamorous image, which some see as hiding everyday realities.

3. A Population Made Up Mostly of Expatriates

Emirati citizens make up only a small minority of Dubai's residents, roughly 10% or less by most estimates. The majority are foreign residents from South Asia, the Middle East, Africa, Europe and elsewhere. Many live in Dubai on renewable work visas, which leads some to describe the city as transient and lacking deep roots.

4. Concerns About Migrant Workers

Human rights organisations have long raised concerns about the conditions of low-wage migrant workers, including recruitment fees, wage issues and housing. The UAE has introduced labour reforms, such as wage protection systems and midday work bans in summer, but critics argue more is needed. This contrast between luxury and labour fuels the "fake" criticism.

5. Managed Media and Public Image

The UAE has strict laws on speech, media and public behaviour. Critics argue that the polished image presented to the world doesn't allow open discussion of problems.

6. Sustainability Questions

Running a large city in the desert requires desalinated water, extensive air conditioning and energy-intensive infrastructure. Environmentalists question whether this model is sustainable, although Dubai is investing heavily in solar power and has hosted major climate events.

Why the "Fake" Label Is Unfair

  • A genuine history: Dubai's trading heritage is visible in Al Fahidi historical district, the souks and the traditional abra boats on Dubai Creek.
  • A real economy: oil now makes up only a small share of Dubai's economy. Trade, logistics, tourism, finance and real estate drive growth.
  • Real communities: millions of people live, work, raise families and build businesses in Dubai, many for decades.
  • Safety and infrastructure: the city is known for low crime, modern infrastructure and efficient services.
  • Cultural diversity: residents from around 200 nationalities create a genuinely international atmosphere.

Education in Dubai

Dubai has a large private school sector offering British, American, Indian, International Baccalaureate and other curricula, regulated by the Knowledge and Human Development Authority (KHDA). Several international universities operate branch campuses in Dubai, attracting students from across the region. Fees can be high, so families should compare options carefully.

Tourism in Dubai

Tourism is a pillar of Dubai's economy. Popular attractions include:

  • Burj Khalifa and Dubai Mall.
  • Palm Jumeirah and the Atlantis resort.
  • Desert safaris and dune drives.
  • Dubai Creek, the gold and spice souks and Al Fahidi district.
  • Museum of the Future and Dubai Frame.
  • Beaches, water parks and year-round events.

The best time to visit is usually November to March, when temperatures are pleasant. Summers are extremely hot and humid.

Business in Dubai

Dubai has positioned itself as a regional business hub. Attractions for companies and entrepreneurs include:

  • Dozens of free zones offering 100% foreign ownership and simplified setup.
  • Recent reforms allowing full foreign ownership of many onshore businesses.
  • No personal income tax, although the UAE introduced a federal corporate tax in 2023 and VAT in 2018.
  • World-class airport and port connections linking Asia, Africa and Europe.
  • Long-term residency options such as the Golden Visa for investors and skilled professionals.

Living in Dubai: Pros and Cons

Pros

  • Tax-free salaries for most employees.
  • Safety, cleanliness and modern infrastructure.
  • International, multicultural community.
  • Excellent global travel connections.

Cons

  • High rents and school fees.
  • Extreme summer heat.
  • Residency usually tied to employment, though long-term visas are expanding.
  • Strict laws on public behaviour and speech.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Dubai really built on artificial land?

Most of Dubai is on natural land, but it has several artificial islands, including Palm Jumeirah and The World islands, built by reclaiming land from the sea.

How old is Dubai?

Dubai has been a settlement for centuries, and the Al Maktoum family has ruled it since 1833. Its modern skyline, however, mostly dates from the 1990s onward.

Is Dubai a good place to live?

For many expatriates, yes, especially for career opportunities, safety and tax-free income. It suits people comfortable with heat, high living costs and local laws.

Is Dubai's economy dependent on oil?

No longer. Oil contributes only a small share of Dubai's GDP; trade, tourism, logistics, real estate and finance are far more important.

Final Thoughts

Dubai's dazzling skyline and artificial islands make it easy to dismiss the city as fake. But behind the spectacle is a real trading city with a long history, a diverse population and an economy that has deliberately moved beyond oil. Like any major city, it has genuine problems alongside its achievements. The best way to judge it is to look past both the glossy marketing and the stereotypes.
