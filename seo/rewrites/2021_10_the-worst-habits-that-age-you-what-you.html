Quick answer: The habits that age you fastest are smoking, unprotected sun exposure, poor sleep, chronic stress, physical inactivity, a diet high in sugar and ultra-processed foods, heavy drinking, loneliness and long hours of sitting. They damage your skin, heart, brain, muscles and cells, and can add years to your biological age. The good news is that many of these effects can be slowed or partly reversed: quitting smoking, wearing sunscreen, sleeping seven to nine hours, moving every day, eating mostly whole foods, limiting alcohol, managing stress and staying socially connected can help you look and feel younger for longer.

Everyone ages, but not everyone ages at the same rate. Two people born in the same year can look and feel a decade apart by middle age. Genes play a role, but research suggests lifestyle has a major influence on how quickly the body ages.

This guide explains the everyday habits that speed up ageing, what science says about why, and practical changes you can start today to protect your health, energy and appearance.

Chronological Age vs Biological Age

Your chronological age is the number of years since you were born. Your biological age reflects how old your body seems based on markers such as blood pressure, lung function, muscle strength, skin condition and changes in your DNA.

Scientists have developed "epigenetic clocks", pioneered by researchers such as Steve Horvath, which estimate biological age from chemical marks on DNA. Studies using these clocks have found that smoking, obesity, poor diet and chronic stress are linked to faster biological ageing, while exercise, healthy eating and good sleep are linked to slower ageing.

Studies of identical twins suggest that genetics explains only part of the variation in how long people live, with lifestyle and environment playing a large role. In other words, many of your daily choices matter.

Why We Age: The Basics

Ageing is complex, but several processes are involved:

  • Oxidative stress: unstable molecules called free radicals damage cells, proteins and DNA.
  • Inflammation: chronic, low-grade inflammation, sometimes called "inflammaging", contributes to heart disease, diabetes and other age-related conditions.
  • Telomere shortening: telomeres, protective caps on the ends of chromosomes, shorten as cells divide. Research by Nobel laureate Elizabeth Blackburn and psychologist Elissa Epel linked chronic stress to shorter telomeres.
  • Glycation: sugar molecules bind to proteins such as collagen, making skin and blood vessels stiffer.
  • Cellular senescence: damaged cells stop dividing but don't die, releasing inflammatory substances.
  • Loss of muscle and bone with inactivity and age.

Many unhealthy habits speed up these processes.

The Worst Habits That Age You

1. Smoking

Smoking is one of the most powerful accelerators of ageing. It reduces blood flow to the skin, breaks down collagen and elastin, and causes wrinkles, especially around the mouth and eyes. Studies of identical twins have shown that the twin who smoked typically looked noticeably older. Smoking also damages the heart, lungs and blood vessels and raises the risk of cancer, stroke and many other diseases. According to the WHO, tobacco kills more than 8 million people a year worldwide.

What to do: quitting at any age helps. Within a year of stopping, the risk of heart disease drops substantially, and skin circulation improves. Nicotine replacement, medication and counselling increase the chances of success. In India, the national tobacco quitline is 1800-11-2356.

2. Too Much Sun Without Protection

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun is responsible for most visible skin ageing, a process called photoaging. It causes wrinkles, sagging, dark spots and rough texture, and raises the risk of skin cancer. A 2013 Australian study published in the Annals of Internal Medicine found that adults who used sunscreen daily showed no detectable increase in skin ageing over four and a half years, unlike those who used it only occasionally.

What to do: use a broad-spectrum sunscreen of SPF 30 or more on exposed skin daily, reapply when outdoors for long periods, wear hats and sunglasses, and avoid tanning beds. Sun protection matters for all skin tones, although darker skin has more natural protection.

3. Poor Sleep

During deep sleep, the body repairs tissues, balances hormones and clears waste from the brain. Chronic sleep deprivation is linked to weight gain, high blood pressure, diabetes, weakened immunity, poor memory and depression. Research has also found that poor sleepers show more signs of skin ageing and recover more slowly from skin damage.

What to do: aim for seven to nine hours a night, keep a regular schedule, keep your bedroom dark and cool, and avoid screens, heavy meals and caffeine late in the day.

4. Chronic Stress

Short-term stress is normal. Long-term stress keeps cortisol and other stress hormones elevated, which can disrupt sleep, raise blood pressure, increase belly fat and weaken immunity. Blackburn and Epel's research found that women under prolonged stress, such as mothers caring for chronically ill children, had shorter telomeres, equivalent to around a decade of additional ageing.

What to do: use stress-management techniques such as regular exercise, mindfulness, breathing exercises, time in nature, hobbies and talking to people you trust. Address major sources of stress where possible.

5. Physical Inactivity and Too Much Sitting

Without regular activity, the body loses muscle mass and strength (a condition called sarcopenia when severe), bone density, balance and cardiovascular fitness. Long periods of sitting are linked to higher risks of heart disease, diabetes and early death, even among people who exercise.

Exercise is one of the most powerful anti-ageing tools known. Studies have found that people who are physically active have better heart health, stronger muscles and bones, sharper memory and, in some studies, longer telomeres than inactive people.

What to do: the WHO recommends at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity, such as brisk walking, each week, plus muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days. Break up long periods of sitting by standing or walking for a few minutes every half hour.

6. Eating Too Much Sugar and Ultra-Processed Food

Diets high in added sugar, refined carbohydrates and ultra-processed foods promote weight gain, inflammation and insulin resistance. High blood sugar also accelerates glycation, which stiffens collagen in the skin and blood vessels. Large studies have linked high consumption of ultra-processed foods with higher risks of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, depression and early death.

What to do: build meals around vegetables, fruits, whole grains, pulses, nuts, seeds and healthy fats. Limit sugary drinks, sweets, packaged snacks and fried foods. Traditional diets such as the Mediterranean diet, and many home-cooked Indian meals rich in dals, vegetables and whole grains, are associated with healthy ageing.

7. Drinking Too Much Alcohol

Heavy drinking dehydrates the skin, disrupts sleep, damages the liver, raises blood pressure and increases the risk of several cancers. Research has linked heavy alcohol use to faster biological ageing and brain shrinkage. The WHO has stated that no level of alcohol consumption is completely safe for health.

What to do: if you drink, keep it light, have several alcohol-free days each week, and avoid binge drinking.

8. Loneliness and Social Isolation

Humans are social creatures. A widely cited analysis by Julianne Holt-Lunstad and colleagues found that strong social relationships were associated with a significantly higher likelihood of survival, and that the effect of social isolation on mortality was comparable to well-known risk factors such as smoking and obesity. Loneliness is linked to depression, heart disease, dementia and weakened immunity.

What to do: invest time in friends and family, join clubs or community groups, volunteer, and reach out to people regularly, even with a short call or message.

9. Not Drinking Enough Water

Mild dehydration can cause tiredness, headaches and poor concentration, and can make skin look duller. A 2023 study by the US National Institutes of Health found that adults with higher blood sodium levels, a sign of poorer hydration, were more likely to show signs of faster biological ageing and to develop chronic diseases.

What to do: drink water regularly through the day, more in hot weather or when active. Your urine should be pale yellow.

10. Skipping Skin Care Basics

Sleeping in make-up, harsh scrubbing, very hot showers and not moisturising can damage the skin's protective barrier. You don't need expensive products, but a simple routine helps.

What to do: cleanse gently, moisturise daily, and use sunscreen in the morning. Ingredients with good evidence for reducing signs of ageing include retinoids (such as retinol or prescription tretinoin) and vitamin C. A dermatologist can advise what suits your skin.

11. Constant Screen Time and Poor Posture

Hours spent looking down at phones and laptops contribute to neck and back pain and poor posture, which can make you look and feel older. Late-night screen use also disrupts sleep.

What to do: raise screens to eye level, take regular breaks, stretch your neck and shoulders, and switch off devices an hour before bed.

12. A Negative Mindset About Ageing

Research by Becca Levy at Yale found that people with more positive views about ageing lived, on average, about seven and a half years longer than those with negative views, even after accounting for health and other factors. Believing that ageing means inevitable decline may make people less likely to stay active and look after their health.

What to do: notice and challenge negative stereotypes about getting older, keep learning, set goals and stay engaged with life.

13. Skipping Health Check-Ups

Many age-related conditions, such as high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes and high cholesterol, cause no symptoms for years while quietly damaging blood vessels, the heart, kidneys and eyes. In India, a large share of people with high blood pressure or diabetes are thought to be undiagnosed.

What to do: from your thirties onwards, have regular checks of blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol and weight, and follow your doctor's advice on cancer screening and eye and dental check-ups.

14. Ignoring Oral Health

Gum disease is linked not only to tooth loss but also to heart disease and diabetes. Missing teeth can change the shape of the face and make eating healthy foods harder.

What to do: brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste, clean between teeth, limit sugary foods and visit a dentist regularly.

15. Yo-Yo Dieting and Extreme Weight Changes

Repeated cycles of crash dieting and weight regain can lead to loss of muscle, sagging skin and a slower metabolism over time. Very low-calorie diets often lack protein and nutrients needed for healthy ageing.

What to do: aim for gradual, sustainable changes in eating and activity rather than extreme diets, and include enough protein and strength training to preserve muscle.

Habits That Help You Age Well

  • Strength training: building muscle protects metabolism, bones, balance and independence in later life.
  • Eating plenty of plants: fruits, vegetables, legumes and whole grains provide antioxidants and fibre.
  • Getting enough protein, especially as you age, to maintain muscle.
  • Learning new things: a new language, instrument or skill keeps the brain active.
  • Purpose: having a reason to get up in the morning, sometimes called ikigai in Japan, is linked to better health and longevity.
  • Regular health check-ups to catch problems such as high blood pressure, diabetes and high cholesterol early.
  • Laughter and gratitude, which support mood and relationships.

Lessons From the World's Longest-Lived People

Researcher Dan Buettner popularised the idea of "Blue Zones", regions such as Okinawa in Japan, Sardinia in Italy, Nicoya in Costa Rica, Ikaria in Greece and Loma Linda in California, where people were reported to live unusually long lives. Some of the original longevity claims have been questioned by other researchers, who point to possible errors in birth records. Even so, the lifestyle patterns described are consistent with wider health research:

  • Moving naturally throughout the day through walking, gardening and household work.
  • Eating mostly plants, with beans and legumes as staples.
  • Eating until about 80% full.
  • Strong family and community ties.
  • A sense of purpose.
  • Regular routines to relieve stress, such as prayer, naps or social gatherings.

Ageing and the Brain

Many people fear memory loss and dementia more than any other part of ageing. The 2024 report of the Lancet Commission on dementia prevention identified 14 modifiable risk factors, including physical inactivity, smoking, excessive alcohol, high blood pressure, diabetes, obesity, hearing loss, untreated vision loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, social isolation, air pollution, traumatic brain injury and lower levels of education. The commission estimated that addressing these factors could prevent or delay a substantial proportion of dementia cases worldwide.

Practical steps to protect your brain include staying physically active, keeping blood pressure and blood sugar under control, treating hearing loss (for example with hearing aids), wearing a helmet when cycling or riding a two-wheeler, staying socially engaged and continuing to learn throughout life.

Ageing Differently for Men and Women

Women experience major hormonal changes during menopause, when falling oestrogen levels can accelerate bone loss, affect skin elasticity and change fat distribution. Weight-bearing exercise, strength training, adequate calcium, vitamin D and protein, and discussing symptoms with a doctor can help. Men tend to lose muscle mass and testosterone gradually and, on average, are less likely to visit doctors for check-ups, which can delay diagnosis of heart disease and other conditions. For both, the core healthy-ageing habits are the same.

Small Daily Swaps

  • Take the stairs instead of the lift.
  • Swap a sugary drink for water, buttermilk or unsweetened tea.
  • Walk while taking phone calls.
  • Add a handful of vegetables or a bowl of dal to lunch and dinner.
  • Replace late-night scrolling with reading.
  • Call a friend instead of scrolling social media.
  • Apply sunscreen as part of brushing your teeth each morning.

Small habits, repeated daily, add up to big changes over years.

It's Never Too Late to Change

Research consistently shows benefits from healthy changes at any age:

  • People who quit smoking, even in their 60s, gain years of life expectancy compared with those who continue.
  • Older adults who start strength training can significantly increase muscle strength within weeks.
  • Improving diet and activity in midlife is linked to lower risk of dementia and heart disease later.
  • Regular sunscreen use can slow further photoaging at any age.

A Simple 4-Week Anti-Ageing Plan

  1. Week 1: start wearing sunscreen daily, drink more water and go to bed at the same time each night.
  2. Week 2: add a 30-minute walk on at least five days, and break up sitting every 30 minutes.
  3. Week 3: replace one processed snack and one sugary drink a day with fruit, nuts or water; add two short strength workouts.
  4. Week 4: schedule time with friends or family, try 10 minutes of daily mindfulness, and book a health check-up.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which habit ages you the fastest?

Smoking is generally considered the single most damaging habit for both appearance and health. Unprotected sun exposure is the biggest cause of visible skin ageing.

Can you reverse ageing?

You can't stop ageing, but healthy changes can slow it and partly reverse some effects. Quitting smoking, exercising, improving diet and sleep, and protecting your skin can improve health markers and how you look and feel.

Does stress really make you look older?

Yes. Chronic stress affects sleep, skin, weight and cellular ageing. Research has linked prolonged stress with shorter telomeres and faster biological ageing.

What is the best exercise for slowing ageing?

A combination works best: aerobic exercise such as brisk walking or cycling for heart health, strength training for muscles and bones, and balance and flexibility exercises such as yoga.

Do anti-ageing supplements work?

Evidence for most supplements is limited. A balanced diet, exercise, sleep and sun protection have far stronger evidence. Talk to a doctor before taking supplements, especially if you take medication.

At what age should I start using anti-ageing skincare?

Sunscreen is worth using at any age. Many dermatologists suggest adding ingredients like retinoids from your mid-twenties or thirties, but a consistent simple routine matters more than starting early with many products.

Does air pollution age the skin?

Studies suggest that long-term exposure to air pollution, particularly fine particles and traffic-related pollution, is associated with more pigment spots and wrinkles, as well as heart and lung disease. Cleansing your skin at the end of the day, using sunscreen and antioxidants, and limiting exposure on high-pollution days may help.

Is sugar really bad for your skin?

High sugar intake contributes to glycation, which stiffens collagen and elastin, and to inflammation. Cutting back on added sugar benefits both skin and overall health.

How much does genetics affect ageing?

Genes influence how you age, but studies suggest lifestyle and environment account for a large share of differences in health and lifespan, especially before very old age.

Final Thoughts

Ageing is inevitable, but how fast you age is partly up to you. The habits that age you most, smoking, sun damage, poor sleep, stress, inactivity, poor diet, heavy drinking and isolation, are all within your power to change. Start with one or two small improvements, build from there, and remember that it's never too late. The goal isn't to look forever young; it's to stay healthy, energetic and independent for as many years as possible.
