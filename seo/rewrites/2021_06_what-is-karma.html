Quick answer: Karma is the principle, found in Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism, that every intentional action has consequences that shape our future. Good actions tend to bring good results, and harmful actions bring suffering, in this life or, according to these traditions, in future lives. Hindu philosophy describes three main types of karma: Sanchita (accumulated past karma), Prarabdha (karma currently unfolding) and Kriyamana or Agami (karma we create now). Karma is not fate or punishment; it emphasises responsibility for our choices.

"What goes around comes around." People across the world use the word karma casually to describe someone getting what they deserve. But in the traditions where it originated, karma is a rich and subtle idea about action, intention and moral responsibility.

This guide explains what karma really means, its types, how different religions understand it, common misconceptions and how the idea can guide everyday life, whatever your beliefs.

The Meaning of Karma

The Sanskrit word karma literally means "action" or "deed". In Indian philosophy, it refers both to actions and to the law of cause and effect that links actions to their results. Every thought, word and deed leaves an impression, and those impressions shape our character, circumstances and future experiences.

Three ideas are central:

  • Intention matters: especially in Buddhism, the motive behind an action largely determines its karmic weight.
  • Results take time: some consequences appear quickly; others ripen much later.
  • We are responsible: karma places responsibility on the individual rather than on chance or the whims of gods.

The Three Types of Karma in Hindu Philosophy

1. Sanchita Karma: Accumulated Karma

Sanchita karma is the total store of karma accumulated from all past actions, including, in the traditional view, past lives. It is like a large warehouse of seeds that have not yet sprouted.

2. Prarabdha Karma: Karma in Motion

Prarabdha karma is the portion of accumulated karma that has begun to bear fruit in this life. It is often linked to circumstances we did not choose, such as our family, body and certain major life events. A traditional image compares it to an arrow already released from the bow: it must complete its flight.

3. Kriyamana (or Agami) Karma: Karma We Create Now

Kriyamana karma is created by our present actions. Some of its results appear quickly; the rest are added to our accumulated store. This is where free will operates. Although we may not control what we were born into, we can choose how we act now.

Karma in Different Traditions

Hinduism

Karma is closely linked to dharma (duty and righteous living), samsara (the cycle of birth and rebirth) and moksha (liberation from that cycle). The Bhagavad Gita teaches karma yoga, the path of selfless action: doing one's duty without attachment to the results, which is said to free a person from karmic bondage.

Buddhism

The Buddha taught that karma is intention: "It is intention that I call karma." Skilful actions rooted in generosity, kindness and wisdom lead to well-being, while unskilful actions rooted in greed, hatred and delusion lead to suffering. The goal is to end the cycle of rebirth by reaching nirvana.

Jainism

Jainism describes karma as fine particles of matter that cling to the soul because of passions and harmful actions. Strict non-violence (ahimsa), self-discipline and right knowledge help shed these particles and liberate the soul.

Sikhism

Sikhism accepts karma and rebirth but emphasises that divine grace, remembrance of God and honest, compassionate living can overcome karmic bonds.

Karma in the Ancient Texts

The idea of karma developed over many centuries in Indian thought.

  • The Vedas: in the earliest Vedic texts, karma referred mainly to ritual action, especially sacrifices performed correctly to bring desired results.
  • The Upanishads: these philosophical texts expanded karma into a moral principle. The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad states that "as a person acts, so does he become": good action makes one good, bad action makes one bad. The Upanishads also link karma to rebirth and to the possibility of liberation.
  • The Bhagavad Gita: part of the epic Mahabharata, it presents Krishna's teaching to the warrior Arjuna. Its famous verse (2.47) says, in essence, "You have a right to your actions, but never to the fruits of your actions." This is the heart of nishkama karma, desireless action.
  • The Dhammapada: a collection of the Buddha's sayings, begins: "Mind precedes all mental states... If one speaks or acts with an impure mind, suffering follows as the wheel follows the foot of the ox."
  • Jain scriptures: describe karma in detail as a subtle substance binding the soul, with specific types obstructing knowledge, perception, bliss and other qualities.

Karma Yoga: The Path of Selfless Action

The Bhagavad Gita describes several paths to spiritual growth, including karma yoga, the yoga of action. It doesn't ask people to withdraw from the world. Instead, it teaches that ordinary work, done in the right spirit, can be a spiritual practice.

Principles of Karma Yoga

  • Do your duty: fulfil your responsibilities to family, work and society, according to your role and abilities.
  • Act without selfish attachment: give your full effort without being consumed by desire for reward or fear of failure.
  • Offer actions to a higher purpose: whether to God, the common good or your values.
  • Maintain equanimity: treat success and failure with balance, learning from both.
  • Serve others: selfless service, known as seva, is a central practice.

Karma Yoga in Modern Life

Karma yoga is surprisingly practical today:

  • A teacher who focuses on helping students learn, rather than on recognition, practises karma yoga.
  • An employee who does excellent work even when nobody is watching reflects its spirit.
  • A volunteer who serves meals at a community kitchen, such as a Sikh gurdwara's langar, embodies selfless service.
  • A parent who cares for children without keeping score expresses it daily.

Psychologically, focusing on effort rather than outcome reduces anxiety and burnout, because you invest in what you can control.

Good, Bad and Neutral Karma

Traditional teachings often classify actions by their quality and their effects.

  • Good (punya or kusala) karma: actions rooted in generosity, compassion, honesty and wisdom, such as helping those in need, speaking truthfully and practising self-control. They tend to lead to happiness and favourable circumstances.
  • Bad (papa or akusala) karma: actions rooted in greed, hatred, cruelty or deception, such as harming others, stealing or lying. They tend to lead to suffering.
  • Neutral karma: everyday actions with little moral weight, like routine tasks.
  • Actions beyond karma: in some teachings, actions performed with complete selflessness and wisdom don't bind the doer at all. This is the ideal of the enlightened person, who acts for the good of all without attachment.

The Three Gunas and Karma

The Bhagavad Gita also describes three gunas, qualities of nature that colour our actions:

  • Sattva: purity, clarity and balance. Sattvic actions are calm, selfless and wise.
  • Rajas: passion, activity and desire. Rajasic actions are driven by ambition and restlessness.
  • Tamas: inertia, ignorance and darkness. Tamasic actions are careless, harmful or lazy.

The Gita suggests cultivating sattva through mindful living, healthy food, study and service, which in turn leads to better actions and better karma.

Collective Karma

Some teachers speak of collective karma: the idea that families, communities and nations also experience the consequences of shared actions. Environmental damage, social injustice and conflict can be seen as examples, where the choices of many people over time produce results that affect everyone. Whether understood spiritually or practically, the concept encourages responsibility not only for our individual actions but for our contribution to society.

Karma and Rebirth

In Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions, karma is closely tied to samsara, the cycle of birth, death and rebirth. According to these teachings:

  • The accumulated impressions of actions shape the circumstances of future births.
  • Rebirth can occur in many forms of life, depending on one's karma.
  • The ultimate goal is liberation from the cycle: moksha in Hinduism and Jainism, nirvana in Buddhism.
  • Liberation comes through wisdom, ethical living, devotion, meditation and the exhaustion or transcendence of karma.

Buddhism differs from Hinduism in rejecting a permanent soul (atman); instead, it describes a continuity of consciousness and causes from one life to the next.

Common Misconceptions About Karma

  • "Karma is fate." Karma includes the results of past actions, but it also stresses that present choices shape the future. It is about responsibility, not helplessness.
  • "Karma is cosmic punishment." In most traditions, karma is a natural law of cause and effect, not a judge seeking revenge.
  • "People who suffer deserve it." Using karma to blame victims of illness, poverty or injustice goes against the compassion these traditions teach. Many teachers warn strongly against this.
  • "Instant karma always happens." Consequences may take time to unfold, and they are not always visible.
  • "Good deeds cancel bad deeds like a bank account." Traditions emphasise transforming intention and character, not keeping a balance sheet.

Karma and Modern Psychology

You don't need to believe in rebirth to see practical wisdom in karma. Psychology offers parallels:

  • Habits shape character: repeated actions strengthen neural pathways, making them easier to repeat.
  • Reciprocity: people tend to respond to kindness with kindness and to hostility with hostility.
  • Reputation: how you treat others influences how they treat you over time.
  • Well-being: research links generosity, gratitude and kindness with greater happiness and health.

How to Practise Good Karma in Daily Life

  1. Act with good intentions: pause before reacting, and ask what motive is driving you.
  2. Speak kindly and honestly: words have consequences too.
  3. Help without expecting rewards: small acts of service count.
  4. Take responsibility: when you cause harm, apologise and make amends.
  5. Do your duty well: work, family and community responsibilities are opportunities for good action.
  6. Let go of outcomes: focus on doing the right thing rather than on what you'll get.
  7. Practise mindfulness: awareness of thoughts and feelings helps you choose wisely.

Examples of Karma in Everyday Life

These simple scenarios show how the principle of cause and effect plays out, whether you see it spiritually or psychologically.

  • At work: a colleague who consistently helps others and shares credit builds goodwill. When she needs support on a difficult project, people are eager to help.
  • In relationships: someone who repeatedly breaks promises finds that friends gradually stop trusting him, even when he is sincere.
  • With health: years of regular exercise, good sleep and healthy eating tend to show up later as better energy and fewer health problems.
  • With money: small, consistent saving habits gradually build security, while habitual overspending creates stress.
  • Inner karma: a person who practises anger daily becomes quicker to anger; one who practises patience becomes calmer. Our repeated actions literally shape our character.

Karma, Free Will and Fate

A common question is whether karma means everything is predetermined. Most traditions say no. Past karma shapes the situations we find ourselves in, but present choices remain free. A popular analogy compares life to a game of cards: the hand you're dealt is your past karma, but how you play it is your free will. Even someone born into hardship can, through present action, create new and better karma.

This balance is why karma is better understood as a teaching about responsibility than about fate. It invites us to accept what has already happened while taking full ownership of what we do next.

What Science Says About "Karma-Like" Effects

Science can't test rebirth, but research does show that many good actions benefit the person who does them, which looks a lot like karma in this life.

  • Generosity and happiness: a study by Elizabeth Dunn and colleagues published in Science in 2008 found that people who spent money on others reported greater happiness than those who spent it on themselves.
  • Volunteering and health: research has linked regular volunteering with lower rates of depression and, in some studies of older adults, longer life.
  • Honesty and wellbeing: a University of Notre Dame study found that participants who reduced lying over ten weeks reported better mental and physical health.
  • Reciprocity: social science consistently finds that people tend to return favours and cooperation, so kindness tends to come back over time.
  • Reputation: in workplaces and communities, a reputation for fairness and reliability brings more trust, support and opportunity.

None of this proves the spiritual law of karma, but it suggests the practical wisdom behind it is well grounded.

Karma in Popular Culture

In everyday English, "karma" is often used loosely to mean instant cosmic payback: someone cuts in line, then trips; a rude driver gets a parking ticket. Songs, films and social media are full of "karma" moments. While these can be amusing, they simplify a much deeper idea. Traditional teachings emphasise intention, inner transformation and long-term consequences rather than quick revenge. The popular phrase "what goes around comes around" captures part of the idea, but not the emphasis on compassion and self-improvement.

Famous Teachings and Quotes on Karma

  • "You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions." — Bhagavad Gita 2.47
  • "As a person acts, so does he become. The doer of good becomes good; the doer of evil becomes evil." — Brihadaranyaka Upanishad
  • "Intention, monks, is what I call karma." — the Buddha, Anguttara Nikaya
  • "We are what our thoughts have made us; so take care about what you think." — attributed to Swami Vivekananda, whose lectures on karma yoga helped introduce the idea to Western audiences
  • "How people treat you is their karma; how you react is yours." — a popular modern saying, often attributed to Wayne Dyer

Swami Vivekananda's book Karma Yoga, based on lectures he gave in New York in the 1890s, remains one of the most accessible introductions to the idea of working without attachment.

Karma at Work and in Business

The principle of karma applies naturally to professional life. Businesses that treat customers honestly, pay suppliers fairly and look after employees tend to build loyalty and trust that pay off over years. Professionals who share knowledge, keep promises and give credit generously find that colleagues support them in return. Conversely, shortcuts, dishonesty and exploitation may bring quick gains but often damage reputations and relationships in the long run. Many business leaders describe this simply as "doing the right thing", which is karma expressed in practical terms.

Karma and Compassion

A thoughtful understanding of karma encourages compassion rather than judgement. If everyone is shaped by countless causes, many beyond their control, we have reason to be patient with others' struggles. The Buddhist practice of metta (loving-kindness) and the Hindu ideal of seeing the divine in every being both flow from this view. Helping someone in difficulty, rather than assuming they "deserve" it, is itself an act of good karma.

A Simple Daily Karma Practice

  1. Morning intention: before starting your day, set an intention, such as "Today I will act with patience and honesty."
  2. Mindful action: during the day, pause before important responses and ask whether your words and actions align with that intention.
  3. One act of service: do at least one helpful act without expecting anything back.
  4. Evening reflection: review the day. What actions created goodwill? Where did you fall short? What will you do differently tomorrow?
  5. Self-forgiveness: acknowledge mistakes without harsh self-judgement, and commit to doing better.

Practised consistently, this simple routine gradually shapes habits and character, which is karma in its most practical form.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is karma real?

As a spiritual law involving rebirth, karma is a matter of faith rather than scientific proof. As a practical principle that actions have consequences, it is widely observed in everyday life.

Can bad karma be removed?

Traditions suggest it can be transformed through sincere repentance, good actions, self-discipline, devotion and spiritual practice.

What is the difference between karma and dharma?

Karma is action and its consequences. Dharma is one's duty or the righteous way of living. Acting in line with dharma is considered to create good karma.

Does karma apply to thoughts?

In Buddhism and many Hindu teachings, yes. Intentions and thoughts are seen as the roots of action and therefore carry karmic weight.

Do you have to be religious to believe in karma?

No. Many people with no religious affiliation embrace karma as a practical philosophy: that kindness, honesty and responsibility tend to create better lives and communities, while harmful actions tend to backfire.

What does "instant karma" mean?

It's a popular phrase for consequences that seem to follow an action immediately, such as someone being rude and then embarrassed moments later. Traditional teachings say consequences can come quickly or slowly, and focus more on inner transformation than on visible payback.

Can prayer or rituals cancel bad karma?

Many traditions teach that sincere prayer, repentance, devotion and good deeds can soften or transform the effects of past actions. Most also emphasise that changing your behaviour is essential; rituals without inner change have limited value.

Does karma affect my next life?

In Hindu, Buddhist and Jain traditions, yes: accumulated karma shapes future births until liberation is attained. Those who don't believe in rebirth often focus on how actions shape this life and the lives of others.

Is karma the same in Hinduism and Buddhism?

Both teach that intentional actions have consequences and are linked to rebirth. Buddhism places particular emphasis on intention and doesn't accept a permanent soul, while Hinduism generally describes an eternal self (atman) that carries karma across lives.

Can you have good karma and still suffer?

Yes. Traditions teach that suffering can result from past karma, from the actions of others or simply from being part of an impermanent world. Suffering is not proof of bad character, and good people experience hardship too.

Does helping others improve my karma?

Helping others with genuine care is considered one of the most powerful ways to create good karma. Most traditions stress that the motive matters: help given sincerely carries more weight than help given for praise.

Final Thoughts

Karma reminds us that our choices matter. We may not control every circumstance of our lives, but each day we decide how to act, what to say and what intentions to carry. Choose kindness, honesty and responsibility, and let go of the need for immediate rewards. Whether you see karma as a spiritual law or simple wisdom, living this way tends to create a better life for you and for those around you.
