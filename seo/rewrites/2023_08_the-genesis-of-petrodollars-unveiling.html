Quick answer: "Petrodollars" are US dollars earned by oil-exporting countries from selling oil, and the "petrodollar system" refers to the long-standing practice of pricing and trading most of the world's oil in US dollars. It took shape in the 1970s, after the US ended the dollar's convertibility to gold in 1971 and the 1973 oil crisis sent prices soaring. Through arrangements with Saudi Arabia and the wider practice among OPEC members, oil came to be priced in dollars, and oil exporters "recycled" their dollar earnings into US Treasury bonds, Western banks and investments. The system has helped sustain global demand for the dollar and its role as the main reserve currency. Today, some oil trade is shifting to other currencies, but the dollar still dominates.

Every time a country buys crude oil on the international market, the price is usually quoted in US dollars, whether the buyer is in India, Japan or Germany, and whether the seller is Saudi Arabia, Nigeria or Norway. This arrangement has shaped global finance for half a century, influencing exchange rates, interest rates, inflation, geopolitics and the economic power of the United States.

This article explains how the petrodollar system began, how it works, why it matters, its impact on countries like India, the debates about its future, and what "de-dollarisation" really means.

Before the Petrodollar: Bretton Woods

At the end of World War II, in 1944, 44 Allied nations met at Bretton Woods in New Hampshire to design a new international monetary system. Under the Bretton Woods agreement, the US dollar was pegged to gold at US$35 per ounce, and other currencies were pegged to the dollar. Central banks could exchange dollars for gold at the US Treasury. This made the dollar the centre of the global financial system.

By the late 1960s, the system was under strain. US spending on the Vietnam War and domestic programmes, along with growing trade deficits, meant there were far more dollars abroad than US gold reserves could cover. Other countries began demanding gold for their dollars.

The Nixon Shock of 1971

On 15 August 1971, US President Richard Nixon announced that the United States would suspend the convertibility of the dollar into gold. This "Nixon Shock" effectively ended the Bretton Woods system. By 1973, major currencies were floating against each other. The dollar was now a "fiat" currency, backed by the strength of the US economy and confidence in the US government rather than gold.

This raised a question: what would sustain worldwide demand for dollars without the gold link?

The 1973 Oil Crisis

In October 1973, during the Yom Kippur War, Arab members of OPEC imposed an oil embargo on the United States and other countries that supported Israel. Oil prices roughly quadrupled within months, causing fuel shortages, inflation and economic turmoil in importing nations. Oil-exporting countries suddenly earned enormous revenues.

The US-Saudi Arrangement

In 1974, the United States and Saudi Arabia, the world's largest oil exporter at the time, established a Joint Commission on Economic Cooperation and deepened their economic and security ties. As part of these arrangements, Saudi Arabia continued to price its oil in dollars and invested much of its surplus oil revenue in US Treasury securities, with the US providing military support and security cooperation. Some details of the arrangements were confidential for decades; in 2016, Bloomberg reported on how US Treasury holdings by Saudi Arabia had been kept confidential under the deals.

Because Saudi Arabia was so influential within OPEC, other members also priced oil in dollars. By the mid-1970s, dollar pricing of oil had become the global norm.

In 2024, claims went viral online that a "50-year petrodollar agreement" between the US and Saudi Arabia had expired. Fact-checkers and analysts pointed out that there was no formal treaty with a 50-year expiry date; the petrodollar system rests on a mix of agreements, market practice and mutual interests rather than a single contract.

How the Petrodollar System Works

  1. Oil is priced in dollars: buyers around the world need dollars to purchase oil.
  2. Importers acquire dollars: countries must earn or buy dollars through exports, foreign investment or currency markets.
  3. Exporters earn dollars: oil-producing countries accumulate large dollar revenues.
  4. Petrodollar recycling: exporters invest surplus dollars in US Treasury bonds, other dollar assets, Western banks and global investments, or use them to buy goods, including weapons, from the US and other countries.
  5. Demand for dollars stays high: this supports the dollar's value and status as the world's main reserve currency.

How Oil Prices Are Set

International oil prices are largely set by trading in benchmark crude grades. The two most widely used benchmarks are Brent, based on North Sea crude and used for much of the world's oil, and West Texas Intermediate (WTI), the main US benchmark. Middle Eastern crude sold to Asia is often priced relative to benchmarks such as Dubai and Oman. Contracts for these benchmarks trade on exchanges in London, New York and elsewhere, overwhelmingly in US dollars.

Prices respond to supply and demand, inventories, geopolitical events, economic growth and production decisions by major exporters. Since 2016, OPEC has coordinated production with several non-OPEC producers, including Russia, in a group often called "OPEC+". Their output decisions can move global prices significantly.

Why the Petrodollar Matters

For the United States

  • Persistent demand for dollars supports its role as the leading reserve currency.
  • Cheaper borrowing: foreign purchases of Treasury bonds help keep US interest rates lower than they might otherwise be.
  • "Exorbitant privilege": a phrase associated with French finance minister Valéry Giscard d'Estaing in the 1960s, describing the advantage the US gains from issuing the world's main currency.
  • Geopolitical influence: dollar dominance strengthens the reach of US financial sanctions, because most international transactions pass through the dollar system.

For Oil Exporters

  • A stable, widely accepted currency for revenue
  • Access to deep, liquid US financial markets for investing surpluses
  • Security partnerships with the US in some cases
  • Vulnerability to US monetary policy and exchange-rate movements

For Oil Importers Like India

  • Oil import costs depend on both oil prices and the rupee-dollar exchange rate.
  • A weaker rupee raises the cost of oil imports, contributing to inflation and wider trade deficits.
  • Countries must maintain dollar reserves to pay for energy imports.
  • US interest rate changes can affect capital flows and currency stability.

The Dollar's Global Dominance Today

The US dollar remains the world's dominant currency. According to IMF data on official foreign exchange reserves, the dollar accounts for close to 60% of allocated global reserves, far ahead of the euro. The Bank for International Settlements' surveys of foreign exchange markets have found that the dollar is on one side of close to 90% of all currency trades. Oil is just one part of this picture, but energy trade helped build and sustain the dollar's central role.

Petrodollar Recycling and Its Effects

In the 1970s, oil exporters deposited huge sums in Western banks, which lent much of this money to developing countries in Latin America, Africa and elsewhere. When US interest rates rose sharply in the early 1980s under Federal Reserve chairman Paul Volcker to fight inflation, many borrowers struggled to repay, contributing to the Latin American debt crisis. Petrodollar flows have also funded sovereign wealth funds, such as those of Norway, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait and Qatar, which now invest globally in companies, real estate and infrastructure.

The Petrodollar and Global Inflation

Because oil is an input into transport, manufacturing, farming and electricity, changes in oil prices ripple through the global economy. The oil shocks of the 1970s contributed to "stagflation", a painful combination of high inflation and weak growth, in many countries. Central banks responded with high interest rates, most famously under Paul Volcker at the US Federal Reserve. When the dollar strengthens and oil prices rise at the same time, importing countries face a double hit: more expensive oil in dollar terms, and a weaker local currency with which to buy it. This combination can push up inflation quickly in emerging economies.

Challenges to the Petrodollar System

Trade in Other Currencies

  • China: launched yuan-denominated crude oil futures on the Shanghai International Energy Exchange in 2018, and some of its oil trade is settled in yuan.
  • Russia: after Western sanctions following its 2022 invasion of Ukraine, Russia increasingly sold oil in other currencies, including yuan, rupees and UAE dirhams.
  • India: in 2023, Indian Oil Corporation paid for a shipment of crude oil from the UAE's national oil company in rupees, as part of efforts to promote rupee settlement in trade.
  • Saudi Arabia: has signalled openness to accepting other currencies and in 2024 joined the mBridge project, an experimental cross-border central bank digital currency platform involving several central banks.

Geopolitical Shifts

The use of financial sanctions has encouraged some countries to reduce reliance on the dollar. BRICS countries have discussed alternatives for trade settlement, though a common BRICS currency faces major practical hurdles.

The Energy Transition

As the world shifts toward renewable energy and electric vehicles, oil's long-term importance may decline, potentially reducing the significance of oil-based dollar flows.

US Shale Production

The US shale boom transformed the US into one of the world's largest oil producers and a net exporter of petroleum products, changing its relationship with Middle Eastern oil.

Oil, the Rupee and the Indian Economy

India imports the large majority of the crude oil it consumes, often estimated at around 85% of its needs, making it one of the world's biggest oil importers. This creates a direct link between global oil prices, the dollar and India's economy:

  • Inflation: higher oil prices raise the cost of fuel, transport and many goods.
  • Current account deficit: a larger oil bill widens the gap between imports and exports.
  • Rupee pressure: greater demand for dollars to pay for oil can weaken the rupee, which in turn makes oil even more expensive in rupee terms.
  • Government finances: fuel taxes, subsidies and the prices set by state-owned oil companies are affected by oil price swings.

To manage these risks, India has built strategic petroleum reserves, diversified its sources of crude, expanded renewable energy, encouraged electric vehicles and promoted rupee settlement for some trade. In 2022, the Reserve Bank of India introduced a framework allowing international trade to be invoiced and settled in rupees through special Vostro accounts, as part of a broader effort to internationalise the rupee.

Sanctions and the Dollar System

Because most international payments involve US dollars and pass through US-linked banks at some point, the US can use the dollar system as a tool of foreign policy. Sanctions can block countries, companies or individuals from accessing dollar transactions, freeze assets and cut them off from major international payment networks such as SWIFT, which is based in Belgium. Countries such as Iran, Venezuela and Russia have faced extensive financial sanctions. This power has encouraged some nations to explore alternatives, including bilateral currency arrangements, domestic payment systems and new cross-border platforms.

The Triffin Dilemma

Economist Robert Triffin pointed out in the 1960s that the country issuing the world's main reserve currency faces a dilemma. To supply enough of its currency to the world, it must run persistent deficits, sending dollars abroad. But over time, those deficits can undermine confidence in the currency. This tension helped bring down the Bretton Woods system and remains relevant to debates about the long-term sustainability of dollar dominance.

Oil Wealth and Sovereign Wealth Funds

Many oil exporters have used petrodollar surpluses to create sovereign wealth funds that invest for future generations and diversify their economies. Norway's Government Pension Fund Global, built from North Sea oil revenues, has grown into one of the world's largest investment funds, owning small stakes in thousands of companies worldwide. Gulf states such as Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait and Qatar also run large funds that invest in global equities, real estate, technology companies and infrastructure. These funds are an important modern form of petrodollar recycling, channelling oil wealth into the global economy.

Common Myths About the Petrodollar

  • "The dollar is backed by oil." The dollar isn't formally backed by oil or any commodity. Its value rests on the US economy, institutions, financial markets and global demand, of which oil trade is one part.
  • "There's a single secret petrodollar treaty." The system grew from a combination of agreements, security relationships, OPEC practice and market convenience.
  • "If oil stopped being priced in dollars, the dollar would collapse overnight." Oil is a significant but limited share of global trade and finance; the dollar's role depends on many other factors.
  • "All oil is already sold in dollars." Most is, but a growing share, particularly involving Russia, China and India, is settled in other currencies.

What a More Multipolar Currency World Could Look Like

If oil and other trade gradually diversify away from the dollar, the result is more likely to be a "multipolar" system than the replacement of the dollar by a single rival. In such a world, regional trade might increasingly be settled in local currencies, central banks might hold a wider mix of reserves including euros, yuan, gold and others, and new payment technologies could reduce reliance on any single network. For oil importers like India, this could reduce some exchange-rate risk and costs, but it would also bring new complexities in managing multiple currencies and partners.

Is the Petrodollar Ending?

Despite growing use of other currencies, the dollar remains dominant in oil trade and global finance. Its advantages, including deep and liquid financial markets, legal protections, convertibility and network effects, are hard to replicate. Most analysts expect gradual diversification rather than a sudden collapse. Alternatives face obstacles: the Chinese yuan isn't fully convertible and capital controls limit its use; the euro lacks a unified bond market comparable to US Treasuries; and other currencies have smaller, less liquid markets.

Key Terms Explained

  • Reserve currency: a currency held in large amounts by central banks for international trade and financial stability.
  • Fiat currency: money not backed by a physical commodity such as gold, whose value rests on government decree and public confidence.
  • Petrodollar recycling: the reinvestment of oil exporters' dollar surpluses into global financial markets and economies.
  • Sovereign wealth fund: a state-owned investment fund, often financed by commodity revenues.
  • De-dollarisation: efforts to reduce reliance on the US dollar in trade, reserves and finance.
  • Vostro account: an account held by a foreign bank with a domestic bank, used to settle trade in the domestic currency.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the definition of a petrodollar?

A petrodollar is a US dollar paid to an oil-exporting country for its oil. The term also describes the system, dating from the 1970s, in which oil is mostly priced and traded in dollars and exporters invest their surplus dollars back into US assets.

What happens if the US loses the petrodollar?

Fewer countries would need dollars to buy oil, which could gradually weaken demand for the dollar and US Treasury bonds and nudge up US borrowing costs. The change would likely be slow: the dollar's role rests mainly on the size and openness of US financial markets, not only on oil pricing, so a shift away from dollar-priced oil would not end dollar dominance overnight.

Which president started the petrodollar?

The arrangement took shape under President Richard Nixon. After he ended the dollar's convertibility into gold in 1971, his administration agreed in 1974 to economic and military cooperation with Saudi Arabia, which priced its oil in dollars and invested its surplus in US Treasury bonds.

Which countries use the US dollar as their currency?

Several countries use the US dollar as their official currency, including Ecuador, El Salvador, Panama (alongside its balboa coins), Timor-Leste, Palau, the Marshall Islands and the Federated States of Micronesia, as well as US territories such as Puerto Rico and Guam.

What are petrodollars?

Petrodollars are US dollars earned by oil-exporting countries from oil sales, and the term also refers to the system of pricing oil in dollars.

When did the petrodollar system start?

It developed in the early-to-mid 1970s, after the end of the dollar's gold convertibility in 1971 and the 1973 oil crisis, with arrangements between the US and Saudi Arabia in 1974 playing a key role.

Why is oil priced in dollars?

Because of historical arrangements, Saudi Arabia's influence in OPEC, the dollar's role as the leading reserve currency and the depth of US financial markets.

How does the petrodollar affect India?

India pays for most oil imports in dollars, so its oil bill depends on both oil prices and the rupee-dollar exchange rate, affecting inflation and the trade deficit.

Will the petrodollar collapse?

A sudden collapse appears unlikely. More probable is gradual diversification, with some oil trade conducted in other currencies while the dollar remains dominant.

What is de-dollarisation?

De-dollarisation refers to efforts by countries to reduce their dependence on the US dollar in trade, reserves and financial transactions, for example by settling trade in local currencies.

Why does a strong dollar hurt oil importers?

Since oil is priced in dollars, a stronger dollar means importers must spend more of their own currency to buy the same amount of oil, even if dollar oil prices don't change.

What is OPEC+?

OPEC+ is the grouping of OPEC members and several other major oil producers, including Russia, that have coordinated oil production since 2016 to influence global prices.

Can India pay for all its oil in rupees?

Not currently. Rupee settlement has been used for some transactions, but most sellers still prefer dollars because they're easier to use and invest globally. Wider rupee use would require greater international demand for rupee assets.

What is the "exorbitant privilege"?

It's a phrase associated with French finance minister Valéry Giscard d'Estaing in the 1960s, describing the advantages the US enjoys because the dollar is the world's main reserve currency, such as cheaper borrowing and global demand for its debt.

Does the energy transition threaten the petrodollar?

Over the long term, reduced oil demand could lessen the importance of oil-related dollar flows, but the dollar's dominance rests on much more than oil.

Final Thoughts

The petrodollar system emerged from a turbulent decade: the end of the gold-backed dollar, the oil shocks of the 1970s and new partnerships between the US and oil producers. It helped cement the dollar's global dominance, shaping trade, finance and geopolitics for half a century. Today, shifting alliances, sanctions, new payment technologies and the energy transition are slowly reshaping the picture. Understanding the petrodollar helps explain why oil prices, exchange rates and US interest rates matter so much to economies around the world, including India.
