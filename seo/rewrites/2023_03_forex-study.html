Quick answer: The forex (foreign exchange) market is where currencies are bought and sold. It's the world's largest financial market, with average daily turnover in the trillions of dollars, used by banks, companies, governments, investors and speculators. Currencies trade in pairs, such as USD/INR or EUR/USD, and prices move with interest rates, inflation, economic data and global events. Forex trading can be highly risky: leverage magnifies both gains and losses, and most retail traders lose money. In India, residents may trade only permitted currency pairs on recognised Indian exchanges; trading on unauthorised online forex platforms is not allowed.

Every time you exchange rupees for dollars before a trip, send money abroad or buy something priced in a foreign currency, you take part in the foreign exchange market. Behind these everyday transactions is a vast global market that never sleeps during the working week and influences prices, trade and investment worldwide.

This guide explains how the forex market works, who participates, what moves exchange rates, the basics of forex trading, the significant risks involved, the legal position in India, and how to protect yourself from scams.

What Is the Forex Market?

The foreign exchange market is a global, decentralised market for exchanging currencies. Unlike a stock exchange, there's no single central marketplace. Instead, trading happens electronically through a network of banks, brokers and financial institutions around the world, a structure known as over-the-counter (OTC) trading.

According to the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) Triennial Central Bank Survey, global forex trading averaged about 7.5 trillion US dollars per day in April 2022, and the 2025 survey showed further growth to nearly 10 trillion. That's far larger than daily trading in global stock markets.

The market operates 24 hours a day, five days a week, moving through major financial centres as the day progresses: Sydney, Tokyo, Singapore, London and New York. London is the largest forex trading centre, followed by New York.

A Brief History of the Forex Market

  • Gold standard era: in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, many currencies were tied to gold, which kept exchange rates largely fixed.
  • Bretton Woods (1944): after the Second World War, major countries agreed to peg their currencies to the US dollar, which was convertible into gold at 35 dollars per ounce. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) and World Bank were created at the same conference.
  • 1971: US President Richard Nixon ended the dollar's convertibility into gold, and by 1973 most major currencies were floating against each other, creating the modern forex market.
  • 1990s–2000s: electronic trading platforms transformed the market, and the euro was introduced in 1999 as a major new currency.
  • India: after the 1991 balance of payments crisis, India moved from a fixed exchange rate toward a market-determined rate in 1993, alongside wider economic liberalisation.

Who Participates in the Forex Market?

  • Central banks, such as the Reserve Bank of India, which manage foreign exchange reserves and sometimes intervene to reduce excessive volatility in their currency.
  • Commercial banks, which trade for clients and on their own account and account for a large share of volume.
  • Companies that import, export or operate internationally and need to convert currencies or hedge exchange-rate risk.
  • Investment funds, pension funds and asset managers investing across countries.
  • Hedge funds and proprietary traders seeking profit from currency movements.
  • Retail traders, individuals trading currencies through brokers.
  • Travellers and individuals sending remittances or paying for overseas education and travel.

How Currency Pairs Work

Currencies are always traded in pairs. In a quote like EUR/USD = 1.0800:

  • EUR is the base currency.
  • USD is the quote currency.
  • The price means 1 euro costs 1.08 US dollars.

If you "buy" EUR/USD, you expect the euro to strengthen against the dollar. If you "sell", you expect it to weaken.

Key Forex Terms

  • Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another.
  • Pip: the smallest standard price move in a currency pair, usually the fourth decimal place for most pairs (0.0001), or the second for yen pairs.
  • Spread: the difference between the buying (ask) and selling (bid) price, a key trading cost.
  • Lot: a standard trading unit. A standard lot is 100,000 units of the base currency; mini and micro lots are smaller.
  • Leverage: borrowing from a broker to control a larger position than your own money allows.
  • Margin: the money you must deposit to open a leveraged position.
  • Margin call: a demand to add funds when losses reduce your margin below required levels.
  • Stop-loss: an order that automatically closes a position at a set price to limit losses.
  • Spot market: buying or selling currency for immediate delivery, usually settled within two business days.
  • Forwards and futures: contracts to exchange currencies at a set rate on a future date.
  • Options: contracts giving the right, but not the obligation, to exchange at a set rate.

What Moves Exchange Rates?

1. Interest Rates

Higher interest rates tend to attract foreign capital seeking better returns, increasing demand for a currency. Central bank decisions and expectations about future rates are among the biggest drivers of currency moves.

2. Inflation

Countries with persistently higher inflation tend to see their currencies lose value over time, as purchasing power declines.

3. Economic Growth and Data

Strong GDP growth, employment figures and business activity often support a currency. Traders watch data releases such as US jobs reports, inflation numbers and central bank statements closely.

4. Trade Balance and Current Account

Countries that import much more than they export need to buy foreign currency to pay for imports, which can weaken their own currency over time. India typically runs a current account deficit, partly due to large oil imports, which can put pressure on the rupee.

5. Capital Flows

Investment by foreign institutions into a country's stocks and bonds increases demand for its currency. Sudden outflows can cause sharp depreciation.

6. Political Stability and Risk Sentiment

Elections, conflicts and political uncertainty can cause volatility. During global crises, investors often move into perceived "safe haven" currencies such as the US dollar, Swiss franc and Japanese yen.

7. Commodity Prices

Currencies of commodity-exporting countries, such as the Australian dollar, Canadian dollar and Norwegian krone, often move with prices of their key exports. For India, a large oil importer, higher oil prices tend to weigh on the rupee.

8. Central Bank Intervention

Central banks sometimes buy or sell currencies to smooth volatility. The RBI holds large foreign exchange reserves, which it can use to manage sharp swings in the rupee.

Purchasing Power Parity and the Big Mac Index

One long-run theory of exchange rates is purchasing power parity (PPP): over time, exchange rates should adjust so that the same basket of goods costs roughly the same in different countries. In practice, exchange rates can differ from PPP for long periods because of trade barriers, capital flows and non-traded goods like housing and services.

The Economist magazine created the light-hearted Big Mac Index in 1986, comparing burger prices around the world to estimate whether currencies are over- or undervalued against the US dollar. It's a simplified illustration of PPP, not a trading tool, but it shows why currencies of lower-income countries, including the rupee, often appear "undervalued" by this measure: local costs, especially labour, are lower.

Exchange Rate Systems

  • Floating: rates are determined mainly by market forces, as with the US dollar, euro and Japanese yen.
  • Managed float: mostly market-driven, but the central bank intervenes to limit volatility. The Indian rupee is often described this way.
  • Fixed or pegged: the currency is tied to another currency or a basket. Several Gulf currencies, such as the UAE dirham and Saudi riyal, are pegged to the US dollar.

How Forex Trading Works

Suppose a trader believes the euro will rise against the US dollar. They buy EUR/USD at 1.0800. If the rate rises to 1.0900, they've gained 100 pips. On a standard lot of 100,000 euros, each pip is worth about 10 dollars, so the gain would be roughly 1,000 dollars before costs. If the rate falls to 1.0700 instead, they lose a similar amount.

The Double-Edged Sword of Leverage

Brokers in many countries offer leverage, allowing traders to control large positions with small deposits. With 30:1 leverage, a deposit of about 3,600 dollars could control a 108,000 dollar position. A 1% move in the exchange rate then becomes roughly a 30% gain or loss on the deposit. A few bad moves can wipe out an account entirely.

Because of these risks, regulators in many regions have restricted retail leverage. The European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA), for example, capped leverage for retail traders on major currency pairs at 30:1 in 2018, and requires brokers to disclose the percentage of retail accounts that lose money. These disclosures commonly show that the majority of retail accounts, often around 70% or more, lose money when trading CFDs, a common way retail traders speculate on currencies.

Fundamental vs Technical Analysis

Traders generally use two approaches to forecast currency moves:

  • Fundamental analysis studies economic factors: interest rates, inflation, growth, trade balances, central bank policy and politics. It's more common for longer-term views.
  • Technical analysis studies price charts, patterns and indicators, such as moving averages and support and resistance levels, on the assumption that past price behaviour can hint at future moves.

Many traders combine both. However, academic research suggests that short-term currency movements are very hard to predict consistently, which is one reason so many retail traders lose money after costs.

The Psychology of Trading

Trading tests emotions as much as knowledge. Common pitfalls include:

  • Overconfidence after a few winning trades, leading to larger, riskier positions.
  • Loss aversion: holding losing trades too long in the hope they'll recover.
  • Revenge trading: trying to win back losses quickly with bigger bets.
  • Fear of missing out on big moves seen on social media.

Strict rules for position size and maximum losses, along with a written trading plan, help reduce emotional decision-making.

Forex Trading in India: What's Legal?

India has strict rules on forex trading under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999, and regulations from the RBI and SEBI. Key points:

  • Permitted: trading currency derivatives (futures and options) on recognised Indian stock exchanges, such as NSE and BSE, through SEBI-registered brokers. Permitted contracts include pairs involving the rupee, such as USD/INR, EUR/INR, GBP/INR and JPY/INR, and certain cross-currency pairs like EUR/USD, GBP/USD and USD/JPY allowed on Indian exchanges.
  • Not permitted: Indian residents remitting money abroad, including through the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, for margin trading or speculative forex trading on overseas or online platforms.
  • Unauthorised platforms: the RBI maintains an Alert List of entities not authorised to deal in forex or operate electronic trading platforms for forex. It has repeatedly warned the public against such platforms, many of which advertise heavily on social media with promises of high returns.

People who deal with unauthorised platforms risk losing their money and may face legal consequences under FEMA. Always check the RBI's Alert List and confirm that a broker is registered with SEBI before trading.

Common Forex Scams to Avoid

  • Guaranteed high returns: no legitimate trading can guarantee profits.
  • Unregistered apps and websites promoted through social media, messaging groups or "mentors".
  • Signal-selling groups charging fees for "sure-shot" trade tips.
  • Managed account schemes where someone offers to trade on your behalf for a share of profits.
  • Withdrawal traps: platforms that show large profits but demand extra "taxes" or "fees" before allowing withdrawals.
  • Romance and friendship scams: online contacts who encourage you to invest through a particular platform.

If you suspect fraud in India, report it on the national cybercrime portal (cybercrime.gov.in) or by calling 1930.

Practical Uses of Forex for Ordinary People

Most people interact with forex not as traders but as users:

  • Travel: compare exchange rates and fees from banks, authorised money changers and forex cards. Airport counters often offer poorer rates.
  • International payments: fees and exchange rate markups vary widely. Check the total cost, not just the advertised fee.
  • Overseas education: fees paid in foreign currency can rise if the rupee weakens. Planning ahead and spreading conversions over time can reduce the impact.
  • Investing abroad: international mutual funds or US stocks expose you to currency movements as well as investment returns.
  • Receiving remittances: India is the world's largest recipient of remittances, according to the World Bank. Families can benefit from comparing transfer services.

How Businesses Manage Currency Risk

Exporters, importers and companies with foreign loans face currency risk. They manage it through hedging tools such as forward contracts (locking in a future exchange rate), currency options, and natural hedges (matching foreign-currency revenues with foreign-currency costs). Indian IT companies, which earn a large share of revenue in US dollars, routinely use these tools to stabilise earnings.

If You Still Want to Learn Trading

For those genuinely interested in currency markets:

  1. Learn the fundamentals of economics, interest rates and market structure.
  2. Use only regulated brokers and permitted products in your country.
  3. Practise with a demo account before risking real money.
  4. Use small position sizes and avoid high leverage.
  5. Always use stop-loss orders and decide your maximum loss in advance.
  6. Keep a trading journal to review decisions honestly.
  7. Never trade with money you need for living expenses, emergencies or important goals.
  8. Understand taxes on trading profits.

Remember that most long-term wealth is built through diversified investing, not short-term trading.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is forex trading legal in India?

Yes, but only in permitted currency derivative contracts traded on recognised Indian exchanges through SEBI-registered brokers. Trading on unauthorised online forex platforms or sending money abroad for leveraged forex trading is not permitted for residents.

Can you get rich from forex trading?

A small number of professional traders profit consistently, but data from regulated markets show most retail traders lose money. Treat claims of easy riches with great caution.

Why does the rupee usually weaken against the dollar over time?

Mainly because India has historically had higher inflation than the US and often runs a current account deficit. Over long periods, these factors tend to weaken the rupee relative to the dollar.

What is the best time to trade forex?

Liquidity is highest when major market sessions overlap, especially the London and New York overlap. For Indian exchange-traded currency derivatives, trading follows the exchange's set hours.

What is the difference between forex and stocks?

Stocks represent ownership in companies, which can grow and pay dividends over time. Forex involves exchanging one currency for another; currencies don't generate earnings, and trading them is mainly about relative value changes.

How do I check if a forex broker is legitimate in India?

Confirm that the broker is registered with SEBI and is a member of a recognised exchange such as NSE or BSE, and check that it does not appear on the RBI's Alert List of unauthorised forex platforms. Be wary of brokers contacting you through social media or messaging apps.

What is a forex card?

A forex card is a prepaid card loaded with foreign currency for travel. It can lock in an exchange rate at the time of loading and is often cheaper and safer than carrying cash or using a regular debit or credit card abroad.

Why do exchange rates differ between banks and websites?

Websites often show the mid-market rate, the midpoint between buying and selling prices. Banks and money changers add a margin and fees, so the rate you actually receive is usually less favourable.

What are the RBI's foreign exchange reserves used for?

Reserves help pay for imports, service foreign debt, maintain confidence in the economy and allow the RBI to smooth excessive volatility in the rupee.

What is a currency carry trade?

A carry trade involves borrowing in a currency with low interest rates and investing in one with higher rates to earn the difference. It can be profitable in calm markets but can lead to large losses if exchange rates move sharply.

Does a weaker rupee help or hurt India?

Both. A weaker rupee helps exporters and families receiving remittances, but makes imports such as oil, electronics and overseas education more expensive, which can add to inflation.

How are forex trading profits taxed in India?

Profits from currency derivatives on Indian exchanges are generally treated as business income and taxed at your applicable slab rate. Keep records and consult a tax professional.

Final Thoughts

The forex market is the backbone of international trade, travel and investment, and understanding how it works helps you make better decisions about travel money, overseas payments and global investing. As a trading arena, however, it's fast, complex and risky, especially with leverage. In India, legal trading is limited to permitted products on recognised exchanges, and unauthorised platforms are a common source of fraud. If you choose to trade, start small, stay within the law, manage risk carefully and never invest money you can't afford to lose.
