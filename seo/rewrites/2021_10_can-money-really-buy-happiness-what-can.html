Quick answer: Money can buy some happiness, but less than most people expect. Having enough money to cover basic needs, avoid constant financial stress and feel some control over your life makes a big difference to wellbeing. Research suggests life satisfaction keeps rising with income, though each extra rupee or dollar adds less, and how you spend money matters as much as how much you have. Spending on experiences, time-saving help, other people and financial security tends to bring more lasting happiness than buying more things. Relationships, health, purpose and gratitude matter even more.

"Money can't buy happiness" is one of the oldest sayings in the world. Yet most of us also know that worrying about rent, school fees or medical bills makes life miserable. So which is true?

The good news is that psychologists and economists have studied this question for decades. This guide explains what the research says about money and happiness, why more money often doesn't feel as good as expected, how to spend money in ways that genuinely increase wellbeing, and what else you can do to be happier whatever your income.

What Does "Happiness" Mean?

Researchers usually measure two kinds of happiness:

  • Emotional wellbeing: how you feel day to day, such as how often you feel joy, calm, stress, sadness or anger.
  • Life evaluation (life satisfaction): how you rate your life overall when you step back and think about it, for example on a scale from 0 to 10.

Money can affect these two in different ways. A higher income may make you feel your life is going well overall, while your daily mood depends more on things like relationships, health, sleep and how you spend your time.

What the Research Says

The Famous 75,000 Dollar Study

In 2010, Nobel Prize-winning psychologist Daniel Kahneman and economist Angus Deaton analysed survey data from hundreds of thousands of Americans. They found that life evaluation kept rising with income, but day-to-day emotional wellbeing appeared to level off at around 75,000 dollars a year. Above that amount, more money seemed to make people more satisfied with their lives but not much happier in the moment.

Newer Findings

In 2021, Matthew Killingsworth published research using real-time mood reports from a smartphone app. He found that emotional wellbeing kept rising with income well above 75,000 dollars, with no clear plateau. The disagreement led to an unusual "adversarial collaboration" between Killingsworth and Kahneman, published in 2023. Together, they concluded that for most people, happiness does keep rising with income. However, for the least happy group of people, around the bottom 20%, happiness rose with income up to about 100,000 dollars and then levelled off. In other words, more money helps most people, but it can't fix every source of unhappiness.

Diminishing Returns

Across almost all studies, the relationship between income and happiness follows a curve of diminishing returns. An extra 10,000 rupees a month transforms life for a family struggling to pay for food and rent. The same amount barely registers for someone earning lakhs a month. Researchers often find that happiness rises roughly in line with the percentage increase in income rather than the absolute amount: doubling your income from 30,000 to 60,000 rupees has a similar effect to doubling it from 3 lakh to 6 lakh.

Countries and Happiness

The annual World Happiness Report, based on Gallup World Poll data, consistently finds that wealthier countries tend to have higher average life satisfaction. But GDP isn't the only factor. Social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity and low corruption also explain much of the difference between countries. The Nordic countries such as Finland and Denmark regularly top the rankings, not just because they are wealthy, but because of strong social safety nets, trust and work-life balance.

Why More Money Often Doesn't Feel as Good as Expected

1. Hedonic Adaptation

Humans adapt quickly to positive changes. A new car, a bigger house or a salary increase feels exciting at first, but within months it becomes the new normal. Psychologists call this the "hedonic treadmill": you keep running toward the next upgrade, but your happiness returns to roughly where it was.

2. Social Comparison

We judge our income and possessions relative to others. Research has found that people's satisfaction depends partly on how their income compares with neighbours, colleagues and friends. If everyone around you also gets richer, your extra money may feel less meaningful. Social media makes comparison constant.

3. Rising Expectations

As income grows, so do expectations. Things that were once luxuries, such as eating out or flying for holidays, start to feel like necessities.

4. Trading Time for Money

Higher-paying jobs can come with longer hours, more stress and less time for family, friends, exercise and rest. If earning more means losing the things that make us happy, the net effect can be negative.

5. Focusing on Things Rather Than Experiences

Many people spend extra money on material possessions, which tend to bring shorter-lived happiness than experiences, relationships and personal growth.

How Money Can Increase Happiness

Research by psychologists Elizabeth Dunn and Michael Norton, summarised in their book Happy Money, suggests that how you spend matters as much as how much you have. Here are evidence-based ways to spend for happiness.

1. Buy Financial Security First

Financial stress is strongly linked to anxiety, poor sleep and relationship problems. Having an emergency fund, paying off high-interest debt and having insurance reduces worry dramatically. Studies have found that having savings is linked to life satisfaction independent of income. Peace of mind is one of the best things money can buy.

2. Buy Experiences

Research by Thomas Gilovich and Leaf Van Boven found that people generally get more lasting happiness from experiences, such as travel, concerts, classes and meals with friends, than from material goods. Experiences become part of our identity, are often shared with others, are less open to comparison and become better in memory over time.

3. Buy Time

A 2017 study by Ashley Whillans and colleagues found that people who spent money on time-saving services, such as paying for cleaning, cooking help or delivery to avoid disliked tasks, reported greater life satisfaction, across income levels. Buying back time for family, rest or hobbies is a powerful use of money.

4. Spend on Others

In experiments by Dunn, Norton and Lara Aknin, people who were asked to spend money on others reported feeling happier than those who spent it on themselves. Similar results have been found across many countries, including poorer ones. Giving gifts, treating friends or donating to causes you care about creates a "warm glow" that personal spending often doesn't.

5. Spend on Many Small Pleasures

Because we adapt to big purchases, frequent small treats, such as a favourite coffee with a friend, fresh flowers or a good book, may bring more total happiness than one large purchase.

6. Pay Now, Consume Later

Anticipation is part of the pleasure. Planning and paying for a holiday in advance lets you enjoy looking forward to it for weeks. Buying on credit reverses this, adding worry after the pleasure has passed.

7. Invest in Health and Growth

Spending on good food, exercise, healthcare, education and skills supports long-term wellbeing and future income.

When Money Hurts Happiness

  • Debt: high-interest debt, especially credit card and personal loans for lifestyle spending, causes significant stress.
  • Materialism: research by Tim Kasser and others found that people who place a very high value on wealth, image and possessions tend to report lower wellbeing and more anxiety.
  • Money conflicts: disagreements about money are among the most common sources of tension in relationships.
  • Overwork: chasing more income at the cost of health, sleep and relationships.
  • Sudden windfalls: studies of lottery winners show mixed results. Big wins improve financial satisfaction but don't always make people happier day to day, and some winners face strained relationships.

What Matters More Than Money

Relationships

The Harvard Study of Adult Development, one of the longest-running studies of human life, has followed participants since 1938. Its directors, Robert Waldinger and Marc Schulz, conclude that good relationships are the strongest predictor of long-term happiness and health, more so than wealth, fame or social class.

Health

Physical and mental health strongly affect daily wellbeing. Exercise, sleep, nutrition and managing stress improve mood regardless of income.

Purpose and Meaning

People who feel their lives have meaning, through work, family, faith, creativity or service, tend to be more satisfied and resilient.

Autonomy

Feeling in control of your time and choices is linked to happiness. One of the best things money can buy is freedom: the ability to say no to a bad job or to spend time on what matters.

Gratitude

Studies by Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough found that people who regularly wrote down things they were grateful for reported higher wellbeing than those who didn't.

Practical Ways to Be Happier, Whatever Your Income

  1. Build an emergency fund of three to six months of expenses to reduce money worries.
  2. Make a simple budget that reflects your values: what genuinely makes you happy, and what doesn't?
  3. Prioritise time with people you love. Schedule it like an important meeting.
  4. Move your body for at least 30 minutes most days.
  5. Protect your sleep.
  6. Practise gratitude by noting three good things each day.
  7. Help others through kindness, volunteering or giving.
  8. Limit comparison by reducing time on social media.
  9. Spend on experiences and time rather than more stuff.
  10. Find purpose through meaningful work, hobbies, learning or community.

A Happiness-Focused Budget

Most budgets focus on cutting spending. A happiness-focused budget also asks which spending actually improves your life. Try this exercise:

  1. List last month's spending in categories: housing, food, transport, shopping, eating out, subscriptions, travel, gifts, savings and so on.
  2. Rate each category from 1 to 10 for how much happiness or value it brought you.
  3. Cut ruthlessly where the score is low. Unused subscriptions, impulse shopping and expensive habits you barely enjoy are easy wins.
  4. Move that money to high-scoring categories, such as experiences with family, hobbies, health or savings for a meaningful goal.
  5. Automate savings first, so security grows without relying on willpower.

This approach isn't about spending less on everything. It's about spending more consciously, so your money reflects your values.

The Psychology of Wanting More

Advertising and social media are designed to make us feel that the next purchase will make us happier. Several psychological tendencies make this message persuasive:

  • Impact bias: research by Daniel Gilbert and Timothy Wilson found that people consistently overestimate how much happier a future purchase or event will make them, and for how long.
  • Focusing illusion: when we think about a single factor, like a higher salary or a bigger house, we exaggerate its importance to our overall happiness. Kahneman summarised this as "nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it."
  • Loss aversion: losing money feels worse than gaining the same amount feels good, so financial setbacks weigh heavily on wellbeing.

Simply knowing about these biases can help. Before a big purchase, ask yourself: "How will I feel about this in six months?" and "What else could this money do for me?"

Money, Freedom and "Enough" Time

One of the most valuable things money can provide is freedom: the ability to choose how you spend your time. Research on time poverty, the feeling of having too many things to do and not enough time, shows it is strongly linked to lower happiness, sometimes more strongly than lack of money. Ashley Whillans' research found that people who value time over money tend to be happier.

Practical ways to use money to buy time include living closer to work to cut commuting, paying for help with chores you dislike, and building savings that give you the option of a career break or a less stressful job. Long commutes in particular have been linked to lower life satisfaction in several studies.

Teaching Children About Money and Happiness

Children learn about money long before they earn it. Parents can help by:

  • Giving pocket money and letting children make small spending decisions, including mistakes.
  • Encouraging saving towards goals they care about.
  • Involving them in giving, such as donating toys or contributing to a cause.
  • Talking about the difference between needs and wants.
  • Valuing experiences and time together over expensive gifts.
  • Modelling calm, open conversations about money.

Money and Happiness in India

For many Indian families, money questions are bound up with family responsibilities: supporting parents, children's education, weddings and buying a home. These obligations can create pressure, but they also give money deep meaning. A few points are especially relevant:

  • Health insurance is crucial, since medical costs are a leading cause of financial stress and debt.
  • Avoid spending beyond your means on social expectations, such as expensive weddings funded by loans.
  • Joint family support can be a source of both financial security and happiness when expectations are clear.
  • Long-term investing, such as SIPs in diversified mutual funds and retirement savings, builds future security and reduces anxiety.

How Much Money Is "Enough"?

There's no universal number, because it depends on where you live, your family size and your goals. A practical way to think about "enough" is having:

  • Basic needs comfortably met: food, housing, healthcare and education.
  • An emergency fund and adequate insurance.
  • No high-interest debt.
  • Steady progress toward retirement and other long-term goals.
  • Some money to spend on experiences and people you care about.

Once you reach this point, extra money still helps, but other parts of life become far more important for happiness. Many people find that defining "enough" frees them from endlessly chasing more.

Money Habits of Financially Happy People

Surveys of financial wellbeing, such as those by the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, suggest that feeling in control of your finances matters as much as income. People with high financial wellbeing tend to:

  • Spend less than they earn, consistently.
  • Have savings to handle unexpected expenses.
  • Pay bills on time and avoid high-interest debt.
  • Have clear financial goals and a plan to reach them.
  • Talk openly about money with their partner.
  • Don't feel the need to keep up with others' spending.
  • Review their finances regularly but don't obsess over them.

None of these habits require a high income. They require attention, planning and a little discipline, and they reduce money-related stress at every income level.

Real-Life Example

Rahul, a software engineer, received a large promotion and immediately upgraded to a bigger apartment and a luxury car on loan. Within a year, the excitement had faded, his EMIs had grown, and his longer hours left little time for his family. He felt more stressed than before. After talking with his wife, they cut back on new purchases, built an emergency fund, started weekly family outings and hired help for household chores to free up weekends. Their income didn't change, but they reported feeling much happier. The difference was how they used their money and time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does money buy happiness?

To an extent, yes. More money is linked to higher life satisfaction and, for most people, better daily mood. But the effect weakens as income rises, and how you spend money and time matters greatly.

What is the happiest way to spend money?

Research points to financial security, experiences, time-saving services and spending on others as the most reliably happiness-boosting uses of money.

Why are some rich people unhappy?

Wealth can't fix poor relationships, health problems, lack of purpose or mental health conditions. Some wealthy people also suffer from constant comparison, overwork or isolation.

Can you be happy without much money?

Yes, especially when basic needs are met. Many people with modest incomes report high life satisfaction thanks to close relationships, purpose, community and good health. However, poverty and financial insecurity do significantly harm wellbeing.

Does winning the lottery make people happier?

Studies show lottery winners usually become more satisfied with their finances and lives overall, but their day-to-day happiness doesn't always improve much, and the effect depends on how they use the money.

Is it selfish to want more money?

Not at all. Wanting financial security for yourself and your family is healthy. Problems arise when money becomes the main measure of self-worth or crowds out relationships and health.

Should I take a higher-paying job I'll enjoy less?

Consider the full picture: extra income, hours, stress, commute, growth opportunities and how the role fits your values. If the extra money would significantly improve your financial security, it may be worth it. If you're already comfortable, a job you enjoy with reasonable hours may add more to your happiness than a pay rise.

Does giving to charity really make you happier?

Studies across many countries suggest it does. People report a boost in happiness after spending on others, and the effect is often stronger when they can see the impact of their giving or feel connected to the cause.

Is it better to save or spend for happiness?

Both matter. Saving builds security and reduces anxiety, which is a major source of wellbeing. Spending thoughtfully on experiences, time and other people adds joy. A balanced plan does both: automate savings first, then spend the rest intentionally.

Do people get happier as they get richer over a lifetime?

Not necessarily. Rising incomes are often accompanied by rising expectations and spending. People who keep their lifestyle modest as income grows, and direct extra money to security, freedom and meaningful experiences, tend to feel the benefits more.

Final Thoughts

Money is a powerful tool for happiness when it provides security, freedom, experiences and the ability to help others. But it's not a replacement for relationships, health, purpose and gratitude. The happiest approach is to earn enough to feel secure, spend wisely on what genuinely matters to you, and invest just as much in the people and activities that make life rich in every sense.
