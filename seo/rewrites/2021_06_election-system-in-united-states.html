Quick answer: The United States elects its President every four years through the Electoral College, not by a direct national popular vote. Each state gets electoral votes equal to its number of members of Congress, and in 48 states the candidate who wins the state's popular vote takes all of its electors. A candidate needs 270 of the 538 electoral votes to win. Members of Congress are elected directly: the whole House of Representatives every two years and one-third of the Senate every two years for six-year terms.

American elections can seem confusing from the outside. There are primaries and caucuses, a general election in November, an Electoral College that meets in December, and a Congress that certifies the result in January. Each state also runs its own elections with its own rules for registration, voting by mail and counting.

This guide walks through the whole system in plain language: who runs elections, how candidates are chosen, how the Electoral College works and how Congress is elected.

Who Runs Elections in the US?

Unlike many countries, the US has no single national election authority that runs voting. The Constitution gives states the main responsibility for running elections, including federal ones. In practice:

  • States set rules on voter registration, voter ID, early voting, mail-in voting and ballot design, usually through a Secretary of State or state election board.
  • Counties and cities run polling places, count ballots and certify local results.
  • Federal law sets some national standards, such as protections under the Voting Rights Act and accessibility requirements, and the Federal Election Commission (FEC) regulates campaign finance for federal races.

This decentralised approach means voting can look quite different from one state to another.

Who Can Vote?

To vote in federal elections, you must be a US citizen, at least 18 years old on Election Day and registered to vote (except in North Dakota, which has no registration). States add their own rules, such as residency requirements and, in some states, restrictions on voting for people with felony convictions. Registration deadlines and ID requirements differ by state, so check your state's official election website well before each election.

How Presidential Candidates Are Chosen: Primaries and Caucuses

Before the general election, each political party chooses its nominee through a series of state contests held mostly between January and June of the election year.

  • Primaries are state-run elections where voters choose their preferred candidate. Some are "closed" (only registered party members vote) and some are "open" (any registered voter can participate).
  • Caucuses are party-run meetings where participants discuss and vote, sometimes in rounds. Fewer states use caucuses today than in the past.
  • Results award delegates to each candidate. At the party's national convention in the summer, delegates formally nominate the presidential candidate, who also announces a running mate for Vice President.

The General Election

The general election is held on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. Many states also offer early in-person voting and voting by mail, so a large share of votes are often cast before Election Day itself.

Voters choose between the party nominees and any independent or third-party candidates who qualified for the ballot in their state. At the same time, they usually vote for members of Congress, state officials, local offices and sometimes ballot measures.

How the Electoral College Works

When Americans vote for President, they are technically choosing a slate of electors pledged to their candidate. Those electors then cast the official votes for President.

The Key Numbers

  • There are 538 electors in total: 435 for House seats, 100 for Senate seats and 3 for Washington, D.C.
  • Each state gets electors equal to its House members plus its two Senators, so every state has at least three.
  • A candidate needs a majority, 270 electoral votes, to win.
  • Seats are reallocated between states after each ten-year census, so electoral vote counts can change.

Winner Takes All

In 48 states and D.C., the candidate who wins the most votes in the state receives all of its electoral votes. Maine and Nebraska instead award two electors to the statewide winner and one to the winner of each congressional district.

Because of this system, campaigns focus heavily on "swing states" where the result is close, rather than on states that reliably vote for one party.

Can You Win the Popular Vote and Lose?

Yes. Because electoral votes are awarded state by state, a candidate can win the most votes nationwide and still lose the Electoral College. This happened in 2000 and 2016, as well as several times in the 1800s.

What Happens After Election Day

  1. States count ballots, resolve any disputes and certify their results.
  2. In mid-December, electors meet in their state capitals to cast their votes.
  3. On January 6, Congress meets in a joint session to count the electoral votes and confirm the winner.
  4. The new President is inaugurated on January 20.

If no candidate reaches 270, the House of Representatives chooses the President, with each state delegation casting one vote. This has not happened since 1824.

How Congress Is Elected

Congress has two chambers, and both are elected directly by voters.

  • House of Representatives: 435 members, each representing a district of roughly equal population. All seats are up for election every two years. Most states use single-member districts and plurality voting, although Maine and Alaska use ranked-choice voting in some races.
  • Senate: 100 members, two per state regardless of population. Senators serve six-year terms, staggered so about one-third of seats are elected every two years.

Elections held halfway through a presidential term are called midterm elections. They often reshape the balance of power in Congress.

Redistricting and Gerrymandering

After each census, states redraw House district boundaries. When districts are drawn to favour one party, it is called gerrymandering. Some states now use independent or bipartisan commissions to reduce this.

State and Local Elections

Americans also elect governors, state legislators, mayors, city councils, school boards, sheriffs and, in many states, judges and prosecutors. Many states allow citizens to vote directly on laws or constitutional changes through ballot initiatives and referendums.

Strengths and Criticisms of the System

Arguments in Favour

  • The Electoral College encourages candidates to build support across many states, not just big cities.
  • State-run elections make large-scale fraud or hacking harder because there is no single system to attack.
  • Frequent elections keep representatives accountable.

Common Criticisms

  • The winner of the national popular vote can lose the election.
  • Campaigns ignore safe states and focus on a handful of swing states.
  • Rules vary widely between states, which can confuse voters.
  • Gerrymandering and high campaign spending can distort representation.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is a US President's term?

Four years. Under the 22nd Amendment, a President can be elected no more than twice.

Is voting compulsory in the US?

No. Voting is voluntary, and turnout in presidential elections has typically ranged between about 50% and 67% of eligible voters in recent decades.

Can electors vote for someone else?

Very rarely. Many states legally bind electors to their pledged candidate, and the Supreme Court ruled in 2020 that states can enforce these pledges.

Why is Election Day on a Tuesday?

Congress set the date in 1845, when the US was largely agricultural. Tuesday gave rural voters time to travel to polling places without interfering with Sunday worship or Wednesday market days.

Who counts the votes?

Local election officials, usually at the county level, count votes under state law. Results are reviewed and certified by state officials before being sent to Congress.

Final Thoughts

The US election system reflects the country's federal design: states run elections, the Electoral College balances population with statehood, and frequent elections keep power changing hands. It is complex and often debated, but understanding the basic pieces, primaries, the general election, the Electoral College and Congress, makes American politics much easier to follow.
