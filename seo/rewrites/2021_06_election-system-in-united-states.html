Quick answer: The United States elects its President every four years through the Electoral College, not by a direct national popular vote. Each state gets electoral votes equal to its number of members of Congress, and in 48 states the candidate who wins the state's popular vote takes all of its electors. A candidate needs 270 of the 538 electoral votes to win. Members of Congress are elected directly: the whole House of Representatives every two years and one-third of the Senate every two years for six-year terms.

American elections can seem confusing from the outside. There are primaries and caucuses, a general election in November, an Electoral College that meets in December, and a Congress that certifies the result in January. Each state also runs its own elections with its own rules for registration, voting by mail and counting.

This guide walks through the whole system in plain language: who runs elections, how candidates are chosen, how the Electoral College works and how Congress is elected.

Who Runs Elections in the US?

Unlike many countries, the US has no single national election authority that runs voting. The Constitution gives states the main responsibility for running elections, including federal ones. In practice:

  • States set rules on voter registration, voter ID, early voting, mail-in voting and ballot design, usually through a Secretary of State or state election board.
  • Counties and cities run polling places, count ballots and certify local results.
  • Federal law sets some national standards, such as protections under the Voting Rights Act and accessibility requirements, and the Federal Election Commission (FEC) regulates campaign finance for federal races.

This decentralised approach means voting can look quite different from one state to another.

Who Can Vote?

To vote in federal elections, you must be a US citizen, at least 18 years old on Election Day and registered to vote (except in North Dakota, which has no registration). States add their own rules, such as residency requirements and, in some states, restrictions on voting for people with felony convictions. Registration deadlines and ID requirements differ by state, so check your state's official election website well before each election.

How Presidential Candidates Are Chosen: Primaries and Caucuses

Before the general election, each political party chooses its nominee through a series of state contests held mostly between January and June of the election year.

  • Primaries are state-run elections where voters choose their preferred candidate. Some are "closed" (only registered party members vote) and some are "open" (any registered voter can participate).
  • Caucuses are party-run meetings where participants discuss and vote, sometimes in rounds. Fewer states use caucuses today than in the past.
  • Results award delegates to each candidate. At the party's national convention in the summer, delegates formally nominate the presidential candidate, who also announces a running mate for Vice President.

The General Election

The general election is held on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. Many states also offer early in-person voting and voting by mail, so a large share of votes are often cast before Election Day itself.

Voters choose between the party nominees and any independent or third-party candidates who qualified for the ballot in their state. At the same time, they usually vote for members of Congress, state officials, local offices and sometimes ballot measures.

How the Electoral College Works

When Americans vote for President, they are technically choosing a slate of electors pledged to their candidate. Those electors then cast the official votes for President.

The Key Numbers

  • There are 538 electors in total: 435 for House seats, 100 for Senate seats and 3 for Washington, D.C.
  • Each state gets electors equal to its House members plus its two Senators, so every state has at least three.
  • A candidate needs a majority, 270 electoral votes, to win.
  • Seats are reallocated between states after each ten-year census, so electoral vote counts can change.

Winner Takes All

In 48 states and D.C., the candidate who wins the most votes in the state receives all of its electoral votes. Maine and Nebraska instead award two electors to the statewide winner and one to the winner of each congressional district.

Because of this system, campaigns focus heavily on "swing states" where the result is close, rather than on states that reliably vote for one party.

Can You Win the Popular Vote and Lose?

Yes. Because electoral votes are awarded state by state, a candidate can win the most votes nationwide and still lose the Electoral College. This happened in 2000 and 2016, as well as several times in the 1800s.

What Happens After Election Day

  1. States count ballots, resolve any disputes and certify their results.
  2. In mid-December, electors meet in their state capitals to cast their votes.
  3. On January 6, Congress meets in a joint session to count the electoral votes and confirm the winner.
  4. The new President is inaugurated on January 20.

If no candidate reaches 270, the House of Representatives chooses the President, with each state delegation casting one vote. This has not happened since 1824.

How Congress Is Elected

Congress has two chambers, and both are elected directly by voters.

  • House of Representatives: 435 members, each representing a district of roughly equal population. All seats are up for election every two years. Most states use single-member districts and plurality voting, although Maine and Alaska use ranked-choice voting in some races.
  • Senate: 100 members, two per state regardless of population. Senators serve six-year terms, staggered so about one-third of seats are elected every two years.

Elections held halfway through a presidential term are called midterm elections. They often reshape the balance of power in Congress.

Redistricting and Gerrymandering

After each census, states redraw House district boundaries. When districts are drawn to favour one party, it is called gerrymandering. Some states now use independent or bipartisan commissions to reduce this.

How Americans Vote: Ways to Cast a Ballot

One reason US elections can look complicated is that there are several ways to vote, and the options differ by state.

  • In person on Election Day: voters go to an assigned polling place, check in with poll workers, show identification if their state requires it and mark a paper ballot or use a voting machine.
  • Early in-person voting: most states let voters cast ballots in person for days or weeks before Election Day at designated locations.
  • Mail-in or absentee voting: every state allows some voters to vote by mail. Some states, such as Colorado, Oregon, Washington and Utah, mail a ballot to every registered voter automatically; others require voters to request one, and some require a reason.
  • Military and overseas voting: under the Uniformed and Overseas Citizens Absentee Voting Act (UOCAVA), service members and citizens living abroad can register and vote absentee.
  • Provisional ballots: if there is a question about a voter's eligibility at the polls, they can cast a provisional ballot that is counted once eligibility is confirmed.

Voter ID Rules

About three dozen states have laws requesting or requiring voters to show some form of identification at the polls. Some accept only photo ID, while others accept documents such as a utility bill or bank statement. The remaining states verify identity in other ways, such as signature matching. Supporters argue ID laws protect against fraud; critics argue they can make voting harder for some eligible citizens. Always check your state's current rules before you vote.

How Votes Are Counted and Certified

Counting can take days or even weeks in close races, which sometimes surprises people used to quick results.

  1. Election night tallies: counties report unofficial results as ballots are counted. Media organisations project winners based on these numbers and statistical models; projections are not official results.
  2. Processing mail ballots: some states let officials start processing mail ballots before Election Day, while others don't allow it until polls open or close, which can delay results.
  3. Canvass: local officials review and reconcile all ballots, including provisional and late-arriving military ballots where allowed.
  4. Audits: many states conduct post-election audits, comparing a sample of paper ballots with machine counts.
  5. Recounts: if a race is very close, state law may trigger an automatic recount or allow a candidate to request one.
  6. Certification: county and then state officials formally certify the results by deadlines set in state law.

Because most states use paper ballots or machines that create a paper record, results can be checked against physical evidence.

The Role of Political Parties

The US has a strong two-party system dominated by the Democratic Party and the Republican Party. Several features of the system make it hard for third parties to win:

  • Winner-take-all elections: with single-member districts and plurality voting, a third-party candidate who wins 15% of the vote everywhere may still win no seats. Political scientists call this tendency Duverger's law.
  • Ballot access rules: new or small parties often need to collect large numbers of signatures in each state to appear on the ballot.
  • Debate thresholds: presidential debates require candidates to reach a polling threshold, which few third-party candidates meet.
  • "Spoiler" concerns: voters may avoid supporting third-party candidates for fear of helping the major candidate they like least.

Despite these barriers, third-party and independent candidates have influenced several elections, and independents have won seats in Congress and governorships.

Campaign Finance in the United States

American campaigns are among the most expensive in the world, with presidential and congressional elections together costing many billions of dollars in recent cycles.

  • Contribution limits: federal law limits how much individuals can give directly to a candidate per election. The limits are adjusted for inflation every two years.
  • Political action committees (PACs): groups that raise and spend money to support or oppose candidates, subject to contribution limits.
  • Super PACs: after the Supreme Court's 2010 decision in Citizens United v. FEC and related rulings, groups that don't coordinate directly with campaigns can raise and spend unlimited amounts.
  • Disclosure: candidates, parties and PACs must report donors and spending to the Federal Election Commission, though some nonprofit groups can spend on political advertising without disclosing all their donors.

The Electoral College in Practice: A Worked Example

Imagine a simplified country with three states:

  • State A (10 electoral votes): Candidate X wins 51% to 49%.
  • State B (8 electoral votes): Candidate X wins 52% to 48%.
  • State C (12 electoral votes): Candidate Y wins 70% to 30%.

Candidate X wins States A and B and receives 18 electoral votes. Candidate Y wins only State C and receives 12. Candidate X wins the election, even though Candidate Y may have received more votes overall because of a huge margin in State C. This simple example shows how winning many states narrowly can beat winning a few states by large margins, and why swing states attract so much attention.

Key Laws and Amendments That Shaped US Voting

The right to vote in the United States has expanded gradually over more than two centuries. At the founding, voting was largely limited to white men who owned property. These changes opened it up:

  • 15th Amendment (1870): prohibited denying the vote based on race. In practice, many Southern states used poll taxes, literacy tests and intimidation to block Black citizens from voting for nearly a century afterwards.
  • 17th Amendment (1913): required senators to be elected directly by voters instead of by state legislatures.
  • 19th Amendment (1920): guaranteed women the right to vote.
  • 24th Amendment (1964): banned poll taxes in federal elections.
  • Voting Rights Act (1965): outlawed discriminatory practices such as literacy tests and required federal approval of voting changes in areas with a history of discrimination. The Supreme Court's 2013 decision in Shelby County v. Holder ended that pre-approval requirement.
  • 26th Amendment (1971): lowered the voting age from 21 to 18.
  • National Voter Registration Act (1993): the "motor voter" law, letting people register when applying for a driver's licence.
  • Help America Vote Act (2002): passed after the disputed 2000 election, it set standards for voting equipment, provisional ballots and statewide voter registration databases.

The 2000 election between George W. Bush and Al Gore, decided by a few hundred votes in Florida and ultimately by the Supreme Court's ruling in Bush v. Gore, led to major reforms in how Americans vote and count ballots. The Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022 later clarified the rules Congress follows when counting electoral votes.

A Timeline of a Presidential Election Cycle

A US presidential election is a marathon that can last nearly two years. Here's how a typical cycle unfolds.

  • 18 to 24 months before Election Day: candidates form exploratory committees, start fundraising and announce their campaigns.
  • The year before the election: candidates campaign in early states, raise money and take part in party debates.
  • January to June of the election year: primaries and caucuses are held state by state. Iowa and New Hampshire have traditionally gone first, although the parties have reshuffled their early calendars in recent cycles.
  • Super Tuesday (usually early March): many states vote on the same day, often deciding the likely nominee.
  • July to August: national party conventions formally nominate the presidential and vice-presidential candidates.
  • September to October: general election debates, intense campaigning in swing states and early voting begins in many states.
  • Early November: Election Day.
  • November to December: counting, certification and the meeting of the electors.
  • January 6: Congress counts the electoral votes.
  • January 20: Inauguration Day.

A key difference is that Americans vote separately for the executive (President) and the legislature (Congress). This makes "divided government", where one party holds the White House and another controls one or both houses of Congress, quite common, and it encourages negotiation but can also lead to gridlock.

Common Myths About US Elections

  • "Mail-in ballots are only counted if the race is close." False. All valid ballots are counted, whether or not they affect the outcome.
  • "The President is elected by the popular vote." Not directly; the Electoral College decides, although the popular vote in each state determines its electors.
  • "Election results are final on election night." Media projections are not official. Results become final only after certification.
  • "Congress chooses the President." Congress counts and confirms electoral votes; it chooses only if no candidate has a majority.

How to Get Involved

  • Check your registration well before deadlines on your state election website or vote.gov.
  • Research candidates and ballot measures using nonpartisan voter guides.
  • Sign up to be a poll worker; many counties need volunteers every election.
  • Vote in primaries and local elections, where turnout is often low and each vote carries more weight.

State and Local Elections

Americans also elect governors, state legislators, mayors, city councils, school boards, sheriffs and, in many states, judges and prosecutors. Many states allow citizens to vote directly on laws or constitutional changes through ballot initiatives and referendums.

Strengths and Criticisms of the System

Arguments in Favour

  • The Electoral College encourages candidates to build support across many states, not just big cities.
  • State-run elections make large-scale fraud or hacking harder because there is no single system to attack.
  • Frequent elections keep representatives accountable.

Common Criticisms

  • The winner of the national popular vote can lose the election.
  • Campaigns ignore safe states and focus on a handful of swing states.
  • Rules vary widely between states, which can confuse voters.
  • Gerrymandering and high campaign spending can distort representation.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is a US President's term?

Four years. Under the 22nd Amendment, a President can be elected no more than twice.

Is voting compulsory in the US?

No. Voting is voluntary, and turnout in presidential elections has typically ranged between about 50% and 67% of eligible voters in recent decades.

Can electors vote for someone else?

Very rarely. Many states legally bind electors to their pledged candidate, and the Supreme Court ruled in 2020 that states can enforce these pledges.

Why is Election Day on a Tuesday?

Congress set the date in 1845, when the US was largely agricultural. Tuesday gave rural voters time to travel to polling places without interfering with Sunday worship or Wednesday market days.

Who counts the votes?

Local election officials, usually at the county level, count votes under state law. Results are reviewed and certified by state officials before being sent to Congress.

Can non-citizens vote in US elections?

No. Only US citizens can vote in federal elections. A small number of municipalities allow non-citizen residents to vote in some local elections.

What is a swing state?

A swing state, or battleground state, is one where both major parties have a realistic chance of winning. In recent elections, states such as Pennsylvania, Michigan, Wisconsin, Georgia, Arizona, Nevada and North Carolina have been among the most closely contested.

Why don't Americans just abolish the Electoral College?

Abolishing it would require a constitutional amendment, which needs approval from two-thirds of both houses of Congress and three-quarters of the states. Smaller states, which benefit from the current system, are unlikely to agree. An alternative proposal, the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact, would have states award their electors to the national popular vote winner once states with 270 electoral votes join.

Final Thoughts

The US election system reflects the country's federal design: states run elections, the Electoral College balances population with statehood, and frequent elections keep power changing hands. It is complex and often debated, but understanding the basic pieces, primaries, the general election, the Electoral College and Congress, makes American politics much easier to follow.
