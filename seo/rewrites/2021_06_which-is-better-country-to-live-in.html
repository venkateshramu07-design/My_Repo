Quick answer: Both Australia and Canada offer high living standards, public healthcare, good education and multicultural societies. Australia suits people who want a warm climate, beach and outdoor lifestyle and generally higher wages. Canada suits people who don't mind cold winters and want clearer pathways to permanent residency and citizenship, proximity to the US and somewhat lower costs outside its biggest cities. The better choice depends on your career, climate preferences, budget and visa options.

Australia and Canada regularly appear near the top of global rankings for quality of life, safety and opportunity. Both are Commonwealth countries with English as a main language, strong economies and welcoming attitudes to skilled migrants. Yet day-to-day life in the two countries feels very different.

This comparison covers climate, jobs and salaries, cost of living, healthcare, education, immigration, culture and lifestyle to help you decide which might suit you better.

1. Climate and Environment

Australia is known for sunshine. Sydney, Brisbane and Perth enjoy warm summers and mild winters; northern areas are tropical, and the interior is desert. The downside is extreme heat, bushfires, droughts and high UV levels.

Canada has dramatic seasons. Vancouver and Victoria are relatively mild and rainy, but most of the country, including Toronto, Montreal, Calgary and Ottawa, has long, snowy winters with temperatures well below freezing. Summers are pleasant and the autumn colours are spectacular.

Choose Australia if you love warmth and beaches. Choose Canada if you enjoy snow sports and distinct seasons.

2. Jobs and Salaries

Both economies are strong and diverse. Australia has major mining, education, healthcare, finance and tourism sectors. Canada is strong in natural resources, technology, finance, manufacturing and healthcare.

  • Average and minimum wages tend to be higher in Australia.
  • Canada's tech sector, especially in Toronto, Vancouver and Montreal, benefits from proximity to the US.
  • Both countries have shortages in healthcare, trades, engineering, IT and education, which often have favourable visa pathways.
  • Professional qualifications may need to be recognised by local bodies in both countries.

3. Cost of Living

Both countries are expensive, especially for housing in major cities. Sydney, Melbourne, Toronto and Vancouver are among the least affordable housing markets in the world relative to local incomes.

  • Housing: both face housing shortages. Smaller cities, such as Adelaide or Brisbane in Australia and Calgary, Edmonton or Halifax in Canada, are typically more affordable.
  • Groceries and dining: broadly similar, with variations by city.
  • Utilities: heating can be a significant cost in Canadian winters; air conditioning can be costly in Australian summers.
  • Transport: outside central areas, a car is usually needed in both countries.

4. Healthcare

Australia's Medicare provides free or subsidised treatment in public hospitals and subsidised GP visits and medicines. Many residents also buy private health insurance for shorter waits and extra services, and higher earners may pay a tax surcharge if they don't.

Canada's public healthcare is run by each province and covers medically necessary hospital and doctor services. Wait times for specialists and non-urgent procedures can be long, and dental care and prescription drugs are only partly covered, although public dental coverage has been expanding.

Both systems are good by international standards. New migrants should check waiting periods and eligibility rules.

5. Education

Both countries offer free public schooling and world-class universities. Australia's top universities include the University of Melbourne, the University of Sydney and ANU. Canada's include the University of Toronto, McGill and the University of British Columbia. Both are popular with international students, although both governments have tightened international student numbers and visa rules in recent years.

6. Immigration and Citizenship

Canada is often seen as more accessible for skilled migrants. Its Express Entry system and Provincial Nominee Programs offer clear pathways to permanent residence, and permanent residents can usually apply for citizenship after living in Canada for three of the previous five years.

Australia uses a points-based skilled migration system with employer-sponsored, state-nominated and independent visas. Permanent residents can generally apply for citizenship after four years of residence, including one year as a permanent resident.

Immigration rules and quotas change frequently, so always check official government websites before making plans.

How the Points Systems Work

Both countries score skilled migration applicants on similar factors, though the exact points differ:

  • Age: younger applicants, roughly 25 to 32, typically score highest.
  • English (and French for Canada): results from approved tests such as IELTS, PTE (Australia), CELPIP (Canada) or TEF/TCF for French. Higher scores add significant points.
  • Education: degrees, especially postgraduate ones, add points. Foreign qualifications usually need an official assessment, such as Canada's Educational Credential Assessment (ECA).
  • Work experience: skilled experience in your nominated occupation, both overseas and locally.
  • Other factors: a partner's skills, study in the country, regional study, job offers and state or provincial nomination.

Canada runs regular Express Entry draws inviting the highest-scoring candidates, sometimes targeted at specific occupations or French speakers. Australia invites applicants through its SkillSelect system based on occupation lists and points.

Healthcare Access for Newcomers

New permanent residents in Australia can usually enrol in Medicare soon after arrival, while some Canadian provinces have waiting periods of up to three months before public coverage begins. Temporary residents, such as students and workers on certain visas, often need private health insurance in both countries. Arrange cover before you travel.

7. Culture and Lifestyle

Australians are known for a relaxed, outdoor lifestyle built around beaches, sport, barbecues and a strong work-life balance culture.

Canadians are known for politeness, a love of nature and winter sports, and a strongly multicultural identity. Quebec adds a distinctive French-speaking culture.

Both countries are highly multicultural, with large immigrant communities from India, China, the Philippines and many other countries.

8. Safety

Both countries are very safe by global standards, with low violent crime rates and strict gun laws. Australia has dangerous wildlife and sun exposure to respect; Canada has harsh winter conditions to prepare for.

9. Location and Travel

Australia is geographically isolated, so international travel is long and expensive, though Southeast Asia and New Zealand are relatively close. Canada borders the United States, making travel and business connections with the US easy, and Europe is closer than from Australia.

10. Taxes

Both countries have progressive income tax systems, meaning higher earners pay higher rates.

  • Australia: income tax rates rise in brackets up to a top rate of 45% for the highest earners, plus a Medicare levy of 2% for most residents. A Goods and Services Tax (GST) of 10% applies to most purchases. Employers must also pay superannuation (retirement) contributions on top of salaries, at 12% from July 2025.
  • Canada: residents pay both federal and provincial income tax. Combined top rates vary by province, from around 44% to over 54%. Sales taxes combine a 5% federal GST with provincial taxes in many provinces, or a single Harmonized Sales Tax (HST) of up to 15%.

Overall tax burdens are broadly similar, but the details depend heavily on your income, province or state and family situation.

11. Retirement Systems

  • Australia: compulsory employer superannuation builds retirement savings automatically, supplemented by a means-tested Age Pension.
  • Canada: the Canada Pension Plan (CPP), or the Quebec Pension Plan in Quebec, is funded by worker and employer contributions, plus Old Age Security (OAS) for most seniors. Individuals can also save in RRSPs and TFSAs.

12. Natural Beauty and Outdoor Life

Australia offers the Great Barrier Reef, Uluru, the Blue Mountains, endless beaches, rainforests and unique wildlife such as kangaroos and koalas. Outdoor life revolves around surfing, swimming, hiking and coastal walks.

Canada offers the Rocky Mountains, Banff and Jasper national parks, Niagara Falls, countless lakes and vast forests. Outdoor life includes skiing, snowboarding, canoeing, hiking and ice hockey. It's also one of the best places in the world to see the northern lights.

13. Getting Around

Both countries are huge, and distances between cities are long, so domestic flights are common. Most Australians live along the coast, especially in the southeast, while most Canadians live within a few hundred kilometres of the US border. Big cities in both countries have public transport systems, but many suburban and rural areas depend on cars.

14. Settling In as a Newcomer

  • Finding work: local experience is highly valued in both countries. Many migrants take a step down at first, then progress quickly. Networking, volunteering and getting local certifications help.
  • Credentials: regulated professions such as medicine, nursing, engineering, law and teaching require registration with local bodies, which can take time and money.
  • Community: both countries have large Indian, Chinese, Filipino and other diaspora communities, with cultural associations, places of worship and familiar food.
  • Housing: rental markets in major cities are competitive. New arrivals often need references, proof of income and a deposit.
  • Driving: you may need to convert your licence or take local tests within a set time after arrival.

Which Country Should You Choose?

Australia may suit you better if you:

  • Prefer warm weather and outdoor, beach-oriented living.
  • Work in a field with high Australian demand and wages.
  • Value a laid-back culture and strong work-life balance.

Canada may suit you better if you:

  • Want a well-established path to permanent residency and citizenship.
  • Enjoy seasons, snow and nature.
  • Want proximity to the US job market and easier travel to Europe.
  • Speak French or want a bilingual environment.

15. Work Culture and Employment Rights

Australia has one of the strongest systems of worker protections in the world. Minimum wages and conditions for many industries are set by "awards" overseen by the Fair Work Commission. Full-time employees receive at least four weeks of paid annual leave, plus personal leave, and many get long service leave after years with the same employer. A "right to disconnect" law introduced in 2024 lets employees refuse unreasonable after-hours contact.

Canada sets employment standards mainly at the provincial level. Paid vacation typically starts at two weeks per year, rising with years of service, plus statutory holidays. Workplace culture is generally polite, collaborative and fairly formal, with growing flexibility around hybrid work.

In both countries, punctuality, direct but respectful communication and work-life balance are valued. Australians are often described as more informal and laid-back at work; Canadians as polite and consensus-seeking.

16. Family Life and Childcare

  • Australia: offers government-funded paid parental leave, which has been expanding toward 26 weeks, and childcare subsidies that reduce costs based on family income. Public schools are free for citizens and permanent residents, though some temporary visa holders may pay fees.
  • Canada: offers Employment Insurance maternity and parental benefits of up to around 12 to 18 months, the tax-free Canada Child Benefit for families, and a national plan to reduce regulated childcare fees to an average of about $10 per day, rolling out across provinces. Public schooling is free.

Both countries are considered excellent places to raise children, with safe communities, good schools and access to nature.

17. Sports and Leisure

Sport is a big part of life in both countries. Australians love cricket, Australian Rules football, rugby, swimming and surfing. Canadians are passionate about ice hockey, along with basketball, lacrosse, curling and skiing. For cricket-loving migrants from South Asia, Australia offers a familiar sporting culture, while Canada's cricket scene is smaller but growing thanks to its diverse communities.

Best Cities to Consider in Each Country

Australia

  • Sydney: Australia's largest city and financial centre, with iconic harbour views and beaches. Excellent job market, but very high housing costs.
  • Melbourne: known for culture, food, coffee, sport and universities. Often ranked among the world's most liveable cities. Weather is famously changeable.
  • Brisbane: warm, subtropical and more affordable than Sydney or Melbourne, with a growing economy ahead of hosting the 2032 Olympic Games.
  • Perth: sunny and relaxed, strong in mining and resources, but geographically isolated.
  • Adelaide: smaller, more affordable and known for wine regions, festivals and a slower pace of life.
  • Canberra: the capital, with many government jobs, high average incomes and cold winters by Australian standards.

Canada

  • Toronto: Canada's largest city and business hub, extremely diverse, with strong finance and tech sectors. Housing is expensive.
  • Vancouver: stunning mountains and ocean, the mildest winters among major Canadian cities and a strong tech and film industry, but very high housing costs.
  • Montreal: French-speaking, artistic and more affordable than Toronto or Vancouver, with a European feel. French is important for many jobs.
  • Calgary: strong energy sector, no provincial sales tax in Alberta and relatively affordable housing, close to the Rocky Mountains.
  • Ottawa: the capital, with stable government jobs and a family-friendly lifestyle.
  • Halifax: a growing coastal city in Atlantic Canada with a slower pace of life and lower costs than the biggest cities.

Real Experiences: What Migrants Often Say

People who have moved to each country often share similar observations.

  • About Australia: many migrants say the lifestyle exceeded expectations: more time outdoors, friendly people and good pay. Common frustrations include the cost of housing, the distance from family and the difficulty of getting the first local job without Australian experience.
  • About Canada: newcomers often praise the welcoming attitude toward immigrants, safety and diversity. The first winter is a shock for many, and finding work in one's own profession can take time. Many say they adapted to the cold faster than expected once they had proper winter clothing and embraced winter activities.

In both countries, migrants who research thoroughly, arrive with savings to cover several months, network actively and remain flexible about their first job tend to settle most successfully.

Pros and Cons Summary

Australia

Pros: warm climate, beaches and outdoor lifestyle; high wages; compulsory superannuation; strong work-life balance culture; proximity to Asia.

Cons: very expensive housing in major cities; isolation from Europe and North America; bushfires, heatwaves and intense sun; long flights home for many migrants.

Canada

Pros: clear immigration pathways; universal public healthcare; spectacular nature; close to the United States; bilingual opportunities.

Cons: long, cold winters; high housing costs in Toronto and Vancouver; long waits for some medical care; salaries that are often lower than in the US or Australia for similar roles.

Questions to Ask Yourself Before Deciding

  1. Which climate would I genuinely enjoy year-round?
  2. Is my occupation in demand in either country, and will my qualifications be recognised?
  3. Which immigration pathway is realistic for my age, skills and language scores?
  4. Where do I have friends, relatives or a community?
  5. How important is being close to my home country or to the US or Europe?
  6. What can I afford for housing, and in which cities?
  7. What are my long-term plans: permanent residency, citizenship, raising a family?

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it cheaper to live in Australia or Canada?

Overall living costs are broadly similar. Housing is very expensive in Sydney, Melbourne, Toronto and Vancouver alike; Australia tends to have higher wages, while Canada often has lower costs outside its biggest cities. Your city matters more than the country.

Is $70,000 a good salary in Australia?

It is close to typical full-time earnings and enough for a comfortable life in most cities if you share housing or live outside the most expensive areas, but it is tight for buying a home in Sydney or Melbourne on one income.

Is it easier to immigrate to Canada or Australia?

It depends on your occupation, age, language skills and qualifications. Many people find Canada's Express Entry slightly more accessible, but both are competitive.

Which country has better salaries?

Australia generally has higher average and minimum wages, but costs are also high. Compare salaries for your specific job and city.

Which is better for families?

Both offer excellent schools, healthcare and safety. Many families choose based on climate, job opportunities and where relatives or communities already live.

Is healthcare free in both countries?

Core hospital and doctor services are publicly funded for eligible residents in both, but you may still pay for some medicines, dental care and private services.

Which country is better for Indian migrants?

Both have large, established Indian communities, with Indian groceries, restaurants, temples, gurdwaras and cultural associations in major cities. Indians are among the largest groups of new permanent residents in both countries. The better choice depends on your profession and visa options.

Which country is better for international students?

Both offer excellent universities and post-study work options. Compare tuition fees, scholarships, part-time work rules, post-graduation work visa length and how studying connects to permanent residency, as these rules change frequently.

Can I move to either country without a job offer?

Yes, through some skilled migration programmes. Canada's Express Entry and Australia's skilled independent visas don't require a job offer, although an offer or state nomination can improve your chances.

Do I need to speak French to live in Canada?

Not outside Quebec. English is the main language in most of Canada. In Quebec, and especially Montreal, French is important for daily life and many jobs. Strong French skills also earn extra points in Canada's immigration system.

Which country is safer?

Both are very safe by global standards, with low rates of violent crime and strict gun laws. Safety differences are more about local neighbourhoods and natural hazards than about the countries as a whole.

Which has better weather, Sydney or Vancouver?

Sydney is much warmer and sunnier, with mild winters. Vancouver has mild winters by Canadian standards but is often grey and rainy from autumn to spring, with pleasant, dry summers.

Final Thoughts

There is no single winner. Australia offers sunshine, higher wages and an outdoor lifestyle; Canada offers clear immigration pathways, natural beauty across four seasons and closeness to North America's largest economy. Think about your career, climate, family and long-term plans, research visa options carefully and, if possible, visit both before deciding.
