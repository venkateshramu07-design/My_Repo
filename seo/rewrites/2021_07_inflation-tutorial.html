Quick answer: Inflation is the general rise in prices of goods and services over time, which reduces the purchasing power of money. It's usually measured by a consumer price index (CPI) and expressed as an annual percentage. The main causes are demand-pull (too much demand), cost-push (rising production costs) and built-in inflation (wage-price expectations). Central banks control inflation mainly by adjusting interest rates, and most developed economies target about 2% per year.

If you've ever noticed that your grocery bill keeps growing even though you're buying the same things, you've experienced inflation. A little inflation is normal in a healthy economy, but high inflation can erode savings, squeeze household budgets and create economic uncertainty, as many countries experienced in 2021 to 2023.

This tutorial explains what inflation is, how it's measured, what causes it, how governments and central banks manage it and what you can do to protect your money.

What Is Inflation?

Inflation means that, on average, prices are rising across the economy. If annual inflation is 5%, something that cost 100 a year ago now costs around 105. Your money buys less than before.

Related terms:

  • Deflation: a general fall in prices.
  • Disinflation: inflation is still positive but slowing.
  • Hyperinflation: extremely rapid inflation, often defined as more than 50% per month, as seen in Zimbabwe in the late 2000s and Venezuela in the late 2010s.
  • Stagflation: high inflation combined with weak growth and high unemployment, as in the 1970s.

How Inflation Is Measured

Statistical agencies track the prices of a "basket" of goods and services that typical households buy, such as food, housing, transport, clothing and healthcare. The most common measure is the Consumer Price Index (CPI).

  • Headline inflation includes all items.
  • Core inflation excludes volatile items like food and energy to show the underlying trend.
  • Other measures include the Producer Price Index (PPI) and, in the US, the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index, which the Federal Reserve targets.

Main Causes of Inflation

1. Demand-Pull Inflation

When total demand for goods and services grows faster than the economy can produce them, prices rise. This can happen when consumers and businesses spend more because of low interest rates, rising incomes, government stimulus or strong confidence.

2. Cost-Push Inflation

When the cost of producing goods rises, businesses pass higher costs on to customers. Causes include higher energy and raw material prices, supply chain disruptions, rising wages and weaker currencies that make imports more expensive. The energy and food price spikes after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 are a recent example.

3. Built-In Inflation

If people expect prices to keep rising, workers demand higher wages and businesses raise prices in anticipation, creating a wage-price spiral. This is why central banks pay close attention to inflation expectations.

4. Monetary Factors

If the money supply grows much faster than economic output over a long period, inflation tends to follow. Extreme money printing is a common feature of hyperinflations.

Effects of Inflation

Who Loses

  • Savers holding cash or low-interest deposits.
  • People on fixed incomes, such as some pensioners.
  • Workers whose wages rise more slowly than prices.
  • Lower-income households, who spend more of their income on essentials like food and energy.

Who May Gain

  • Borrowers with fixed-rate debts, since they repay with money that's worth less.
  • Owners of assets that rise with inflation, such as some property or commodities.
  • Governments with large debts, as inflation reduces the real value of debt.

What Is a "Normal" Inflation Rate?

Most central banks in developed economies, including the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of England, target inflation of about 2% per year. The Reserve Bank of India targets 4%, within a band of 2% to 6%. Low, stable inflation is considered healthy because it encourages spending and investment while giving central banks room to cut interest rates in downturns.

How Governments and Central Banks Control Inflation

Monetary Policy

  • Raising interest rates: makes borrowing more expensive and saving more attractive, cooling spending and demand.
  • Quantitative tightening: reducing the central bank's holdings of bonds to tighten financial conditions.
  • Forward guidance: communicating future policy to shape expectations.

Fiscal Policy

  • Reducing government spending or increasing taxes to cool demand.
  • Targeted support for vulnerable households during price shocks.

Supply-Side Measures

  • Improving supply chains, energy supply and productivity.
  • Reducing trade barriers to lower import costs.

Comparing Inflation Across Countries

Inflation rates vary widely depending on economic structure, currency stability, energy dependence and policy. During 2022, inflation peaked at about 9% in the US and above 11% in the UK, levels not seen in decades, before falling back substantially in 2023 and 2024. The UAE, which pegs its dirham to the US dollar and subsidises some essentials, typically experiences more moderate inflation, although housing costs in cities like Dubai can rise quickly. For current figures, check national statistics agencies or the IMF's World Economic Outlook database.

How to Protect Your Money From Inflation

  • Don't keep too much in cash: hold an emergency fund, but invest long-term money.
  • Invest in diversified assets: shares have historically outpaced inflation over long periods.
  • Consider inflation-linked bonds: such as US TIPS or UK index-linked gilts.
  • Fix borrowing costs: fixed-rate loans can protect you from rising interest rates.
  • Grow your income: develop skills and negotiate pay rises that keep pace with prices.
  • Review your budget: track rising costs and adjust spending.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is inflation always bad?

No. Low, stable inflation is considered healthy. Problems arise when inflation is high, volatile or unexpected, or when prices fall (deflation), which can discourage spending.

Why do interest rates go up when inflation is high?

Higher interest rates reduce borrowing and spending, which lowers demand and helps bring inflation down.

What caused the high inflation of 2021 to 2023?

A combination of strong demand after the pandemic, government stimulus, supply chain disruptions, labour shortages and energy and food price spikes linked to the war in Ukraine.

How does inflation affect my savings?

If your savings earn less interest than the inflation rate, their real value falls, meaning they buy less over time.

Final Thoughts

Inflation affects everyone, from the price of your groceries to the value of your savings and the interest on your loans. Understanding what causes it and how central banks respond helps you make better financial decisions. Keep an emergency fund, invest long-term money wisely and keep building your earning power, and you'll be better prepared whatever the inflation rate.
