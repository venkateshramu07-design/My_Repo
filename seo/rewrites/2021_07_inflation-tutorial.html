Quick answer: Inflation is the general rise in prices of goods and services over time, which reduces the purchasing power of money. It's usually measured by a consumer price index (CPI) and expressed as an annual percentage. The main causes are demand-pull (too much demand), cost-push (rising production costs) and built-in inflation (wage-price expectations). Central banks control inflation mainly by adjusting interest rates, and most developed economies target about 2% per year.

If you've ever noticed that your grocery bill keeps growing even though you're buying the same things, you've experienced inflation. A little inflation is normal in a healthy economy, but high inflation can erode savings, squeeze household budgets and create economic uncertainty, as many countries experienced in 2021 to 2023.

This tutorial explains what inflation is, how it's measured, what causes it, how governments and central banks manage it and what you can do to protect your money.

What Is Inflation?

Inflation means that, on average, prices are rising across the economy. If annual inflation is 5%, something that cost 100 a year ago now costs around 105. Your money buys less than before.

Related terms:

  • Deflation: a general fall in prices.
  • Disinflation: inflation is still positive but slowing.
  • Hyperinflation: extremely rapid inflation, often defined as more than 50% per month, as seen in Zimbabwe in the late 2000s and Venezuela in the late 2010s.
  • Stagflation: high inflation combined with weak growth and high unemployment, as in the 1970s.

How Inflation Is Measured

Statistical agencies track the prices of a "basket" of goods and services that typical households buy, such as food, housing, transport, clothing and healthcare. The most common measure is the Consumer Price Index (CPI).

  • Headline inflation includes all items.
  • Core inflation excludes volatile items like food and energy to show the underlying trend.
  • Other measures include the Producer Price Index (PPI) and, in the US, the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index, which the Federal Reserve targets.

Main Causes of Inflation

1. Demand-Pull Inflation

When total demand for goods and services grows faster than the economy can produce them, prices rise. This can happen when consumers and businesses spend more because of low interest rates, rising incomes, government stimulus or strong confidence.

2. Cost-Push Inflation

When the cost of producing goods rises, businesses pass higher costs on to customers. Causes include higher energy and raw material prices, supply chain disruptions, rising wages and weaker currencies that make imports more expensive. The energy and food price spikes after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 are a recent example.

3. Built-In Inflation

If people expect prices to keep rising, workers demand higher wages and businesses raise prices in anticipation, creating a wage-price spiral. This is why central banks pay close attention to inflation expectations.

4. Monetary Factors

If the money supply grows much faster than economic output over a long period, inflation tends to follow. Extreme money printing is a common feature of hyperinflations.

Effects of Inflation

Who Loses

  • Savers holding cash or low-interest deposits.
  • People on fixed incomes, such as some pensioners.
  • Workers whose wages rise more slowly than prices.
  • Lower-income households, who spend more of their income on essentials like food and energy.

Who May Gain

  • Borrowers with fixed-rate debts, since they repay with money that's worth less.
  • Owners of assets that rise with inflation, such as some property or commodities.
  • Governments with large debts, as inflation reduces the real value of debt.

What Is a "Normal" Inflation Rate?

Most central banks in developed economies, including the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of England, target inflation of about 2% per year. The Reserve Bank of India targets 4%, within a band of 2% to 6%. Low, stable inflation is considered healthy because it encourages spending and investment while giving central banks room to cut interest rates in downturns.

How Governments and Central Banks Control Inflation

Monetary Policy

  • Raising interest rates: makes borrowing more expensive and saving more attractive, cooling spending and demand.
  • Quantitative tightening: reducing the central bank's holdings of bonds to tighten financial conditions.
  • Forward guidance: communicating future policy to shape expectations.

Fiscal Policy

  • Reducing government spending or increasing taxes to cool demand.
  • Targeted support for vulnerable households during price shocks.

Supply-Side Measures

  • Improving supply chains, energy supply and productivity.
  • Reducing trade barriers to lower import costs.

How CPI Is Calculated: A Simple Example

Imagine a simplified basket containing just three items that a typical household buys each month:

ItemPrice last yearPrice this year
Rice (10 kg)500540
Bus pass1,0001,050
Electricity1,5001,560
Total basket3,0003,150

The basket cost rose from 3,000 to 3,150, an increase of 150, or 5%. That's the inflation rate for this simplified basket. Real CPI calculations include hundreds of items, weighted by how much households typically spend on each category, and are collected from many locations every month. In India, CPI is published by the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI); in the US, by the Bureau of Labor Statistics; and in the UK, by the Office for National Statistics.

Real vs Nominal: Why Inflation Changes the Numbers

Economists distinguish between nominal values (measured in today's money) and real values (adjusted for inflation).

  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation. If your savings account pays 4% and inflation is 6%, your real return is about −2%: your money is losing purchasing power.
  • Real wages: if your salary rises 5% but inflation is 7%, your real pay has fallen about 2%.
  • Real GDP: measures economic growth after removing the effect of rising prices.

Always think in real terms when planning savings, investments and salary negotiations.

The Rule of 70: How Fast Prices Double

A quick way to understand inflation's long-term impact is the Rule of 70: divide 70 by the inflation rate to estimate how many years it takes for prices to double.

  • At 2% inflation, prices double in about 35 years.
  • At 5% inflation, prices double in about 14 years.
  • At 7% inflation, prices double in about 10 years.

This is why retirement planning must account for inflation. Expenses of ₹50,000 per month today could become ₹1,00,000 per month in 14 years at 5% inflation.

Famous Episodes of Inflation in History

  • Weimar Germany (1921–1923): prices rose so fast that people carried banknotes in wheelbarrows; at the peak, prices doubled every few days.
  • Hungary (1945–1946): experienced the worst hyperinflation ever recorded, with prices doubling roughly every 15 hours at the peak.
  • The US in the 1970s: oil shocks and loose monetary policy pushed inflation above 13% by 1980. Federal Reserve Chair Paul Volcker raised interest rates to around 20%, causing a recession but breaking inflation.
  • India in the early 1990s and 2008–2013: periods of high inflation, partly driven by food and fuel prices, led to the adoption of formal inflation targeting in 2016.
  • Zimbabwe (2007–2008): monthly inflation reached astronomical levels, and the central bank printed a 100-trillion-dollar note.
  • Venezuela (2016–2019): collapsing oil revenue and money printing led to hyperinflation and mass emigration.

Inflation and Interest Rates: How Central Banks Decide

Central banks look at a range of indicators before changing interest rates: current inflation, core inflation, wage growth, unemployment, economic growth, inflation expectations and global conditions. In India, the Reserve Bank of India's Monetary Policy Committee meets several times a year to set the repo rate, the rate at which it lends to commercial banks. Changes in the repo rate flow through to home loans, car loans, fixed deposit rates and business borrowing. Policy changes take time, often 12 to 18 months, to have their full effect on inflation, so central banks try to act based on where inflation is heading, not just where it is today.

Comparing Inflation Across Countries

Inflation rates vary widely depending on economic structure, currency stability, energy dependence and policy. During 2022, inflation peaked at about 9% in the US and above 11% in the UK, levels not seen in decades, before falling back substantially in 2023 and 2024. The UAE, which pegs its dirham to the US dollar and subsidises some essentials, typically experiences more moderate inflation, although housing costs in cities like Dubai can rise quickly. For current figures, check national statistics agencies or the IMF's World Economic Outlook database.

Inflation in India: What You Should Know

India's inflation is influenced by factors that differ from those in many developed economies.

  • Food prices: food makes up a large share of India's CPI basket, nearly half in the older series, so monsoon rains, crop failures and vegetable prices such as tomatoes and onions can cause sharp swings.
  • Fuel prices: India imports most of its crude oil, so global oil prices and the rupee's exchange rate affect transport and production costs.
  • Inflation targeting: since 2016, the RBI has had a formal mandate to keep CPI inflation at 4%, within a tolerance band of 2% to 6%.
  • Government measures: export restrictions, buffer stocks of food grains, fuel tax changes and subsidies are sometimes used to ease price spikes.
  • CPI base revision: the government periodically updates the CPI basket and weights to reflect changing consumption patterns.

Inflation and Your Investments

Different asset types respond differently to inflation:

  • Cash and savings accounts: lose purchasing power when interest rates are below inflation.
  • Fixed-rate bonds: lose value when inflation and interest rates rise unexpectedly, because their fixed payments become less valuable.
  • Shares: companies can often raise prices over time, so equities have historically beaten inflation over long periods, though they can struggle during sudden inflation spikes.
  • Real estate: property values and rents often rise with inflation, though high interest rates can hurt property prices.
  • Gold: traditionally seen as an inflation hedge, especially popular in India, though its price can be volatile and it produces no income.
  • Inflation-indexed bonds: such as US TIPS and UK index-linked gilts, directly adjust for inflation.

Shrinkflation and Hidden Inflation

Sometimes prices don't change, but you get less. Shrinkflation happens when companies reduce package sizes while keeping prices the same, such as a 100-gram biscuit pack becoming 90 grams, or a bag of chips containing more air. A related practice, sometimes called skimpflation, involves cutting quality or service, for example fewer staff in shops or cheaper ingredients. Statistical agencies try to account for these changes by measuring price per unit of quantity, but consumers often notice them before they appear in official numbers. Checking the price per kilogram or litre on shelf labels helps you compare true costs.

Your Personal Inflation Rate

The official inflation rate reflects an average household's spending. Your own experience may be very different. If you spend heavily on rent in a city where rents are rising quickly, your personal inflation may be much higher than the national figure. If you own your home outright and rarely drive, it may be lower.

To estimate your personal inflation rate:

  1. List your main monthly expenses and their costs a year ago.
  2. Note what the same items cost today.
  3. Calculate the percentage change in your total.

Knowing your own rate helps you set realistic salary expectations, savings targets and retirement plans.

How Businesses Deal With Inflation

  • Raising prices: companies with strong brands or few competitors can pass higher costs to customers more easily.
  • Cutting costs: improving efficiency, renegotiating contracts or automating processes.
  • Hedging: locking in prices for raw materials or currencies through financial contracts.
  • Adjusting wages: employers may give larger raises to retain staff, which can feed into further price increases.
  • Changing products: resizing packages or reformulating products, sometimes leading to shrinkflation.

Small businesses often struggle more than large ones because they have less bargaining power and thinner margins.

Deflation: Why Falling Prices Can Be Dangerous

If inflation is bad, wouldn't falling prices be good? Not usually. When prices fall across the economy for a sustained period, people and businesses may delay purchases, expecting things to be cheaper later. Spending drops, companies cut production and jobs, wages fall and the real burden of debts increases because debts stay fixed while incomes shrink. Japan experienced long periods of mild deflation from the late 1990s, contributing to decades of weak growth, and deflation made the Great Depression of the 1930s far worse. That's why central banks aim for low positive inflation, not zero.

Inflation and Wages: The Wage-Price Spiral

When prices rise, workers naturally ask for higher pay to maintain their living standards. If businesses then raise prices to cover higher wage costs, and workers again demand more, a wage-price spiral can develop. Central banks watch wage growth closely. However, many economists note that in recent episodes, wages often lagged behind prices, meaning many workers' real incomes fell before recovering. Negotiating pay rises that at least match inflation is important for protecting your standard of living.

Practical Budgeting Tips During High Inflation

  • Track prices: notice which categories are rising fastest in your budget.
  • Buy store brands: they often cost less for similar quality.
  • Plan meals: reduce food waste and impulse purchases.
  • Review subscriptions and bills: switch providers or negotiate where possible.
  • Use bulk buying wisely: for non-perishables you'll definitely use.
  • Prioritise high-interest debt: rising rates make it more expensive.

Inflation Glossary

  • Base effect: when inflation looks unusually high or low because of price movements in the same month a year earlier.
  • Core inflation: inflation excluding volatile food and energy prices.
  • Headline inflation: the overall inflation rate including all items.
  • Purchasing power: how much a unit of money can buy.
  • Repo rate: the rate at which a central bank lends to commercial banks; a key tool for controlling inflation.
  • Stagflation: high inflation combined with stagnant growth and high unemployment.

How Inflation Affects Borrowers and Lenders

Inflation transfers wealth between borrowers and lenders. If you have a fixed-rate home loan of ₹50 lakh and inflation runs high, your EMIs stay the same while your salary may rise, making the loan easier to repay in real terms. Lenders receive money that buys less than expected. However, when central banks raise interest rates to fight inflation, people with floating-rate loans see their EMIs or loan tenures increase, which can strain household budgets.

How to Protect Your Money From Inflation

  • Don't keep too much in cash: hold an emergency fund, but invest long-term money.
  • Invest in diversified assets: shares have historically outpaced inflation over long periods.
  • Consider inflation-linked bonds: such as US TIPS or UK index-linked gilts.
  • Fix borrowing costs: fixed-rate loans can protect you from rising interest rates.
  • Grow your income: develop skills and negotiate pay rises that keep pace with prices.
  • Review your budget: track rising costs and adjust spending.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who benefits from inflation?

Borrowers with fixed-rate loans benefit, because they repay with money worth less. Governments with large debts, owners of assets such as property and some shares, and businesses that can raise prices faster than costs can also gain. Savers in low-interest accounts and people on fixed incomes lose.

Is inflation always bad?

No. Low, stable inflation is considered healthy. Problems arise when inflation is high, volatile or unexpected, or when prices fall (deflation), which can discourage spending.

Why do interest rates go up when inflation is high?

Higher interest rates reduce borrowing and spending, which lowers demand and helps bring inflation down.

What caused the high inflation of 2021 to 2023?

A combination of strong demand after the pandemic, government stimulus, supply chain disruptions, labour shortages and energy and food price spikes linked to the war in Ukraine.

How does inflation affect my savings?

If your savings earn less interest than the inflation rate, their real value falls, meaning they buy less over time.

How does inflation affect the stock market?

Moderate, stable inflation is usually fine for shares. High or rising inflation tends to hurt stock prices in the short term because it leads to higher interest rates, raises companies' costs and reduces the present value of future profits. Over long periods, however, shares have generally outpaced inflation.

Is a weaker currency linked to inflation?

Often, yes. When a country's currency weakens, imports such as oil, electronics and raw materials become more expensive, which can push up domestic prices. This is called imported inflation.

How does inflation affect retirees?

Retirees living on fixed pensions or interest income are especially vulnerable, because their income may not rise as fast as prices. Including some growth investments, inflation-linked income sources and regular budget reviews helps protect purchasing power over a long retirement.

Can inflation be predicted?

Central banks, economists and markets forecast inflation, but predictions are uncertain, especially when shocks like pandemics, wars or energy crises occur. Inflation expectations surveys and bond market prices offer clues about what people expect.

What is the difference between CPI and WPI?

The Consumer Price Index measures prices paid by households for goods and services. The Wholesale Price Index, used in India, tracks prices at the wholesale level for goods traded between businesses. CPI is now the main measure the RBI uses for its inflation target.

Does printing money always cause inflation?

Not always immediately. If the economy has spare capacity or people save the extra money, prices may not rise much. But sustained, excessive money creation relative to economic output tends to cause inflation over time.

Why do prices rarely go back down after inflation?

Inflation measures the rate of price increases. When inflation falls from 8% to 3%, prices are still rising, just more slowly. Prices of some items can fall, but a general fall in prices (deflation) is uncommon and usually a sign of economic weakness.

Final Thoughts

Inflation affects everyone, from the price of your groceries to the value of your savings and the interest on your loans. Understanding what causes it and how central banks respond helps you make better financial decisions. Keep an emergency fund, invest long-term money wisely and keep building your earning power, and you'll be better prepared whatever the inflation rate.
