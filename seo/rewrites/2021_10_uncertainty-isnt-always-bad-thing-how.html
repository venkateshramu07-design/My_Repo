Quick answer: Uncertainty feels uncomfortable because the brain prefers predictability and treats the unknown as a possible threat. But uncertainty isn't always bad: it creates room for growth, opportunity, creativity and pleasant surprises. To deal with it, focus on what you can control, accept what you can't, plan for a few likely scenarios, take small steps instead of waiting for perfect information, limit worry and news overload, look after your body, and lean on people you trust. Building tolerance for uncertainty is a skill that improves with practice.

Will I keep my job? Will this relationship last? Is this the right decision? What will the economy do next year? Life is full of questions without clear answers. For many people, not knowing feels worse than bad news itself.

This guide explains why uncertainty is so stressful, why it can actually be good for you, and practical, research-based ways to handle it with more calm and confidence.

Why Uncertainty Feels So Uncomfortable

Our brains are prediction machines. They constantly try to anticipate what will happen next so we can prepare. When outcomes are unclear, the brain can treat uncertainty as a potential danger and activate the stress response.

A striking 2016 study by Archy de Berker and colleagues at University College London found that people were more stressed when there was a 50% chance of receiving a mild electric shock than when they knew for certain they would receive one. In other words, uncertainty itself can be more stressful than a known unpleasant outcome.

Psychologists use the term intolerance of uncertainty to describe how strongly someone reacts to the unknown. People high in intolerance of uncertainty tend to worry more, seek constant reassurance and avoid situations with unpredictable outcomes. Research by Michel Dugas and colleagues has linked it to generalised anxiety disorder, and it's now a target of some anxiety treatments.

Why Uncertainty Isn't Always Bad

1. It Makes Growth Possible

Every important change in life, a new job, a move, a relationship, starting a business, involves uncertainty. If outcomes were always guaranteed, we'd never learn or grow. Stepping into the unknown builds confidence and skills.

2. It Creates Opportunity

Uncertain times often open doors. Many successful businesses were started during recessions, when competition was weaker and costs lower. Career changes, new ideas and fresh starts frequently emerge from periods of disruption.

3. It Fuels Curiosity and Creativity

Not knowing encourages exploration. Scientists, artists and entrepreneurs work at the edge of the unknown. Curiosity, the desire to resolve uncertainty, is linked to learning, engagement and wellbeing.

4. It Makes Life Interesting

Imagine knowing exactly how every film, football match or holiday would turn out. Much of life's excitement comes from not knowing. Research by Timothy Wilson and Daniel Gilbert found that people's positive feelings about a pleasant event can last longer when they're uncertain why it happened, because they keep thinking about it.

5. It Builds Resilience

Each time you cope with uncertainty, you gather evidence that you can handle the unknown. Over time, this makes future uncertainty less frightening.

6. It Keeps You Humble and Open-Minded

Accepting that you can't know everything encourages better decisions: considering different possibilities, listening to others and changing course when evidence changes.

What History and Philosophy Teach Us

People have always lived with uncertainty, often far more than we do today: harvests could fail, illnesses had no cure, and news travelled slowly. Many philosophical and spiritual traditions developed ways of coping:

  • Stoicism taught focusing on your own judgements and actions, and accepting external events calmly.
  • Buddhism emphasises impermanence: everything changes, and suffering often comes from clinging to how we want things to be.
  • The Bhagavad Gita advises doing your duty without attachment to the fruits of action, focusing on effort rather than outcomes.
  • Taoism encourages flowing with change rather than resisting it, like water adapting to its container.

These ideas share a common message with modern psychology: we suffer less when we focus on what we can do now and loosen our grip on outcomes we can't control.

The Upside of Not Knowing: Real Examples

  • Career pivots: many people discover their most fulfilling work after an unexpected layoff forces them to explore new paths.
  • Scientific discovery: Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin in 1928 came from noticing something unexpected in a contaminated petri dish.
  • Businesses born in downturns: companies such as Airbnb and Uber were founded around the time of the 2008–2009 financial crisis.
  • Personal growth: studies of post-traumatic growth by Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun found that many people report greater appreciation of life, closer relationships and new possibilities after very difficult, uncertain experiences.

Common Unhelpful Reactions to Uncertainty

  • Overthinking and worry: replaying "what if" scenarios without reaching solutions.
  • Catastrophising: assuming the worst possible outcome is the most likely.
  • Seeking constant reassurance from friends, the internet or experts.
  • Over-planning or excessive information gathering to try to eliminate all risk.
  • Avoidance and procrastination: putting off decisions to escape discomfort.
  • Trying to control everything and everyone, which often increases stress and conflict.
  • Making impulsive decisions just to end the discomfort of not knowing.

These reactions may bring short-term relief but often increase anxiety in the long run, because they teach the brain that uncertainty is unbearable.

How to Deal With Uncertainty: Practical Strategies

1. Separate What You Can and Can't Control

Draw two columns. In one, list what's in your control: your effort, preparation, habits, responses and choices. In the other, list what isn't: the economy, other people's decisions, the weather, the past. Put your energy into the first column. The ancient Stoic philosopher Epictetus taught that peace comes from focusing on what is "up to us" and accepting what is not, an idea echoed in the well-known Serenity Prayer.

2. Accept Uncertainty Instead of Fighting It

Acceptance doesn't mean giving up. It means acknowledging, "I don't know how this will turn out, and that's uncomfortable, but I can live with it." Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), developed by Steven Hayes, teaches people to make room for difficult feelings while continuing to act in line with their values.

3. Plan for Scenarios, Not Certainties

Instead of trying to predict the exact future, think through a few possible scenarios:

  • Best case: what would you do if things go well?
  • Most likely case: what's realistic, and how will you handle it?
  • Worst case: what would you do, and who could help?

Having a rough plan for each reduces fear, because you know you have options. Often the worst case, once written down, turns out to be more manageable than it felt.

4. Take Small Steps

You don't need complete information to act. Take the next small, reversible step: apply for one job, have one conversation, try one experiment. Action creates information and momentum, which reduce uncertainty more effectively than endless thinking.

5. Limit Worry Time

Set aside a specific 15 to 20 minute "worry period" each day. When worries arise at other times, note them down and postpone them until then. Studies of this technique, called worry postponement or stimulus control, have found it can reduce the amount of time people spend worrying.

6. Reduce Information Overload

During uncertain times, such as economic downturns, elections or health crises, it's tempting to check news constantly. Research during the COVID-19 pandemic linked heavy news consumption with higher anxiety. Choose a few reliable sources, check them once or twice a day, and turn off unnecessary notifications.

7. Challenge Catastrophic Thinking

When your mind jumps to the worst outcome, ask:

  • What evidence supports this? What evidence goes against it?
  • What's the most likely outcome?
  • Have I handled similar situations before?
  • What would I tell a friend in this situation?

This technique comes from cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), one of the most widely researched treatments for anxiety.

8. Build Routines

When the big picture is uncertain, daily routines provide stability. Regular sleep, meals, exercise and work hours give a sense of structure and control.

9. Look After Your Body

Stress is harder to manage when you're tired, hungry or inactive. Exercise reduces stress hormones and improves mood. Sleep strengthens emotional regulation. Slow breathing, with longer exhales than inhales, calms the nervous system within minutes.

10. Practise Mindfulness

Much of the distress around uncertainty comes from living in an imagined future. Mindfulness brings attention back to the present moment, where you can actually act. Even five minutes a day of focusing on your breath, or noticing sights and sounds around you, can reduce rumination.

11. Connect With Others

Talking to people you trust helps you feel less alone and can offer new perspectives. Social support is one of the strongest buffers against stress. Be careful, though, not to rely on others for constant reassurance; sharing feelings is different from repeatedly asking "Will it be okay?"

12. Remember Your Track Record

Think back to past uncertain times: exams, job changes, moves, illnesses. You got through them, often better than you expected. Remind yourself of the skills and support that helped.

13. Practise Tolerating Small Uncertainties

Like a muscle, tolerance for uncertainty grows with exercise. Therapists sometimes encourage clients to deliberately try small uncertain actions: trying a new restaurant without reading reviews, taking a different route to work, or sending an email without re-reading it five times. Each experience shows the brain that not knowing is survivable.

Uncertainty in Different Areas of Life

At Work

Restructurings, layoffs and changing technology, including AI, create career uncertainty. Focus on what you can do: build in-demand skills, maintain your professional network, keep an updated CV, do excellent work and build an emergency fund. These steps increase your options whatever happens.

With Money

Markets rise and fall unpredictably. Instead of trying to predict them, build resilience: an emergency fund, diversified long-term investments, adequate insurance and manageable debt. A financial plan designed for uncertainty doesn't need perfect predictions.

In Relationships

Not knowing where a relationship is heading can be stressful. Honest communication about expectations helps, but some uncertainty is part of any relationship. Focus on how you treat each other now and whether the relationship reflects your values.

With Health

Waiting for test results or living with a chronic condition involves deep uncertainty. Gather reliable information from your doctor, ask questions, follow treatment plans, and seek emotional support. Limit online searching of symptoms, which often increases anxiety.

In Big Life Decisions

Moving abroad, changing careers, marrying or having children all involve unknowns. No amount of research removes all uncertainty. Make the best decision you can with the information available, based on your values, and accept that you'll adapt as things unfold.

Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

When you need to make a decision without full information:

  1. Clarify your values and goals. What matters most to you?
  2. Gather enough information, but set a limit. Perfect information rarely exists.
  3. Consider reversibility. Jeff Bezos has described "two-way door" decisions, which can be reversed if they don't work out, and "one-way door" decisions, which are hard to undo. Make two-way door decisions quickly; take more care with one-way doors.
  4. Use the 10-10-10 rule, popularised by Suzy Welch: how will you feel about this decision in 10 minutes, 10 months and 10 years?
  5. Accept that good decisions can have bad outcomes, and vice versa. Judge decisions by the quality of your thinking, not just the result. Former professional poker player Annie Duke calls the mistake of judging decisions only by outcomes "resulting".

Helpful Things to Say to Yourself

The way you talk to yourself shapes how uncertainty feels. Try replacing anxious thoughts with more balanced ones:

Anxious thoughtBalanced alternative
"I need to know how this will turn out.""I'd like to know, but I can cope without knowing yet."
"Something bad is going to happen.""Something could go wrong, and things could also go well. I'll deal with whatever comes."
"I can't handle this.""I've handled hard things before. I can take this one step at a time."
"I have to make the perfect decision.""I'll make the best decision I can with what I know, and adjust if needed."
"Everyone else has it figured out.""Everyone faces uncertainty; most people just don't talk about it."

Leading Others Through Uncertainty

Managers, parents and community leaders often have to guide others when they don't have all the answers themselves. Helpful approaches include:

  • Be honest about what you know and don't know. People trust leaders who admit uncertainty more than those who make false promises.
  • Communicate regularly, even when there's little news. Silence breeds rumours.
  • Focus on shared values and next steps. "We don't know what next quarter holds, but here's what we're doing this week."
  • Involve people in planning where possible, which gives them a sense of control.
  • Show calm and care. Your emotional tone affects others.

Helping Children Cope With Uncertainty

Children also feel anxious about the unknown, such as changing schools, family changes or news events. Parents can help by:

  • Keeping daily routines as consistent as possible.
  • Giving honest, age-appropriate information without overwhelming detail.
  • Validating feelings: "It's normal to feel nervous about starting at a new school."
  • Focusing on what they can control, such as packing their bag or practising the route.
  • Modelling calm responses to uncertainty yourself.
  • Encouraging small, brave steps rather than avoiding everything new.

A 7-Day Uncertainty Tolerance Practice

  1. Day 1: write two lists: what you can control and what you can't, about your biggest current worry.
  2. Day 2: set a 15-minute worry period and postpone worries outside it.
  3. Day 3: do something small without researching it first, such as trying a new dish.
  4. Day 4: limit news and social media to two short checks.
  5. Day 5: write best, likely and worst-case scenarios for one situation, with a plan for each.
  6. Day 6: take one small action on a decision you've been postponing.
  7. Day 7: reflect: what was easier than expected? What helped most?

Repeat the practices that worked. Tolerance for uncertainty grows gradually through experience.

Real-Life Example

When Arjun's company announced layoffs, he spent weeks anxiously refreshing news sites and checking his inbox, barely sleeping. Eventually, he tried a different approach. He listed what he could control: updating his CV, contacting former colleagues, saving more each month and completing an online course in a skill his industry needed. He limited news to once a day and started running again. His role was eventually cut, but he found a new job within two months, partly thanks to the connections and skills he'd built. "The uncertainty didn't go away," he said, "but I stopped letting it run my life."

Building Your "Uncertainty Toolkit"

It helps to have a short list of go-to actions for moments when uncertainty feels overwhelming. Write yours down and keep it on your phone:

  • Three slow breaths with long exhales.
  • A ten-minute walk outside.
  • Writing down the worry and one next step.
  • Calling a particular friend or family member.
  • A favourite calming song or podcast.
  • Reminding yourself of one past challenge you overcame.

Having this plan ready means you don't have to think clearly in the moment you're most anxious; you just follow the list.

When Uncertainty Becomes Anxiety

It's normal to feel uneasy about the unknown. But if worry is constant, hard to control, interferes with sleep, work or relationships, or causes physical symptoms such as a racing heart or stomach problems for weeks at a time, you may be experiencing an anxiety disorder. Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I hate uncertainty so much?

Some people are naturally more sensitive to uncertainty, influenced by temperament, past experiences and current stress levels. The good news is that tolerance for uncertainty can be increased with practice.

Is it possible to feel comfortable with uncertainty?

Yes. While few people enjoy all uncertainty, many learn to tolerate it and even appreciate the possibilities it brings. Mindfulness, acceptance and gradually facing uncertain situations all help.

How do I stop overthinking about the future?

Use a worry period, write down your thoughts, focus on the next small action, engage in absorbing activities, and bring your attention back to the present through mindfulness or physical activity.

Is uncertainty good for creativity?

Often, yes. Not knowing the answer encourages exploration and new ideas. Many creative breakthroughs come from people willing to work without guaranteed outcomes.

What is intolerance of uncertainty?

It's a tendency to find uncertainty unacceptable and to react to it with strong worry, avoidance or reassurance-seeking. It's linked to anxiety and can be reduced through therapy and practice.

Can exercise really help with uncertainty?

Yes. Physical activity reduces stress hormones, improves sleep and mood, and gives you a sense of control. Even a short walk can ease anxious thoughts.

How do I make a decision when I'm afraid of choosing wrong?

Remember that most decisions can be adjusted later. Clarify what matters most, set a deadline, choose the option that best fits your values, and commit to learning from the outcome either way.

Why does waiting for news feel so hard?

Waiting leaves you in uncertainty without anything to do. Keeping busy with absorbing activities, planning for different outcomes, and limiting how often you check for updates make waiting easier to bear.

Is it normal to feel uncertain even when life is going well?

Yes. Many people feel uneasy when things are good, worrying it won't last. Noticing this pattern and enjoying the present moment without needing guarantees about the future can help.

Can journaling help with uncertainty?

Writing down worries, possible scenarios and next steps helps organise thoughts and often makes problems feel more manageable. Reviewing old entries also shows how many feared outcomes never happened.

Final Thoughts

Uncertainty is a permanent part of life. We can't eliminate it, but we can change our relationship with it. Focus on what you can control, plan for possibilities rather than certainties, take small steps forward, look after your body and mind, and lean on others. Over time, you may discover that the unknown isn't only a source of fear. It's also where growth, opportunity and some of life's best surprises are found.
