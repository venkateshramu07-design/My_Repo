Quick answer: Great leaders surround themselves with worthy people: capable, honest and loyal advisers and team members who share the mission, bring different strengths and are willing to tell the truth. Such allies widen a leader's knowledge, catch mistakes before they become disasters, carry the work when the leader can't, and stand firm in a crisis. The ancient Tamil classic the Thirukkural captures this in a couplet translated as "The king, who knows to live with worthy men allied, has nought to fear from any foeman's pride." To build such alliances, leaders should hire for character as well as competence, welcome dissent, share credit, earn trust and keep investing in relationships.

No leader succeeds alone. Behind every effective head of state, business founder, school principal or community organiser is a circle of people who advise, challenge, support and carry out the work. The quality of that circle often determines whether a leader rises or falls.

This article explores the wisdom of choosing worthy allies, drawing on the Thirukkural, history and modern leadership research. It explains what makes someone a "worthy" ally, why such alliances protect leaders against adversity, the danger of surrounding yourself with flatterers, and practical ways to build and keep a strong team.

The Wisdom of the Thirukkural

The Thirukkural is a classic Tamil text of 1,330 couplets, traditionally attributed to the poet-philosopher Thiruvalluvar. Scholars date it differently, but it is generally thought to be roughly 1,500 to 2,000 years old. It is divided into three books on virtue (aram), wealth and governance (porul), and love (inbam), and its practical guidance on ethics and leadership is still widely quoted.

Chapter 45 of the Thirukkural is devoted to seeking the support of great or worthy people. One of its couplets, Kural 446, appears in G. U. Pope's nineteenth-century English translation as:

"The king, who knows to live with worthy men allied,
Has nought to fear from any foeman's pride."

Other couplets in the same chapter make a related point: a ruler who has trusted counsellors willing to correct him is hard to bring down, while a ruler without people who will speak frankly to him may be ruined even without enemies. The message is timeless: a leader's real protection lies not only in power or wealth but in the integrity and wisdom of the people around them, and in being willing to listen to them.

What Makes Someone a "Worthy" Ally?

  • Integrity: they are honest, ethical and consistent, even when no one is watching.
  • Competence: they have real knowledge, skills and judgement in their areas.
  • Loyalty to the mission: they're committed to shared goals, not just to the leader personally.
  • Courage: they will tell the leader uncomfortable truths.
  • Different perspectives: they bring experiences and viewpoints the leader lacks.
  • Reliability: they deliver on commitments, especially under pressure.
  • Humility: they focus on results rather than personal glory.
  • Emotional steadiness: they stay calm in crises and help others do the same.

Notice that loyalty here means loyalty to the shared purpose and to honest service, not blind obedience. A worthy ally might disagree strongly with the leader precisely because they care about the outcome.

Why Worthy Alliances Protect Leaders

1. They Extend the Leader's Knowledge

No individual can be expert in everything. Strong advisers fill gaps in finance, technology, law, operations, people and strategy, helping leaders make better-informed decisions.

2. They Catch Mistakes Early

Leaders, like everyone, have blind spots. Advisers willing to question assumptions can prevent costly errors before they happen.

3. They Multiply Capacity

Capable allies can lead projects, manage teams and represent the organisation, allowing the leader to focus on priorities. A leader surrounded by weak people ends up doing everything, and eventually fails.

4. They Provide Stability in Crises

When adversity strikes, whether a financial shock, a scandal, a competitor's attack or a disaster, a trusted team can respond quickly and coherently. Allies also provide emotional support, helping leaders stay grounded.

5. They Strengthen Credibility

Stakeholders judge leaders partly by the people around them. Respected, ethical team members increase trust among employees, investors, voters and partners.

6. They Ensure Continuity

Strong teams can continue the work if a leader is absent, ill or moves on. Developing capable successors is one of a leader's most important responsibilities.

7. They Hold Leaders Accountable

Power can tempt people toward arrogance or unethical shortcuts. Worthy allies help keep leaders honest.

The Danger of Surrounding Yourself With Flatterers

History is full of leaders brought down not by enemies but by the people around them: flatterers who told them only what they wanted to hear, self-serving advisers who pursued their own interests, or "yes men" who never raised concerns.

  • Groupthink: psychologist Irving Janis coined the term "groupthink" to describe how cohesive groups can suppress dissent and make poor decisions. He studied cases such as the 1961 Bay of Pigs invasion, where advisers to US President John F. Kennedy failed to challenge a flawed plan.
  • Echo chambers: leaders who hear only agreement lose touch with reality.
  • Loyalty over competence: appointing people for personal loyalty rather than ability weakens organisations.
  • Fear cultures: when people are punished for bad news, problems stay hidden until they explode.

Examples From History

Chandragupta Maurya and Chanakya

In Indian tradition, the founder of the Maurya Empire, Chandragupta Maurya, is closely associated with his adviser Chanakya, also known as Kautilya, to whom the Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft, is traditionally attributed. Their partnership is often cited as an example of a ruler's success resting on a wise and strategic counsellor, though historians debate many details of the traditional accounts.

Akbar's Court

The Mughal emperor Akbar gathered talented people from different backgrounds at his court. His revenue minister Raja Todar Mal reformed land revenue administration, his friend and historian Abul Fazl chronicled the reign, and figures such as Birbal and the musician Tansen became famous. Akbar's willingness to listen to people of different faiths and views is often seen as one of the strengths of his reign.

Abraham Lincoln's "Team of Rivals"

Historian Doris Kearns Goodwin's book Team of Rivals describes how US President Abraham Lincoln appointed several of his former political rivals to his cabinet during the Civil War. Despite their egos and disagreements, their abilities strengthened his administration, and Lincoln's skill in managing them is widely admired.

Lessons From the Indian Epics: Ignoring Good Counsel

The epics offer vivid examples of what happens when leaders ignore worthy advisers.

  • Ravana and Vibhishana: in the Ramayana, Ravana's brother Vibhishana urges him to return Sita and avoid war. Ravana angrily rejects the advice and drives Vibhishana away. Vibhishana joins Rama, and Ravana's kingdom is ultimately destroyed. The story is often read as a warning about leaders who silence honest counsel.
  • Dhritarashtra and Vidura: in the Mahabharata, the wise minister Vidura repeatedly advises King Dhritarashtra to act justly toward the Pandavas and restrain his son Duryodhana. Dhritarashtra listens but doesn't act, swayed by attachment to his son. The result is the catastrophic war of Kurukshetra. Vidura's counsel, collected as the Vidura Niti, is still read for its guidance on ethics and statecraft.

Both stories make the same point as the Thirukkural: having worthy advisers is not enough. A leader must also have the humility to listen to them and the courage to act on their advice.

Other Leadership Themes in the Thirukkural

The Thirukkural's section on wealth and governance contains many chapters relevant to leaders. Alongside the chapter on seeking the support of worthy people, there are chapters on avoiding the company of the base or mean-minded, on carefully testing people before trusting them, and on assigning work to those whose abilities match the task. Taken together, they describe a leader who chooses people thoughtfully, tests their character, places them in suitable roles, keeps away from corrupting influences and listens to frank advice. This guidance, written for kings, applies just as well to managers, entrepreneurs and community leaders today.

What Modern Leadership Research Says

  • "First who, then what": in Good to Great, Jim Collins argued that companies that made the leap to sustained success first got "the right people on the bus" before deciding the direction.
  • Psychological safety: Amy Edmondson's research shows that teams perform and learn better when members feel safe to speak up, admit mistakes and challenge ideas.
  • Diverse teams: research suggests diverse teams can make better decisions and be more innovative, especially when inclusion is managed well, because they consider more perspectives and scrutinise facts more carefully.
  • Trust: Patrick Lencioni's model of team dysfunction identifies absence of trust as the foundation of many team problems.

How to Build a Circle of Worthy Allies

1. Hire for Character and Competence

Skills can be taught; integrity is harder to instil. Look for evidence of honesty, reliability and values alongside expertise. Check references carefully.

2. Seek People Different From You

Choose people with different backgrounds, skills and thinking styles. If everyone thinks like you, you don't need them.

3. Welcome Disagreement

Explicitly invite dissent: "What am I missing?" "Who disagrees, and why?" Reward people who raise concerns respectfully.

4. Earn Trust Through Your Own Behaviour

Be honest, keep promises, admit mistakes and treat people fairly. Worthy people won't stay with an unworthy leader.

5. Share Credit and Take Responsibility

Give credit generously for successes and accept responsibility for failures. This builds loyalty.

6. Empower and Develop People

Give allies real responsibility, decision-making power and opportunities to grow.

7. Build Networks Beyond Your Organisation

Mentors, peers in other organisations, advisory boards and coaches offer outside perspectives.

8. Remove Toxic Influences

Even talented people can damage a team through dishonesty, bullying or self-interest. Address such behaviour quickly.

9. Invest in Relationships

Spend time with your team beyond formal meetings. Understand their goals, challenges and lives.

How Allies Help a Leader Through a Crisis

Adversity tests alliances. A crisis typically moves through stages, and worthy allies contribute at each one.

  • Early warning: people close to customers, operations or finances often see trouble first. If they trust the leader, they raise concerns early, when problems are still manageable.
  • Assessment: experts in different areas help the leader understand what's really happening, separating facts from rumours.
  • Decision: advisers present options and risks honestly, including uncomfortable ones, so the leader can choose wisely.
  • Action: capable team members take charge of different parts of the response, from communications to operations, without waiting to be told every step.
  • Morale: steady, respected allies reassure staff and stakeholders, preventing panic.
  • Learning: after the crisis, honest allies help review what went wrong and how to prevent it next time.

Leaders surrounded by flatterers often fail at the very first stage: they don't hear bad news until it's too late.

An Illustrative Example

Imagine the founder of a growing food-delivery startup. In the early days, they hired close friends who were enthusiastic and loyal but who rarely questioned decisions. As the company expanded, they began adding experienced people: a finance head who had worked through a previous downturn, an operations manager from a logistics company and an independent board member known for blunt advice.

A year later, the finance head warned that the company's discount-heavy growth was burning cash far faster than planned. The founder initially resisted, but the board member backed the warning with data. Together, the team cut unprofitable promotions, renegotiated supplier contracts and focused on the most profitable cities. When a funding slowdown hit the sector months later, several competitors shut down, but this company survived.

The founder later reflected that the most valuable people weren't those who always agreed but those who cared enough to disagree. This example is illustrative, but it reflects a common story in business: honest, experienced allies are often the difference between surviving and failing in hard times.

Building Your Personal "Board of Advisers"

You don't need to run a company or country to benefit from worthy allies. Anyone can build a personal circle of advisers:

  • A mentor who has walked the path you're on and can offer guidance.
  • A peer at a similar stage who understands your challenges.
  • A truth-teller who will give you honest feedback, even when it's hard to hear.
  • A connector who can introduce you to people and opportunities.
  • A specialist in an area you lack, such as finance, law or health.
  • An encourager who believes in you during setbacks.

Nurture these relationships by offering help in return, keeping in touch regularly and showing appreciation.

Being a Worthy Ally Yourself

Leadership wisdom often focuses on choosing good followers, but the reverse matters too. Organisations need people who are worthy allies to their leaders and colleagues:

  • Speak up respectfully when you see problems.
  • Deliver on your commitments.
  • Support decisions once they're made, even if you argued for a different option, unless they're unethical.
  • Share credit and help colleagues succeed.
  • Stay honest, even when it's inconvenient.
  • Keep developing your skills so you can contribute more.

The best alliances are built by people who are both good leaders and good allies.

How to Keep Worthy People

Attracting good people is only half the task; keeping them is the other half. Worthy allies leave when they feel unheard, undervalued or asked to compromise their integrity. To retain them, involve them in important decisions, recognise their contributions publicly, give them room to grow, pay them fairly, protect them from politics and blame, and stay true to the values that drew them in. A leader's consistency over time is what turns talented individuals into a loyal, lasting alliance.

Diversity: Strength and Challenge

A diverse circle of allies brings broader knowledge and creativity, but it also requires skill to manage. Different perspectives can lead to conflict, misunderstandings and slower decisions. Leaders should establish shared values and goals, create norms for respectful debate, ensure everyone's voice is heard, and make final decisions clearly after hearing all views.

Practical Habits for Hearing the Truth

Even leaders with good intentions can become isolated as they gain power, because people naturally hesitate to challenge someone senior. These habits help keep honest information flowing:

  • Speak last in meetings. If the leader shares their view first, others tend to agree. Asking for others' opinions first surfaces more honest views.
  • Assign a devil's advocate. For major decisions, ask someone to argue the opposite case deliberately.
  • Hold a "pre-mortem". Psychologist Gary Klein's technique asks the team to imagine that a plan has failed in the future and explain why. This makes it easier to voice doubts.
  • Go to the front line. Talk directly with customers and front-line staff rather than relying only on reports.
  • Use anonymous channels. Surveys and suggestion systems can reveal concerns people won't raise openly.
  • Thank the messenger. When someone brings bad news, thank them publicly. Everyone will notice.

Signs You Have the Wrong People Around You

  • No one ever disagrees with you.
  • You hear about problems only when they're crises.
  • Team members compete for your favour rather than collaborating.
  • People blame others rather than taking responsibility.
  • Talented people keep leaving.
  • Decisions are driven by politics rather than evidence.

Worthy Allies in Everyday Life

The principle applies far beyond palaces and boardrooms.

  • Families: parents who seek advice from experienced relatives, friends or professionals often make better decisions about education, finances and health.
  • Students: study groups of motivated, honest peers help each other learn and stay on track, while the wrong company can pull you away from your goals.
  • Small businesses: a trustworthy accountant, a reliable supplier and an experienced mentor can protect a business from costly mistakes.
  • Community leaders: volunteers and organisers succeed when they build teams of committed, honest people rather than relying on a few personalities.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why should leaders surround themselves with worthy people?

Because capable, honest allies extend a leader's knowledge, catch mistakes, share the workload, provide stability in crises and help keep the leader accountable.

What does the Thirukkural say about leaders and allies?

Chapter 45 of the Thirukkural advises rulers to seek the support of wise and worthy people. Kural 446, in G. U. Pope's translation, says a king allied with worthy men need not fear any enemy's pride.

How can a leader avoid "yes men"?

Hire people with independent judgement, invite dissent, reward honesty, and never punish people for respectfully delivering bad news.

What qualities make a good adviser?

Integrity, expertise, courage to speak frankly, commitment to shared goals, reliability and calm judgement.

Can loyalty be a weakness in leadership?

Yes, when loyalty to a person replaces loyalty to the truth or the organisation's mission. Leaders should value people who are loyal to shared goals and willing to challenge them, rather than those who simply agree.

How do I find good mentors?

Look within your organisation, professional associations, alumni networks and industry events. Approach people with specific questions, respect their time, and build the relationship gradually.

Who translated the Thirukkural into English?

There are many translations. G. U. Pope's 1886 verse translation is one of the best known, and others include translations by V. V. S. Aiyar, P. S. Sundaram and many more modern scholars.

How can a new leader identify worthy people in an existing team?

Spend the first weeks listening. Notice who gives honest answers, who delivers consistently, who colleagues turn to for help and who raises problems constructively. Test trust gradually by giving responsibility and observing how people handle it.

Is it wise to appoint friends to key positions?

Only if they're genuinely the best people for the role and can be held to the same standards as anyone else. Appointing friends for loyalty alone can weaken teams and damage trust.

Final Thoughts

The Thirukkural's insight, offered many centuries ago, remains strikingly relevant: a leader's greatest protection is the company they keep. Worthy allies, honest, capable and committed to a shared purpose, make leaders wiser, stronger and more resilient. But building such alliances requires the leader to be worthy too: to listen, to share credit, to accept criticism and to put the mission above ego. Leaders who do this rarely stand alone when adversity comes.

If this article resonated with you, share it with a leader who values the people around them.
