Quick answer: To socialise more easily, start small and practise regularly: smile, make eye contact and greet people; ask open-ended questions and listen with genuine curiosity; share a little about yourself; and follow up with people you click with. Join regular activities like clubs, classes, sports, volunteering or faith and community groups, because repeated contact is how acquaintances become friends. If you feel shy or anxious, prepare a few conversation starters, focus on the other person rather than on yourself, and remember that most people are as nervous as you are. Social skills improve with practice, like any other skill.

Humans are social creatures. Friendships and community bring joy, support and a sense of belonging, and they're strongly linked to health and happiness. Yet many people find socialising difficult. Shyness, social anxiety, busy schedules, moving to a new city or spending most of the day online can make connecting with others feel hard.

The good news is that socialising is a skill that anyone can improve. This guide explains why social connection matters, practical techniques for starting and keeping conversations going, how to make friends as an adult, tips for introverts and people with social anxiety, and how to build lasting relationships.

Why Socialising Matters

Strong social connections are among the most powerful predictors of wellbeing:

  • The Harvard Study of Adult Development, which has followed participants since 1938, found that close relationships were among the strongest predictors of happiness and health in later life.
  • A widely cited analysis by Julianne Holt-Lunstad and colleagues, covering data from over 300,000 people, found that strong social relationships were associated with a significantly higher likelihood of survival over the study periods.
  • In 2023, the US Surgeon General issued an advisory describing an "epidemic of loneliness and isolation" and its links to heart disease, depression, anxiety and dementia.
  • The World Health Organization launched a Commission on Social Connection in 2023, recognising loneliness as a global public health concern.

Social connection also helps careers, since many opportunities come through people we know, and it gives us support during difficult times.

Why Socialising Can Feel Hard

  • Shyness: feeling uncomfortable or self-conscious in social situations, especially with new people.
  • Social anxiety: a more intense fear of being judged, embarrassed or rejected, which can lead to avoiding social situations.
  • Introversion: introverts recharge through time alone and may find large groups draining, though they often enjoy deep one-to-one conversations.
  • Life transitions: moving cities, starting university, changing jobs, becoming a parent or retiring can disrupt social networks.
  • Busy schedules that leave little time for friends.
  • Digital habits: online interaction can't fully replace in-person connection, and heavy social media use may increase comparison and feelings of isolation.

The Basics: Body Language and First Impressions

Much of social communication is non-verbal. Small changes make you appear more approachable:

  • Smile genuinely when greeting people.
  • Make comfortable eye contact, without staring.
  • Keep an open posture: uncrossed arms, facing the person, relaxed shoulders.
  • Put your phone away during conversations.
  • Nod and use small acknowledgements like "I see" or "That's interesting" to show you're listening.
  • Remember and use names. Repeating someone's name when you meet them helps you remember it and makes them feel valued.

How to Start a Conversation

Starting is often the hardest part. Simple, low-pressure openers work best:

  • Comment on your shared situation: "This is a great event. How do you know the host?" or "Have you been to this class before?"
  • Ask for a small opinion or recommendation: "Is the coffee here any good?" or "Have you read anything good lately?"
  • Give a genuine compliment: "That's a great presentation; how did you get into that topic?"
  • Introduce yourself simply: "Hi, I'm Priya. I just joined the team."

Don't worry about being witty. Most people appreciate friendly, simple openers.

How to Keep a Conversation Going

1. Ask Open-Ended Questions

Questions that can't be answered with "yes" or "no" invite people to share more. Instead of "Do you like your job?", try "What do you enjoy most about your work?" Research by Harvard psychologists Karen Huang, Alison Wood Brooks and colleagues found that people who asked more questions, especially follow-up questions, were rated as more likeable by conversation partners.

2. Listen Actively

Listen to understand, not just to reply. Show interest with follow-up questions, summarise what you heard ("So you moved here for the job?"), and avoid interrupting.

3. Share About Yourself

Conversations work best as an exchange. Share your own experiences, opinions and stories, building on what the other person says. Gradual, mutual self-disclosure is how closeness develops. In a famous 1997 study, psychologist Arthur Aron and colleagues found that pairs of strangers who took turns answering increasingly personal questions felt significantly closer after 45 minutes.

4. Find Common Ground

Look for shared interests, experiences or values: hometowns, hobbies, films, sports, food, travel or work. Common ground creates connection.

5. Use the "FORD" Topics

If you're stuck, the acronym FORD offers safe, engaging topics: Family, Occupation, Recreation and Dreams.

6. Avoid Common Conversation Killers

  • Talking only about yourself.
  • Constant complaining or negativity.
  • Controversial topics with people you've just met, unless the setting invites them.
  • Checking your phone.
  • One-word answers.
  • "One-upping" other people's stories.

7. End Gracefully

Ending conversations well is part of good social skills. Try: "It's been great talking with you. I'm going to grab a drink, but let's catch up later," or "I'd love to continue this. Can I get your number?"

A Sample Conversation

Here's how a simple conversation at a workshop might flow using these techniques:

  • You: "Hi, I'm Arjun. Is this your first time at one of these photography workshops?"
  • Them: "Yes, I'm Meena. I just bought a camera and don't really know what I'm doing yet."
  • You: "Same here, I've mostly used my phone. What made you want to get into photography?"
  • Them: "I travel a lot for work and wanted better pictures of the places I visit."
  • You: "That sounds great. Where was your favourite trip recently?"
  • Them: "Probably Hampi. The ruins at sunset were amazing."
  • You: "I've always wanted to go there. I went to Jaisalmer last year and loved photographing the fort. Maybe we can compare notes after the session?"

Notice the pattern: a simple opener, open-ended questions, active listening, sharing something about yourself, finding common ground and suggesting a follow-up. None of it requires special charm, just curiosity and friendliness.

Joining and Including Others in Group Conversations

Group conversations can feel trickier than one-to-one chats.

  • Joining a group: stand nearby, listen for a moment, make eye contact, and add a relevant comment or question when there's a natural pause.
  • Contributing: build on what others say rather than abruptly changing the topic.
  • Including others: if someone is standing at the edge, invite them in: "Have you met Rahul? He was just telling us about his trek."
  • Sharing the floor: notice who hasn't spoken and ask for their opinion.

Being the person who includes others is one of the quickest ways to be liked and remembered.

How to Make Friends as an Adult

Making friends after school or college can be harder, because we don't automatically see the same people every day. Research by Jeffrey Hall at the University of Kansas estimated that it takes roughly 50 hours of time together to move from acquaintance to casual friend, around 90 hours to become a friend, and more than 200 hours to become a close friend. Friendship requires repeated time together.

Where to Meet People

  • Classes and workshops: cooking, dance, languages, art, music or fitness.
  • Sports and fitness groups: running clubs, cycling groups, yoga classes, badminton, cricket or football leagues.
  • Volunteering: NGOs, animal shelters, community clean-ups, teaching programmes.
  • Hobby groups and clubs: book clubs, trekking groups, photography clubs, board game meetups.
  • Faith and cultural communities.
  • Work: lunch with colleagues, office clubs, team events.
  • Neighbourhood and residents' associations.
  • Alumni networks from school or college.
  • Apps and online platforms designed for meeting people with shared interests, used safely.

Turning Acquaintances Into Friends

  1. Show up regularly to the same activities.
  2. Take the initiative: suggest coffee, a walk or a meal after an activity.
  3. Follow up: send a message to continue a conversation or share something relevant.
  4. Be consistent: regular contact matters more than grand gestures.
  5. Be patient: friendships take time to develop.

Research by Wendy Liu, Erica Boothby and colleagues on the "liking gap" found that after conversations with strangers, people consistently underestimated how much their conversation partner liked them. In other words, people probably like you more than you think, so reaching out again is usually welcome.

Tips for Introverts

  • Choose settings that suit you: small groups or one-to-one meetings rather than large parties.
  • Arrive early to events, when fewer people are present and conversations are easier.
  • Plan recovery time after social events.
  • Play to your strengths: introverts are often excellent listeners and enjoy meaningful conversations.
  • Set small goals: talk to two new people, then you can leave.
  • Find activity-based socialising, such as hiking or classes, where the activity provides structure and conversation flows naturally.

Overcoming Shyness and Social Anxiety

  1. Start small: greet a neighbour, chat with a shopkeeper, or ask a colleague about their weekend.
  2. Gradually face feared situations: step by step exposure is one of the most effective ways to reduce social anxiety.
  3. Challenge negative thoughts: replace "Everyone will think I'm boring" with "Most people are focused on themselves, not judging me."
  4. Remember the spotlight effect: research by Thomas Gilovich found people overestimate how much others notice their appearance and behaviour.
  5. Prepare a few topics or questions in advance.
  6. Focus outward: concentrating on the other person reduces self-consciousness.
  7. Use calming techniques such as slow breathing before and during social situations.
  8. Seek professional help if anxiety is severe or persistent. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is an effective treatment for social anxiety disorder.

Socialising at Events and Parties

  • Set a simple goal: meet three new people or have one meaningful conversation.
  • Arrive with a friend if helpful, but avoid spending the whole event only with them.
  • Stand near the food or drinks area, where conversations start naturally.
  • Help the host by offering to greet people or serve food, which gives you a role and easy reasons to talk.
  • Exchange contacts with people you connect with, and follow up within a few days.
  • Leave on a good note, thanking the host and saying goodbye to people you spoke with.

The Role of Humour

Light, friendly humour helps people relax and bond. You don't need to be a comedian; laughing at shared situations, gently poking fun at yourself, and enjoying others' jokes are enough. Avoid humour that targets people's appearance, background or beliefs, and be mindful that sarcasm can be misunderstood, especially with people you've just met or across cultures.

Socialising at Work

  • Greet colleagues and learn their names.
  • Join lunches, coffee breaks and team events.
  • Offer help and share credit.
  • Ask colleagues about their work and interests.
  • Be reliable and positive.
  • Keep conversations professional and avoid gossip.

Good workplace relationships make work more enjoyable and can boost your career.

Socialising Online: Benefits and Limits

Online communities can help people find others with shared interests, stay connected with distant friends, and offer support for niche hobbies or experiences. However, research suggests that in-person interaction provides benefits that online contact alone often doesn't. Use online tools to start or maintain connections, and move to video calls or in-person meetings when possible and safe. When meeting people from the internet, meet in public places and tell someone where you'll be.

Helping Children and Teenagers Build Social Skills

  • Arrange regular playdates and group activities for younger children.
  • Role-play situations such as introducing themselves, joining a game or handling disagreements.
  • Encourage team activities like sports, music, drama or scouting.
  • Model good social behaviour: children learn by watching how adults greet, listen and resolve conflicts.
  • Talk about feelings and empathy: "How do you think your friend felt when that happened?"
  • Balance screen time with face-to-face interaction.
  • Support shy children gently without forcing them or labelling them as "shy" in front of others.

Being a Good Friend

Socialising isn't only about meeting new people; it's also about being someone others want to be around. Qualities people value in friends include:

  • Reliability: keeping plans and promises.
  • Loyalty: speaking well of friends when they're not there.
  • Good listening: giving full attention.
  • Encouragement: supporting their goals.
  • Honesty with kindness.
  • Fun: sharing laughter and good experiences.

Maintaining Friendships

  • Stay in touch regularly through calls, messages and meetups.
  • Remember important events such as birthdays, job changes and family occasions.
  • Be there in hard times, not just good ones.
  • Celebrate their successes genuinely.
  • Respect boundaries and be understanding when people are busy.
  • Resolve conflicts calmly and apologise when needed.

Loneliness: When to Seek Help

Everyone feels lonely sometimes, especially during transitions. But long-term loneliness can affect mood, sleep and health. If you feel persistently isolated, sad or hopeless, consider:

  • Reaching out to an old friend or family member, even if it's been a long time.
  • Joining a support group or community organisation.
  • Talking to a counsellor or therapist.
  • In India, calling Tele-MANAS on 14416 for free mental health support.

Asking for help is a sign of strength, and many people are glad to reconnect.

Cultural Differences in Socialising

Social norms vary across cultures. Eye contact, personal space, physical greetings, humour and directness differ between countries and communities. In multicultural settings, such as workplaces in big Indian cities or abroad, observe how others interact, ask politely when unsure, and stay respectful and curious about differences. Showing genuine interest in someone's background is often a great conversation starter.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is it so hard to make friends as an adult?

Adults have less unstructured time and fewer natural settings for repeated contact. Joining regular activities and taking initiative helps overcome this.

How do I stop being awkward in conversations?

Practise regularly, ask open-ended questions, listen carefully and focus on the other person. Awkward moments happen to everyone; most people forget them quickly.

Is it okay to prefer being alone?

Yes. Enjoying solitude is healthy. What matters is having enough meaningful connection for you. Feeling persistently lonely, however, is a sign to reach out.

How can I socialise if I've just moved to a new city?

Join interest-based groups, explore community events, connect with colleagues, use alumni networks, and say yes to invitations, even if you feel tired or nervous at first.

What if I'm rejected?

Rejection is part of socialising and usually isn't personal. People may be busy or distracted. Keep trying with different people and groups.

How do I remember people's names?

Repeat the name when you're introduced ("Nice to meet you, Kavya"), use it once or twice in conversation, and link it to something memorable about the person. Writing it down afterwards also helps.

What should I talk about with someone I've just met?

Start with the shared context, such as the event or place, then explore their work, interests, travels, favourite food or recent books and films. Follow their lead and ask follow-up questions.

How many friends do I need?

There's no ideal number. Research suggests that a few close, supportive relationships matter more for wellbeing than a large number of casual acquaintances.

Can social skills really be learned?

Yes. Like any skill, they improve with practice, feedback and patience. Many confident socialisers were once shy.

Final Thoughts

Socialising doesn't require being the life of the party. It's about showing genuine interest in others, being yourself and giving relationships time to grow. Start with small steps: a smile, a greeting, a question. Join activities where you'll see the same people regularly, follow up with those you connect with, and be patient. With practice, socialising becomes easier and more enjoyable, and the friendships you build can enrich every part of your life.

If you found these tips helpful, share them with someone who'd like to build more connections.
