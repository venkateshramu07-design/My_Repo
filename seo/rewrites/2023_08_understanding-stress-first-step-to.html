Updated 20 October 2026

Quick answer: Stress is the body and mind's response to demands or threats that challenge our ability to cope. It activates the nervous system and hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol, preparing us to act. Short-term stress can be helpful, sharpening focus and energy, but chronic stress, when demands continue without enough recovery, can harm sleep, mood, immunity, digestion, heart health and relationships. Understanding stress, including what triggers it for you, how your body reacts and how your thinking shapes it, is the first step toward managing it. A truly stress-free life isn't realistic, but a life with less harmful stress and better recovery is achievable.

"I'm so stressed" is one of the most common phrases in modern life. We use it to describe deadlines, traffic, exams, money worries, family conflicts and the general feeling of having too much to do. Yet many people don't understand what stress actually is, why it affects them so strongly, or why it sometimes helps and sometimes harms.

This article explains the science of stress: what happens in your body and brain, the different types of stress, common causes, how stress affects health, why people react differently, and how understanding your stress can help you manage it more effectively.

What Is Stress?

Stress is the response of the body and mind to any demand or change that requires adjustment. The demand itself is called a stressor; the reaction is the stress response.

The modern concept of stress was popularised by Hungarian-Canadian endocrinologist Hans Selye in the 1930s. Through experiments, he observed that the body responds to many different demands, such as injury, cold or fatigue, with a similar general pattern. He also coined the term eustress for positive, motivating stress, distinguishing it from distress, harmful stress.

Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome

Selye described three stages of the body's response to ongoing stress:

  1. Alarm: the body recognises a threat and activates the fight-or-flight response, releasing stress hormones.
  2. Resistance: if the stressor continues, the body adapts and tries to cope, maintaining a heightened state of readiness.
  3. Exhaustion: if stress persists for a long time without relief, the body's resources become depleted, increasing vulnerability to illness and burnout.

What Happens in Your Body

The Fast Response: Sympathetic Nervous System

When the brain perceives a threat, the hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system. The adrenal glands release adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline, which:

  • Increase heart rate and blood pressure
  • Speed up breathing
  • Release glucose for energy
  • Tense muscles
  • Sharpen alertness
  • Slow digestion

The Slower Response: The HPA Axis

The hypothalamus also triggers the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. The pituitary gland signals the adrenal glands to release cortisol, which keeps the body alert, raises blood sugar and influences immune function. Normally, cortisol levels fall once the threat passes.

The Recovery: Parasympathetic Nervous System

The parasympathetic nervous system, sometimes called the "rest and digest" system, calms the body after stress, slowing heart rate and restoring digestion. Healthy stress involves activation followed by recovery.

Allostatic Load: When Stress Accumulates

Neuroscientist Bruce McEwen introduced the concept of allostatic load, the cumulative wear and tear on the body from repeated or chronic stress. When the stress response is activated too often, lasts too long, or doesn't switch off properly, it can contribute to high blood pressure, metabolic changes, weakened immunity and changes in brain regions involved in memory and emotion.

Common Causes of Stress

  • Work: workload, deadlines, job insecurity, conflict.
  • Money: debt, low income, unexpected expenses.
  • Relationships: conflict, loneliness, divorce.
  • Health: illness, pain, a loved one's health problems.
  • Life changes: moving, marriage, having a baby, retirement.
  • Education: exams, competition, academic pressure.
  • Environment: noise, crowding, pollution, long commutes.
  • Daily hassles: small irritations that accumulate.
  • Internal factors: perfectionism, worry, unrealistic expectations.

Life Events and Stress

In 1967, psychiatrists Thomas Holmes and Richard Rahe developed the Social Readjustment Rating Scale, which assigned values to life events based on how much adjustment they required. The death of a spouse ranked highest, followed by events such as divorce, marital separation and imprisonment. Even positive events, such as marriage or a new job, scored as stressful because they require change. The scale was an early attempt to link accumulated life changes with risk of illness.

Why People React Differently to Stress

Two people can face the same situation, such as a job interview, and experience very different levels of stress. Psychologists Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman's transactional model of stress explains why. Stress depends on two appraisals:

  • Primary appraisal: "Is this situation a threat, a challenge or irrelevant to me?"
  • Secondary appraisal: "Do I have the resources to cope with it?"

When a situation seems threatening and resources seem inadequate, stress is high. When it seems like a challenge we can handle, stress may even feel energising. Factors influencing these appraisals include personality, past experiences, skills, social support, health and beliefs about stress.

How Stress Affects Health

Physical Effects

  • Headaches and muscle tension
  • Sleep problems
  • Digestive issues such as stomach aches and irritable bowel symptoms
  • High blood pressure and increased cardiovascular risk
  • Weakened immunity
  • Fatigue

In a well-known study published in 1991, psychologist Sheldon Cohen and colleagues exposed volunteers to common cold viruses and found that people reporting higher psychological stress were more likely to develop colds, supporting a link between stress and immune function.

Mental and Emotional Effects

  • Anxiety, irritability and low mood
  • Difficulty concentrating and making decisions
  • Memory problems
  • Feeling overwhelmed
  • Increased risk of depression and anxiety disorders with chronic stress

Behavioural Effects

  • Changes in appetite
  • Increased use of alcohol, tobacco or caffeine
  • Withdrawal from social activities
  • Procrastination
  • Conflict in relationships

Stress and Ageing

Research led by Elissa Epel and Elizabeth Blackburn found that mothers caring for chronically ill children who reported the highest stress had shorter telomeres, protective caps on chromosomes associated with cellular ageing. This suggests that chronic stress may affect the body at a cellular level.

The Brain Under Stress

Stress changes how the brain works. Research by neuroscientist Amy Arnsten has shown that even mild acute stress can impair the prefrontal cortex, the region responsible for planning, reasoning, impulse control and working memory, while strengthening more reflexive responses driven by the amygdala. This helps explain why people under stress often struggle to think clearly, make impulsive decisions or react emotionally. Chronic stress has been associated with changes in brain regions involved in memory and emotion, though many of these changes may be reversible with recovery.

Stress and the Gut

The brain and the digestive system communicate constantly through nerves, hormones and immune signals, a connection often called the gut-brain axis. Stress can cause butterflies, nausea, changes in appetite, indigestion and bowel changes, and can worsen conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome. Gut problems, in turn, can increase discomfort and anxiety. Managing stress often improves digestive symptoms, and vice versa.

Stress and the Heart

The large international INTERHEART study, published in The Lancet in 2004, examined risk factors for heart attacks in thousands of people across 52 countries. It found that people reporting permanent stress at work or at home had roughly double the odds of a heart attack compared with those reporting no stress, after accounting for other factors. Chronic stress may contribute through raised blood pressure, inflammation and unhealthy coping behaviours such as smoking or poor diet.

Good Stress vs Bad Stress

Not all stress is harmful. The Yerkes-Dodson principle suggests that moderate arousal can improve performance, while too little leads to boredom and too much impairs functioning. Eustress can motivate learning, achievement and growth. The key differences between helpful and harmful stress are intensity, duration, sense of control and opportunities for recovery.

Two Ways of Coping

Lazarus and Folkman described two broad coping styles:

  • Problem-focused coping: tackling the stressor directly, such as making a plan, seeking information, managing time or asking for help. This works well when the situation can be changed.
  • Emotion-focused coping: managing your emotional reaction, such as relaxation, talking to friends, reframing or acceptance. This is especially useful when the situation can't be changed.

Effective coping usually combines both. Some emotion-focused strategies, such as avoidance, denial or substance use, may offer short-term relief but worsen problems over time.

Building Stress Resilience

  • Strong relationships: social support buffers the effects of stress.
  • Healthy routines: regular sleep, exercise and meals keep the body's stress systems balanced.
  • A sense of control: focusing on what you can influence reduces helplessness.
  • Purpose and meaning: knowing why you're facing a challenge makes it easier to endure.
  • Flexible thinking: seeing setbacks as temporary and solvable.
  • Regular recovery: daily moments of rest and relaxation prevent stress from accumulating.

Recognising Your Personal Stress Signals

Understanding your own stress begins with noticing your warning signs. Ask yourself:

  • What physical symptoms appear when I'm stressed: tight shoulders, headaches, stomach problems?
  • How does my mood change: irritable, anxious, withdrawn?
  • How does my behaviour change: snacking, scrolling, snapping at people, procrastinating?
  • What situations, people or thoughts most often trigger stress?

Keeping a stress diary for a week or two can reveal patterns you might otherwise miss.

Measuring Stress: The Perceived Stress Scale

Psychologists often measure stress with the Perceived Stress Scale, developed by Sheldon Cohen and colleagues in 1983. Rather than counting events, it asks how often people have felt that their lives were unpredictable, uncontrollable or overloaded in the past month. Questions include how often you've felt nervous and stressed, felt unable to control important things in your life, or felt difficulties piling up so high you couldn't overcome them. This approach reflects an important insight: stress depends not only on what happens to us but on how we perceive our ability to cope.

The Stress-Sleep Cycle

Stress and sleep influence each other powerfully. Stress raises arousal and cortisol, making it harder to fall asleep and stay asleep. Poor sleep, in turn, makes the brain more reactive to stress and reduces the ability to regulate emotions. This can create a vicious cycle. Protecting sleep, with regular sleep times, a calming pre-bed routine, limited screens and caffeine, and writing down worries before bed, is one of the most effective ways to break it.

Stress Across Different Groups

  • Children: may show stress through stomach aches, sleep problems, clinginess, irritability or changes in behaviour, rather than describing it in words.
  • Teenagers: face academic pressure, social media, peer relationships and identity questions. Exam stress is particularly common in India.
  • Working adults: juggle jobs, finances, family responsibilities and often caregiving.
  • Women: may carry a disproportionate share of household work and caregiving alongside paid work, increasing stress load.
  • Older adults: may face health problems, loss, loneliness and financial concerns.

Stress in Modern Indian Life

Rapid urbanisation, long commutes, competitive education, high housing costs, changing family structures and the pressure to succeed contribute to stress for many Indians. Joint families can provide support but also tension, while nuclear families may lack nearby help. At the same time, Indian traditions offer valuable resources for managing stress, such as yoga, pranayama breathing, meditation, strong community ties and festivals that bring people together. Combining these traditions with modern understanding of stress can be powerful.

An Illustrative Example

Imagine a young professional who frequently gets headaches, sleeps poorly and snaps at family members. They assume they're just tired. After keeping a stress diary for two weeks, they notice that headaches appear on days with back-to-back meetings and late-night emails, and that Sunday evenings bring a knot in the stomach as they think about the week ahead.

Understanding these patterns helps them act. They block short breaks between meetings, stop checking email after 9 pm, and spend Sunday evenings planning the week briefly and then doing something relaxing. They also start walking each morning. Within a month, headaches are less frequent and sleep improves. This example is illustrative, but it shows how recognising your stress signals and triggers is the first step toward meaningful change.

From Understanding to Managing Stress

Once you understand your stress, you can respond more effectively:

  • Change the stressor where possible: solve problems, set boundaries, ask for help.
  • Change your appraisal: view challenges as opportunities and remind yourself of your resources.
  • Strengthen your resources: sleep, exercise, nutrition, skills and social support.
  • Activate recovery: slow breathing, relaxation, mindfulness, nature and hobbies.
  • Accept what you can't change and focus energy elsewhere.

Quick Ways to Activate Your Body's Calming System

Because the parasympathetic nervous system is responsible for recovery, techniques that activate it can quickly reduce the physical effects of stress:

  • Slow breathing: breathe in for about four seconds and out for about six, for a few minutes.
  • Physiological sigh: a double inhale through the nose followed by a long exhale, which some research suggests can rapidly reduce arousal.
  • Progressive muscle relaxation: tense and release muscle groups one by one.
  • Time in nature: even a short walk among trees or near water can help.
  • Social connection: a warm conversation or hug can calm the stress response.
  • Movement: exercise uses up stress hormones and improves mood.

Myths About Stress

  • "Stress is always bad for you." Short-term stress can enhance performance and growth.
  • "If I don't feel stressed, I'm not stressed." Stress can show up physically or behaviourally before you consciously notice it.
  • "Only major events cause stress." Daily hassles can add up to significant stress.
  • "Successful people don't get stressed." Everyone experiences stress; successful people often manage recovery well.
  • "Relaxing means doing nothing." Active recovery, like exercise and hobbies, can be more restorative than passive rest.

When to Seek Help

Talk to a doctor or mental health professional if stress is persistent and overwhelming, interferes with work, sleep or relationships, causes physical symptoms such as chest pain or frequent illness, or leads to hopelessness, panic attacks or thoughts of self-harm. In India, Tele-MANAS (14416) provides free mental health support. In an emergency, contact emergency services immediately.

Key Takeaways

  • Stress is a natural response to demands, involving the nervous system and hormones like adrenaline and cortisol.
  • Short-term stress can help; chronic stress without recovery harms body and mind.
  • How you appraise a situation, as a threat or a challenge, strongly influences your stress.
  • Stress affects the brain, heart, gut, immunity and sleep.
  • Recognising your personal triggers and signals is the first step to managing stress.
  • Recovery habits and social support build resilience.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is stress in simple words?

Stress is your body and mind's reaction to demands or threats that challenge your ability to cope.

Is all stress bad?

No. Short-term, manageable stress can be motivating. Chronic, overwhelming stress without recovery is harmful.

What hormone is released during stress?

Adrenaline and cortisol are the main stress hormones.

Can stress make you sick?

Chronic stress is linked to weakened immunity, high blood pressure, digestive problems, sleep disorders and mental health conditions.

Is a stress-free life possible?

Not completely, because life always involves demands and change. But you can reduce harmful stress and build better recovery and coping habits.

What is the difference between stress and anxiety?

Stress is a response to an external demand or pressure; anxiety is a feeling of worry or fear that can persist even without a clear current stressor. Chronic stress can contribute to anxiety.

How long does it take to recover from stress?

The body's acute stress response usually settles within minutes to hours once the stressor passes. Recovery from chronic stress can take weeks or months of better sleep, reduced demands and support.

Why do I feel stressed even when nothing is wrong?

Accumulated small stresses, poor sleep, worry about the future, or an underlying anxiety condition can keep the stress response active. Tracking patterns and seeking support can help identify the cause.

Can stress cause weight gain?

Chronic stress can affect appetite and cravings for high-calorie foods, and cortisol may influence fat storage, particularly around the abdomen. Poor sleep linked to stress also affects appetite hormones.

What is eustress?

Eustress is positive, motivating stress, such as excitement before a performance or the challenge of a new project, which can boost focus and growth.

Does exercise really reduce stress?

Yes. Regular physical activity helps the body use up stress hormones, releases mood-improving chemicals, improves sleep and builds a sense of control. Even short walks can help.

What is allostatic load?

A term introduced by neuroscientist Bruce McEwen for the cumulative wear and tear on the body from repeated or chronic stress.

Can children experience chronic stress?

Yes. Ongoing family conflict, poverty, bullying or academic pressure can cause chronic stress in children. Supportive, stable relationships with caring adults are the strongest protection.

What is the General Adaptation Syndrome?

Hans Selye's model describing three stages of the body's response to prolonged stress: alarm, resistance and exhaustion.

Can yoga and pranayama help with stress?

Many people find yoga and breathing practices such as pranayama calming, and studies suggest they can reduce perceived stress and anxiety, especially when practised regularly.

Final Thoughts

Stress is a natural, built-in survival system that helps us meet challenges. Problems arise when it's activated too often, for too long, without enough recovery. By understanding how stress works in your body and mind, recognising your personal triggers and signals, and appreciating how your thoughts shape your experience, you take the first and most important step toward a calmer, healthier life.
