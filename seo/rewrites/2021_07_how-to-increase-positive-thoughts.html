Quick answer: You can increase positive thoughts by practising gratitude daily, noticing and challenging negative self-talk, spending time with supportive people, limiting negative media, staying present through mindfulness, taking care of your body and adopting a growth mindset. Positive thinking isn't about ignoring problems; it's about seeing them realistically and believing you can improve things. With repetition, these habits make optimistic thinking feel more natural.

Our brains have a natural negativity bias. Psychologists believe it evolved to keep us safe: noticing threats mattered more for survival than noticing pleasant things. Today, though, this bias can make us dwell on criticism, replay mistakes and worry about things that never happen.

The good news is that thought patterns can change. Research in positive psychology shows that simple, regular practices can shift your focus toward the good and improve wellbeing. This guide covers the most effective ones.

Why Positive Thinking Matters

Optimism has been linked in research to:

  • Lower stress and better coping with difficulties.
  • Better heart health and longer life expectancy in some long-term studies.
  • Stronger relationships.
  • Greater persistence toward goals.

Importantly, healthy positive thinking is realistic. It acknowledges problems but focuses on solutions and possibilities.

1. Practise Gratitude

Gratitude is one of the most studied ways to boost positive emotions. Regularly noticing what's going well trains your attention to look for good things.

  • Three good things: each evening, write down three things that went well and why.
  • Gratitude letter: write to someone who helped you and, if possible, read it to them.
  • Gratitude pauses: before meals or at bedtime, name one thing you're thankful for.

2. Notice and Challenge Negative Thoughts

Many negative thoughts are automatic and exaggerated. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) teaches you to catch and question them.

Common thinking traps include:

  • All-or-nothing thinking: "If it's not perfect, it's a failure."
  • Catastrophising: "This will be a disaster."
  • Mind reading: "They think I'm useless."
  • Overgeneralising: "I always mess things up."

When you notice one, ask:

  1. What's the evidence for and against this thought?
  2. Is there a more balanced way to see it?
  3. What would I say to a friend thinking this?

3. Use Kinder Self-Talk

Pay attention to how you speak to yourself. Replace harsh criticism with supportive, realistic language:

  • "I'm so stupid" → "I made a mistake, and I can learn from it."
  • "I can't do this" → "This is hard, but I can take the first step."
  • "Nothing ever works" → "This didn't work. What can I try next?"

Research on self-compassion, led by psychologist Kristin Neff, links treating yourself kindly with lower anxiety and greater resilience.

4. Surround Yourself With Positivity

Moods are contagious. The people, content and environments around you shape your thoughts.

  • Spend more time with people who support and encourage you.
  • Limit time with those who constantly complain or criticise.
  • Curate your social media feed; unfollow accounts that make you feel worse.
  • Limit news consumption to set times rather than constant scrolling.
  • Fill your space with things that make you feel good: plants, light, music, meaningful photos.

5. Focus on the Present Moment

Many negative thoughts involve regretting the past or worrying about the future. Mindfulness brings attention back to now.

  • Take a few minutes daily to focus on your breath.
  • Notice sensations during everyday activities, such as the taste of food or the feeling of walking.
  • When your mind wanders into worry, gently return to the present.

6. Take Care of Your Body

Thoughts and physical health are closely connected.

  • Exercise: regular movement reduces symptoms of anxiety and depression.
  • Sleep: tiredness makes negative thoughts feel more convincing.
  • Nutrition: regular, balanced meals help stabilise mood.
  • Nature: time outdoors is linked to lower stress and better mood.

7. Cultivate a Growth Mindset

Psychologist Carol Dweck's research describes a growth mindset as the belief that abilities can be developed through effort and learning. People with this mindset see challenges as opportunities and setbacks as temporary.

  • Add "yet" to your sentences: "I can't do this yet."
  • Praise effort and strategy, not just results.
  • Reflect on what each setback teaches you.

8. Do Kind Things for Others

Acts of kindness boost the mood of both giver and receiver. Help a colleague, compliment someone sincerely, volunteer or send a supportive message. Research suggests doing several kind acts in one day can noticeably lift wellbeing.

9. Celebrate Small Wins

Don't wait for big achievements to feel good. Acknowledge progress: finishing a task, making a healthy choice or handling a difficult conversation well.

10. Visualise a Positive Future

The "best possible self" exercise, in which you write about a future where things have gone well, has been shown in studies to increase optimism. Spend 10 to 15 minutes imagining and writing about your life going as well as it realistically could.

The Science of Positive Thinking

Neuroplasticity: Your Brain Can Change

The brain constantly rewires itself based on what we repeatedly think and do, a property called neuroplasticity. Neuropsychologist Rick Hanson, author of Hardwiring Happiness, describes the brain as "Velcro for bad experiences and Teflon for good ones". His suggestion: when something good happens, pause for 10 to 20 seconds to really take it in. Repeatedly savouring positive experiences helps strengthen the neural pathways associated with them.

The Broaden-and-Build Theory

Psychologist Barbara Fredrickson's research suggests that positive emotions do more than feel good. They broaden our attention and thinking, making us more creative and open to possibilities, and over time they build lasting resources such as skills, relationships and resilience. Negative emotions narrow our focus to deal with threats, which is useful in danger but limiting in everyday life.

Learned Optimism

Martin Seligman, often called the father of positive psychology, found that optimists and pessimists explain setbacks differently. Pessimists see bad events as permanent ("It will always be like this"), pervasive ("It ruins everything") and personal ("It's all my fault"). Optimists see them as temporary, specific and partly due to circumstances. Seligman showed that people can learn to change their explanatory style, reducing depression and increasing persistence.

Optimism and Health

Long-term studies, including research from Harvard and Boston University, have linked higher optimism with lower risk of heart disease and a greater chance of living to 85 or beyond. Researchers think optimistic people may handle stress better and be more likely to maintain healthy habits.

A 21-Day Positive Thinking Plan

Week 1: Notice the Good

  • Each evening, write down three good things that happened and why.
  • Pause to savour one pleasant moment every day for 20 seconds.
  • Reduce news and social media to set times.

Week 2: Challenge the Negative

  • Keep a thought journal: write down negative thoughts, the situation and a more balanced alternative.
  • Practise one kind self-statement each morning.
  • Do one act of kindness each day.

Week 3: Build the Habit

  • Write a gratitude letter to someone who helped you.
  • Complete the "best possible self" writing exercise.
  • Add ten minutes of mindfulness to your morning.
  • Reflect on how your thinking has changed over three weeks.

How Negative Thinking Affects Your Life

It's worth understanding why chronic negative thinking deserves attention:

  • Stress: repeatedly imagining worst-case scenarios keeps the body in a stress response, raising cortisol and disturbing sleep.
  • Rumination: going over problems again and again without solving them is strongly linked with depression and anxiety, according to research by psychologist Susan Nolen-Hoeksema.
  • Self-fulfilling prophecies: expecting failure can reduce effort, making failure more likely.
  • Relationships: constant criticism and pessimism can push people away.
  • Missed opportunities: fear of failure may stop you from applying for jobs, starting projects or meeting new people.

The aim isn't to eliminate negative thoughts entirely, which is impossible, but to stop them from running your life.

Quick Techniques for Negative Moments

When a negative thought spiral starts, try one of these short techniques:

  • The STOP technique: Stop what you're doing, Take a breath, Observe your thoughts and feelings, then Proceed with intention.
  • Distance yourself: research by Ethan Kross shows that talking to yourself in the third person, using your own name ("Why is Priya feeling upset?"), creates psychological distance and reduces emotional intensity.
  • "And" instead of "but": replace "I made progress, but it's not enough" with "I made progress, and I can keep going."
  • The 10-10-10 rule: ask how you'll feel about this in 10 minutes, 10 months and 10 years.
  • Move your body: a brisk walk, stretching or dancing to one song can shift your mood quickly.
  • Call someone: a short conversation with a supportive friend often breaks the loop.

Positive Thinking Exercises You Can Try Today

  1. Savouring walk: take a 10-minute walk and deliberately notice five pleasant things, such as a colour, a sound or a smell.
  2. Strength spotting: write down three of your personal strengths and one way you used each recently.
  3. Kindness log: note kind things others did for you this week.
  4. Reframing practice: take one frustrating situation and write three possible positive or neutral interpretations.
  5. Future letter: write a letter from your future self, five years from now, thanking you for the habits you started today.

Positive Affirmations: Do They Work?

Affirmations are positive statements you repeat to yourself, such as "I am capable". Research suggests they work best when they're believable and connected to your values. A study by Joanne Wood and colleagues found that people with low self-esteem sometimes felt worse after repeating overly positive statements like "I am a lovable person", because the statements conflicted with their beliefs. More effective approaches include:

  • Value affirmations: writing about values that matter to you, such as family, honesty or creativity.
  • Realistic statements: "I'm learning to handle this" rather than "I'm perfect at this."
  • Questions instead of statements: "How can I make today a good day?"

Real-Life Examples of Reframing

Reframing doesn't deny reality; it finds a more useful way to see it. Here are some everyday examples:

  • Job rejection: "I'm not good enough" becomes "This role wasn't the right match. I learned more about interviewing, and I'll apply what I learned next time."
  • Traffic jam: "My day is ruined" becomes "I can't control the traffic. I'll call ahead and listen to a podcast I've been meaning to hear."
  • Mistake at work: "I always mess up" becomes "I made one mistake on a busy day. I'll fix it and add a checklist so it doesn't happen again."
  • Cancelled plans: "Nobody wants to see me" becomes "My friend is probably busy. I'll use the evening to rest and suggest another date."
  • Weight gain: "I have no willpower" becomes "My routine slipped during a stressful time. I can start with one small healthy change this week."
  • Exam failure: "I'm stupid" becomes "My study method didn't work. I'll try practice testing and ask for help."

Each reframe keeps the facts but changes the story, and the new story points toward action.

Positive Thinking in Relationships

How we think about the people close to us shapes how we treat them. Couples researcher John Gottman found that happy couples maintain a habit of "scanning for the positive": noticing and appreciating what their partner does right, rather than constantly looking for faults. Try these habits:

  • Say thank you for everyday things your partner, family or friends do.
  • Assume good intentions when someone forgets or makes a mistake, unless there's a clear pattern.
  • Share good news and respond enthusiastically when others share theirs, which psychologist Shelly Gable calls "active-constructive responding".
  • Remember and talk about happy shared memories.
  • Replace criticism with requests: "Could you help me with the dishes tonight?" instead of "You never help."

The Role of Purpose and Meaning

Positive thinking is easier when your life feels meaningful. Psychiatrist Viktor Frankl, who survived Nazi concentration camps, wrote in Man's Search for Meaning that people can endure almost anything if they have a reason to live. Having goals you care about, relationships you value and ways to contribute to others gives your mind something positive to focus on, especially during hard times. Ask yourself: what matters most to me, and how can I spend a little more time on it each week?

Gratitude Journal Prompts

If "three good things" starts to feel repetitive, try these prompts:

  • Who made my day a little easier today, and how?
  • What is something in my home that I'm glad to have?
  • What skill or ability am I grateful for?
  • What challenge from the past am I now grateful for, because of what it taught me?
  • What small pleasure did I enjoy today: a meal, a song, a moment of quiet?
  • Which person in my life do I sometimes take for granted?
  • What is going well in my health, work or relationships right now?
  • What am I looking forward to this week?

A Positive Morning and Evening Routine

Morning (15 minutes)

  • Avoid checking your phone for the first 15 to 30 minutes.
  • Drink a glass of water and get some daylight.
  • Take five slow breaths and set one intention for the day.
  • Write one thing you're looking forward to.

Evening (10 minutes)

  • Write three good things from the day.
  • Note one thing you handled well, however small.
  • Release one worry by writing it down with a next step.
  • Put screens away and wind down.

Positive Thinking at Work

  • Start meetings by noting a recent success or something going well.
  • Reframe problems as challenges: "What's one thing we can try?"
  • Thank colleagues specifically for their contributions.
  • Avoid joining chronic complaining; redirect conversations toward solutions.
  • Celebrate progress on long projects, not just the final outcome.

Positive Thinking During Difficult Times

When facing illness, loss, job loss or other serious hardship, forced cheerfulness can feel impossible and even insulting. In these times, positive thinking looks different:

  • Allow yourself to grieve and feel what you feel.
  • Focus on getting through one day, or even one hour, at a time.
  • Look for small sources of comfort: a kind message, a warm drink, sunlight.
  • Lean on people who care about you, and accept help.
  • Remind yourself of past difficulties you survived.
  • Hold onto hope that things can improve, even if you can't see how yet.

Helping Children Think Positively

  • Ask at dinner, "What was the best part of your day?"
  • Praise effort and persistence rather than intelligence.
  • Model positive self-talk when you make mistakes.
  • Teach them to name feelings and talk about them.
  • Read stories about characters who overcome challenges.

Common Mistakes With Positive Thinking

  • Toxic positivity: denying real problems or forcing yourself to feel happy.
  • Expecting instant change: new thought patterns take weeks of practice.
  • Ignoring emotions: sadness, anger and fear are normal and carry useful information.
  • Relying only on thinking: actions, relationships and health habits matter too.

When to Seek Help

If negative thoughts are constant, overwhelming or accompanied by hopelessness, loss of interest, sleep or appetite changes, they may be symptoms of depression or anxiety. Please speak to a doctor or mental health professional. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, contact a local emergency number or crisis helpline immediately.

Frequently Asked Questions: How to Increase Positive Thoughts

Can you train your brain to think positively?

Yes. Repeated practices like gratitude, reframing and mindfulness strengthen new mental habits over time.

How long does it take to become more positive?

Some people notice mood improvements within a couple of weeks of daily gratitude or mindfulness practice. Lasting change usually takes a few months.

Is positive thinking the same as being optimistic?

They overlap. Optimism is a general expectation that things will turn out well; positive thinking includes specific habits that support that outlook.

What's the quickest way to stop a negative thought?

Pause, name it ("this is a worry thought"), take a few slow breaths and ask whether it's helpful or true. Then redirect your attention to an action you can take.

What are the best books on positive thinking?

Popular, research-based choices include Learned Optimism by Martin Seligman, Positivity by Barbara Fredrickson, Hardwiring Happiness by Rick Hanson, Mindset by Carol Dweck, Chatter by Ethan Kross and Man's Search for Meaning by Viktor Frankl.

Is it normal to have negative thoughts every day?

Yes. Everyone has negative thoughts. They become a problem only when they're constant, overwhelming or stop you from living the life you want. The goal is balance, not perfection.

Can positive thinking cure illness?

No. Positive thinking can support wellbeing, coping and healthy habits, but it doesn't replace medical treatment. It's harmful to suggest that people became ill, or stayed ill, because they weren't positive enough.

How can I stay positive around negative people?

Set boundaries on how much time you spend with them, avoid getting pulled into complaining, gently steer conversations toward solutions and make sure you also spend time with uplifting people.

Why do I remember criticism more than praise?

That's the brain's negativity bias. Research suggests negative events often have a stronger psychological impact than equally sized positive ones. Deliberately noticing and savouring positive feedback helps balance this.

Does meditation help with positive thinking?

Yes. Mindfulness meditation helps you observe thoughts without getting caught up in them, and loving-kindness meditation, which involves wishing well to yourself and others, has been shown to increase positive emotions.

Final Thoughts

Positive thinking isn't about pretending everything is perfect. It's about training your mind to notice what's good, respond to problems constructively and treat yourself with kindness. Start with one habit, such as writing three good things each night, and build from there. Over time, your mind will become a more hopeful, supportive place to live.
