Quick answer: A currency's value is measured in two main ways. Externally, by its exchange rate against other currencies, such as how many rupees it takes to buy one US dollar, and by broader indices like the nominal and real effective exchange rates (NEER and REER), which compare it against a basket of trading partners' currencies. Internally, by its purchasing power, how much it can buy at home, which falls as inflation rises. Economists also use purchasing power parity (PPP) to compare what currencies can buy across countries. Exchange rates are driven by interest rates, inflation, trade, capital flows, growth, confidence and central bank policy.

News headlines often say the rupee "fell to a record low" or "strengthened" against the dollar. But what does that really mean, and is a stronger currency always better? Measuring a currency's value is more nuanced than a single exchange rate suggests.

This guide explains the different ways currency value is measured, what drives it, how India's rupee is managed, and what changes in currency value mean for ordinary people, businesses and the economy.

What Gives a Currency Value?

Modern currencies are fiat money: they aren't backed by gold or another commodity but have value because governments declare them legal tender and people trust that they will be accepted for goods, services and taxes. That trust depends on the stability of the economy, the credibility of the central bank and the government's management of public finances.

A currency's value therefore reflects confidence in the economy behind it: its productivity, inflation, institutions and prospects.

Measure 1: Bilateral Exchange Rates

The most familiar measure is the bilateral exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another. If USD/INR is 85, one US dollar costs 85 rupees.

  • If the rate rises to 88, the rupee has depreciated (weakened): you need more rupees to buy a dollar.
  • If it falls to 82, the rupee has appreciated (strengthened).

In India, the Financial Benchmarks India Limited (FBIL) publishes daily reference rates for the rupee against major currencies, which are widely used for valuing contracts and transactions. Market rates change continuously during trading hours.

Bilateral rates are easy to understand, but they can be misleading. If the rupee falls against the dollar but the dollar is rising against all currencies, the rupee may actually be holding steady against India's other trading partners.

Measure 2: Effective Exchange Rates (NEER and REER)

To get a broader picture, central banks calculate effective exchange rates, which compare a currency against a basket of trading partners' currencies, weighted by trade importance.

  • Nominal effective exchange rate (NEER): a weighted average of a currency's exchange rates against its trading partners' currencies.
  • Real effective exchange rate (REER): the NEER adjusted for differences in inflation between countries. It better reflects a country's price competitiveness.

The RBI publishes NEER and REER indices for the rupee, including a 40-currency index. If India's inflation is higher than its trading partners' and the nominal exchange rate doesn't fall enough to compensate, the REER rises, meaning Indian goods become relatively more expensive abroad. Economists watch REER closely to judge whether a currency is overvalued or undervalued in competitive terms.

A Simple Example of Real vs Nominal Value

Suppose the rupee stays at exactly 85 per US dollar for a year. In nominal terms, nothing has changed. But if Indian prices rose 5% that year while US prices rose 2%, Indian goods have become about 3% more expensive relative to American goods at the same exchange rate. In real terms, the rupee has appreciated, and Indian exporters have become slightly less competitive. This is why economists look at real effective exchange rates rather than only headline rates when assessing competitiveness.

Measure 3: Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)

PPP compares what a currency can buy in different countries. The idea is that, in the long run, exchange rates should adjust so that identical goods cost the same everywhere. In reality, many goods and services, especially local services and housing, are much cheaper in lower-income countries.

Organisations such as the World Bank and IMF publish PPP conversion factors. Using PPP, India's economy appears much larger than using market exchange rates, because a rupee buys more in India than its dollar equivalent would buy in the United States. By PPP measures, India has been ranked as the world's third-largest economy, behind China and the United States, according to IMF and World Bank estimates.

The Economist magazine's light-hearted Big Mac Index uses the price of a burger in different countries as a simple illustration of PPP.

Measure 4: Domestic Purchasing Power and Inflation

A currency's internal value is what it can buy at home. Inflation erodes this value. If inflation averages 6% a year, prices roughly double in about 12 years, so 100 rupees buys about half as much as before.

In India, inflation is measured mainly by the Consumer Price Index (CPI), published by the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation. The RBI's monetary policy framework targets CPI inflation of 4%, within a tolerance band of 2% to 6%, a framework adopted in 2016. Keeping inflation stable protects the domestic value of the rupee.

Measure 5: Currency Indices

Some indices track a currency's overall strength. The best known is the US Dollar Index (DXY), which measures the dollar against a basket of six major currencies, with the euro making up the largest share. When the DXY rises, the dollar is strengthening broadly, which often puts pressure on emerging market currencies like the rupee.

Supply and Demand in the Currency Market

At its core, an exchange rate is a price set by supply and demand. Demand for rupees comes from foreigners who want to buy Indian goods and services, invest in Indian shares, bonds and companies, or send money to India. Supply of rupees comes from Indians who want to buy foreign goods, travel abroad, invest overseas or repay foreign loans, because they must sell rupees to buy foreign currency.

  • When foreign investors pour money into Indian stocks, demand for rupees rises and the rupee tends to strengthen.
  • When oil prices surge, Indian importers need more dollars, increasing supply of rupees and weakening the currency.
  • When software exporters convert their dollar earnings, they sell dollars and buy rupees, supporting the currency.

Every factor that affects currency value ultimately works through these flows of supply and demand.

How the Indian Rupee Is Managed

India follows a managed float: the rupee's value is largely determined by market forces, but the RBI intervenes to curb excessive volatility rather than to target a specific level. It does this by buying or selling US dollars in the market, using its foreign exchange reserves, which are among the largest in the world.

Historically, India moved through several regimes: the rupee was linked to the British pound before independence and for some years after, was devalued in 1966, and was later managed against a basket of currencies. After the 1991 balance of payments crisis, India devalued the rupee and moved to a market-determined exchange rate in 1993 as part of broader economic reforms.

Reserve Currencies and the Dominance of the US Dollar

A reserve currency is one that central banks hold in large amounts as part of their foreign exchange reserves. The US dollar dominates: IMF data on the currency composition of official foreign exchange reserves have shown the dollar accounting for close to 60% of allocated reserves in recent years, followed by the euro, with smaller shares for the Japanese yen, British pound, Chinese renminbi and others. The dollar is also used in a large share of international trade invoicing, commodity pricing and global debt.

This dominance gives the US significant influence: changes in US interest rates can move capital flows and exchange rates worldwide. When the US Federal Reserve raises rates, emerging market currencies, including the rupee, often face pressure as investors seek higher returns in dollars.

Lessons From Currency Crises

  • India 1991: with foreign exchange reserves falling to only enough for a few weeks of imports, India faced a balance of payments crisis. It pledged gold to raise funds, devalued the rupee and launched major economic reforms. The crisis led to the liberalisation that transformed India's economy.
  • Asian financial crisis 1997: currencies in Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea and other countries collapsed after investors withdrew capital, exposing weaknesses in banking systems and heavy foreign-currency borrowing.
  • Taper tantrum 2013: when the US Federal Reserve signalled it might slow its bond purchases, investors pulled money from emerging markets. The rupee fell sharply, and India was grouped among the "Fragile Five" economies before measures by the RBI and government helped stabilise it.

These episodes show why strong reserves, manageable foreign debt, credible inflation control and healthy banks are essential to protect a currency's value.

Strong vs Weak Currency: Who Wins?

A Stronger Rupee

  • Benefits: cheaper imports (oil, electronics, gold), lower cost of foreign travel and overseas education, lower inflation pressure, cheaper repayment of foreign-currency debt.
  • Drawbacks: Indian exports become more expensive abroad, hurting exporters and IT services companies; remittances from abroad buy fewer rupees.

A Weaker Rupee

  • Benefits: exports become more competitive, IT and export companies earn more in rupees, remittances stretch further, foreign tourists find India cheaper.
  • Drawbacks: imports become costlier, fuel and electronics prices may rise, inflation can increase, and overseas education and travel become more expensive.

Neither strong nor weak is universally good. What matters most for an economy is stability and fundamentals, not the level of the exchange rate alone.

Why the Rupee Has Weakened Against the Dollar Over Time

The rupee has gradually depreciated against the US dollar over the decades. The main reasons include:

  • Inflation differential: India has generally had higher inflation than the US, so over time the rupee loses value relative to the dollar.
  • Current account deficits: India often imports more goods than it exports, particularly oil and gold.
  • Global dollar strength: during periods of global stress or rising US interest rates, money tends to flow into the dollar.

Gradual depreciation is common for fast-growing emerging economies and doesn't necessarily signal weakness. India's large foreign exchange reserves, strong services exports and steady remittances help support the rupee and limit volatility.

The Rupee's Growing International Role

India has taken steps to encourage wider use of the rupee in international trade. In 2022, the RBI introduced a framework allowing international trade to be invoiced and settled in rupees through special accounts, and India has signed arrangements with several countries to facilitate trade in local currencies. Payment systems like UPI have been linked with systems in a growing number of countries, making cross-border payments easier.

The RBI has also piloted a central bank digital currency, the digital rupee (e₹), for both wholesale and retail use. These initiatives are gradual, and the US dollar remains dominant, but they reflect India's goal of reducing dependence on any single foreign currency over time.

How to Read Currency News

  • Check the direction: "USD/INR rises" means the rupee weakened.
  • Look at the bigger picture: is the rupee falling against all currencies, or only against a strengthening dollar?
  • Consider the pace: slow, steady moves are less concerning than sudden sharp falls.
  • Watch the causes: oil prices, US interest rates and foreign investment flows often explain rupee movements.
  • Remember the context: "record low" headlines are common because gradual depreciation regularly sets new lows over time.

Currency Value and Interest Rates: The RBI's Balancing Act

Interest rates and currency value are closely linked. When the RBI raises its policy repo rate, Indian bonds and deposits become more attractive to foreign investors, which can support the rupee. But higher rates also make borrowing more expensive for households and businesses, potentially slowing growth. When the RBI cuts rates to support growth, the rupee may face some pressure if foreign investors seek higher returns elsewhere.

The RBI must therefore balance several goals: keeping inflation near its target, supporting growth, maintaining financial stability and avoiding disorderly currency movements. Its foreign exchange reserves give it a tool to manage short-term volatility without having to change interest rates for currency reasons alone.

How Currency Changes Affect You

  • Fuel prices: India imports most of its crude oil, so a weaker rupee can raise petrol, diesel and cooking gas costs.
  • Electronics: phones, laptops and components often become more expensive when the rupee weakens.
  • Gold prices: gold is priced internationally in dollars, so rupee weakness raises domestic gold prices.
  • Overseas education and travel: fees and expenses abroad rise when the rupee falls.
  • Remittances: families receiving money from abroad benefit when the rupee weakens.
  • International investments: funds holding US stocks gain extra rupee returns when the dollar strengthens.

Currency Value and Everyday Planning

Understanding currency value helps with practical decisions:

  1. Planning overseas education: build a buffer of 10% to 15% in your budget for possible rupee depreciation, and consider converting fees gradually rather than all at once.
  2. Buying imported goods: big purchases like electronics or cars may become more expensive after significant rupee falls; prices often adjust with a lag.
  3. Diversifying investments: holding some international funds can offset the impact of rupee depreciation on global expenses.
  4. Running a small business: if you import raw materials or export products, discuss hedging options with your bank.
  5. Sending or receiving money abroad: compare total costs, including exchange rate markups, across banks and licensed money transfer services.

Key Terms Recap

  • Appreciation: a currency gains value against another.
  • Depreciation: a currency loses value against another.
  • NEER: nominal effective exchange rate against a trade-weighted basket.
  • REER: NEER adjusted for inflation differences.
  • PPP: purchasing power parity, comparing what currencies can buy.
  • Reserves: foreign currency and gold held by the central bank.
  • Current account: a country's trade in goods and services, plus income and transfers such as remittances.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the strongest currency in the world?

By exchange rate per unit, currencies such as the Kuwaiti dinar are among the highest valued. But a high unit value doesn't mean a stronger economy; it reflects how the currency was set up. The US dollar is the most important and widely used currency globally.

Who decides the value of the rupee?

Mainly market forces of supply and demand in the foreign exchange market. The RBI intervenes to reduce excessive volatility but does not fix a target rate.

Is a falling rupee bad for India?

Not necessarily. Gradual depreciation can help exporters and reflects inflation differences. Rapid, disorderly falls are more concerning because they can fuel inflation and reduce confidence.

What is the difference between devaluation and depreciation?

Devaluation is a deliberate reduction of a currency's value by a government or central bank under a fixed or pegged system. Depreciation is a market-driven decline under a floating or managed float system.

How can I protect myself from rupee depreciation?

If you have future foreign-currency expenses, such as overseas education, consider saving gradually in international funds or planning conversions over time. Diversifying investments across countries can also reduce currency risk.

What is a currency peg?

A peg fixes a currency's value to another currency or basket. For example, the UAE dirham has been pegged to the US dollar for decades. Pegs offer stability but require the central bank to hold enough reserves to defend the fixed rate.

Why do some currencies have very low unit values?

Some currencies, such as the Indonesian rupiah or Vietnamese dong, have large numbers per US dollar due to past inflation or historical choices. Unit value alone says little about economic strength.

Can a country simply print more money to become richer?

No. Printing money without matching growth in goods and services typically causes inflation, reducing the currency's purchasing power. Extreme cases, such as Zimbabwe in the late 2000s, led to hyperinflation.

How often does the RBI publish REER data?

The RBI publishes effective exchange rate indices regularly in its bulletins and database, allowing analysts to track changes in the rupee's real value over time.

Does a stock market rally strengthen the rupee?

Often, yes, if the rally attracts foreign investment. Foreign investors need to buy rupees to invest in Indian shares, increasing demand for the currency. If they sell and withdraw, the rupee can weaken.

Why does the rupee react to US Federal Reserve decisions?

Higher US interest rates make dollar assets more attractive, which can draw investment away from emerging markets like India, putting pressure on the rupee. Lower US rates can have the opposite effect.

Is a digital rupee different from UPI?

Yes. UPI is a payment system that moves money between bank accounts. The digital rupee is a form of currency issued directly by the RBI in digital form, similar to cash but electronic.

What is the best single measure of a currency's strength?

There isn't one. For competitiveness, economists prefer the real effective exchange rate; for everyday comparisons, bilateral rates; and for living standards, purchasing power parity.

Final Thoughts

A currency's value isn't captured by one number. Bilateral exchange rates, effective exchange rates, purchasing power parity and domestic inflation each tell part of the story. Behind them lie interest rates, inflation, trade, investment flows, confidence and central bank policy. For India, a managed, gradually adjusting rupee backed by strong reserves aims to balance competitiveness with stability. Understanding these measures helps you make sense of the headlines and plan better for expenses and investments that depend on currency movements.
