Quick answer: Countries are usually described as developed or developing based on a mix of income, living standards and economic structure. Developed countries generally have high income per person, high life expectancy, widespread education, strong infrastructure and economies dominated by services and advanced industry. Developing countries have lower average incomes and face greater challenges in health, education, infrastructure and poverty. There is no single official definition: the World Bank groups countries by income (low, lower-middle, upper-middle and high), the United Nations uses the Human Development Index and a "least developed countries" list, and the IMF separates "advanced economies" from "emerging market and developing economies". Many experts now prefer these more precise categories to the simple developed/developing split.

Terms like "developed country", "developing country", "Third World" and "Global South" appear constantly in news, economics textbooks and political debates. Yet they're often used loosely, and different organisations define them differently. Understanding how countries are classified helps make sense of global inequality, aid, trade rules and international negotiations.

This article explains the main classification systems, the indicators behind them, the key differences between developed and developing countries, where India fits, and why the traditional labels are increasingly questioned.

Why Classify Countries at All?

Classifications are tools. They help:

  • International organisations decide who qualifies for concessional loans, grants and technical assistance.
  • Trade bodies apply special rules, such as longer transition periods or preferential tariffs, for poorer countries.
  • Researchers compare progress across countries and track global goals such as the Sustainable Development Goals.
  • Investors assess risk and opportunity in different markets.
  • Governments set targets and measure progress.

Because each organisation has its own purpose, each uses its own system.

The Main Classification Systems

1. World Bank Income Groups

The World Bank classifies economies into four groups based on gross national income (GNI) per capita in US dollars, calculated using its Atlas method:

  • Low income
  • Lower-middle income
  • Upper-middle income
  • High income

The thresholds are updated every year on 1 July to account for inflation. In recent years the high-income threshold has been roughly US$14,000 per person, and the low-income threshold roughly US$1,100 to US$1,150. Countries can move between groups as their incomes change.

In 2016 the World Bank stopped using the term "developing world" in its World Development Indicators, arguing that the binary label had become less meaningful and preferring specific income groups instead.

2. United Nations Human Development Index (HDI)

The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) publishes the Human Development Index, which combines three dimensions:

  • A long and healthy life: life expectancy at birth.
  • Knowledge: expected years of schooling for children and mean years of schooling for adults.
  • A decent standard of living: GNI per capita adjusted for purchasing power.

Countries are grouped into four categories:

Very high human development0.800 and above

The HDI was created in 1990, inspired by the work of economists Mahbub ul Haq and Amartya Sen, to shift attention from income alone to people's capabilities and wellbeing.

3. UN Least Developed Countries (LDCs)

The UN maintains a list of least developed countries, currently around 44, that face severe structural barriers to sustainable development. Three criteria are used: income per capita, a Human Assets Index (health and education), and an Economic and Environmental Vulnerability Index. LDCs receive special support in trade, aid and climate finance. Countries can "graduate" from the list; Botswana, Cape Verde, the Maldives, Samoa, Equatorial Guinea, Vanuatu, Bhutan and São Tomé and Príncipe have done so.

4. IMF Advanced Economies and Emerging and Developing Economies

The International Monetary Fund's World Economic Outlook divides the world into about 40 "advanced economies" and a much larger group of "emerging market and developing economies". The IMF notes that its classification isn't based on strict criteria but considers income per capita, export diversification and integration into the global financial system.

5. WTO Self-Declaration

At the World Trade Organization, countries largely self-declare whether they're developing, which entitles them to "special and differential treatment" such as longer implementation periods. This has caused controversy, because some large and relatively wealthy economies continue to claim developing status.

6. Historical Labels: First, Second and Third World

During the Cold War, the "First World" referred to the United States and its allies, the "Second World" to the Soviet Union and its allies, and the "Third World" to countries not aligned with either bloc, many of them newly independent and poorer. French demographer Alfred Sauvy coined the term "Third World" in 1952. Over time, "Third World" became associated with poverty and is now widely considered outdated and potentially pejorative.

7. Global North and Global South

The "Global North" and "Global South" describe broad economic and political divides rather than strict geography; Australia and New Zealand are usually counted in the North. The idea was popularised by the 1980 Brandt Report, which drew the "Brandt Line" separating richer and poorer nations. Today, "Global South" is widely used in diplomacy and by developing countries themselves, though critics point out that it groups very different countries together.

Key Indicators Used to Measure Development

  • GDP and GNI per capita: average income or output per person.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP): adjusts income for differences in prices between countries.
  • Life expectancy and infant mortality: indicators of health and healthcare quality.
  • Literacy and years of schooling: education outcomes.
  • Poverty rates: share of people living below national or international poverty lines.
  • Inequality: measured by the Gini coefficient or income shares.
  • Economic structure: share of agriculture, industry and services in output and employment.
  • Infrastructure: access to electricity, clean water, sanitation, roads and internet.
  • Governance: rule of law, corruption, political stability and institutional quality.

GDP, GNI and PPP Explained Simply

Many classifications depend on a few economic measures that are often confused.

  • Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total value of goods and services produced within a country's borders in a year.
  • Gross national income (GNI) is GDP plus income residents earn from abroad, minus income sent abroad by foreigners. For most countries GDP and GNI are close, but they can differ significantly for countries with large foreign investment or large remittance inflows.
  • Per capita means divided by population. It gives an average per person, not what a typical person actually earns.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusts for the fact that the same amount of money buys more in some countries than others. A haircut or a meal costs much less in India than in Switzerland, so comparing incomes at market exchange rates understates living standards in lower-cost countries.

An illustration: two countries might each have income per person of US$2,500 at market exchange rates. If prices in one are much lower, its income measured at PPP might be several times higher, meaning people can actually buy more goods and services. That's why the HDI uses PPP-adjusted income, while World Bank income groups use market exchange rates through the Atlas method.

Beyond Income: Other Ways to Measure Development

  • Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI): published by UNDP and the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative, it measures deprivations in health, education and living standards, such as lack of clean water, electricity, cooking fuel or schooling, rather than income alone.
  • Inequality-adjusted HDI (IHDI): adjusts the HDI for inequality within a country. The bigger the gap between HDI and IHDI, the more unequally development is shared.
  • Gini coefficient: measures income or wealth inequality on a scale from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 or 100 (maximum inequality).
  • Gender indices: UNDP's Gender Inequality Index and the World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Index assess gaps between women and men in health, education, economic participation and political power.
  • World Happiness Report: ranks countries by people's own ratings of their lives, alongside factors such as social support, freedom, generosity and trust.
  • Environmental measures: carbon emissions per person, material footprint and the planetary-pressures-adjusted HDI consider whether development is sustainable.

Using several measures together gives a fuller picture than any single label.

Characteristics of Developed Countries

  • High income per person and high standards of living
  • Long life expectancy and low infant mortality
  • High literacy and widespread access to higher education
  • Economies dominated by services and advanced manufacturing
  • Well-developed infrastructure and technology
  • Strong institutions, rule of law and social safety nets
  • Low population growth and ageing populations
  • High levels of urbanisation

Examples commonly cited include the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Germany, France, Japan, Australia, the Nordic countries, Singapore and South Korea.

Characteristics of Developing Countries

  • Lower average income and higher poverty rates
  • Larger share of employment in agriculture and informal work
  • Gaps in healthcare, education and infrastructure
  • Higher population growth and younger populations
  • Greater vulnerability to economic shocks, conflict and climate change
  • Often rapid urbanisation, sometimes with informal settlements
  • Dependence on exports of commodities in some cases

Developing countries are extremely diverse. The group includes fast-growing economies such as India, Indonesia and Vietnam, upper-middle-income countries such as Brazil, Mexico and China, and low-income countries facing severe challenges.

Common Challenges for Developing Countries

  • Debt: many lower-income countries spend a large share of government revenue on debt repayments, leaving less for health and education.
  • Climate vulnerability: developing countries often face the worst effects of floods, droughts, heatwaves and cyclones despite having contributed least to historical emissions.
  • Commodity dependence: economies relying on a few exports, such as oil, minerals or crops, are exposed to price swings.
  • Informal employment: large informal sectors mean many workers lack job security, pensions and social protection, and governments collect less tax.
  • Brain drain: skilled professionals, such as doctors, nurses and engineers, may emigrate for better pay.
  • Governance and conflict: corruption, weak institutions and conflict can undermine development.
  • Infrastructure gaps: unreliable power, transport and internet raise costs for businesses and households.

Challenges Facing Developed Countries

Being developed doesn't mean being free of problems. Developed countries face their own challenges:

  • Ageing populations and pressure on pensions and health care.
  • Rising inequality and regional divides.
  • Housing affordability in major cities.
  • Slow productivity growth in many economies.
  • High carbon emissions per person and the need to decarbonise.
  • Mental health concerns, loneliness and declining trust in institutions.

Short Case Studies

South Korea

In the 1960s, South Korea was one of the poorer countries in the world. Through investment in education, export-led industrialisation, technology and infrastructure, it became a high-income economy with globally competitive companies in electronics, cars and shipbuilding. It moved from receiving aid to providing it, joining the OECD's Development Assistance Committee in 2010.

Botswana

At independence in 1966, Botswana was among the poorest countries in the world. Careful management of diamond revenues, relatively stable democratic institutions and investment in public services helped it achieve one of the fastest growth rates in the world for several decades. It graduated from the UN's least developed countries list in 1994 and is now an upper-middle-income country, though it still faces high inequality and the need to diversify beyond diamonds.

Bangladesh

Bangladesh has made notable progress since independence in 1971, driven by garment exports, remittances, microfinance and social programmes that improved health and girls' education. It met all three criteria for graduating from the least developed countries category and has been preparing for graduation, alongside challenges such as climate vulnerability and dependence on a single export sector.

Where Does India Fit?

India is classified by the World Bank as a lower-middle-income economy and by the UNDP in the medium human development category. The IMF includes it among emerging market and developing economies.

At the same time, India is one of the world's largest economies by total GDP and among the fastest-growing major economies. This illustrates a key point: total GDP measures the size of an economy, while GDP per capita reflects average living standards. India's large economy is spread across a population of more than 1.4 billion people, so income per person remains relatively low.

India has made significant progress in reducing extreme poverty, expanding electricity, sanitation, banking and digital payments, and improving life expectancy. Challenges remain in jobs, nutrition, learning outcomes, healthcare access and inequality between regions. The government's stated goal of becoming a developed nation by 2047, the centenary of independence, is often referred to as "Viksit Bharat @2047".

How Countries Move From Developing to Developed

Several economies have transformed within a few generations. South Korea, Singapore and Taiwan are frequently cited examples. In 2021, UNCTAD reclassified South Korea from the developing to the developed group, the first such change since the organisation was founded in 1964. Common factors in these transformations include:

  • Heavy investment in education and skills
  • Export-oriented industrialisation
  • High rates of saving and investment
  • Improving infrastructure
  • Effective institutions and long-term planning
  • Adoption of technology and innovation
  • Land reform and broad-based growth in some cases

Others have stalled at middle-income levels, a situation economists call the "middle-income trap", where growth slows before countries reach high income.

Criticisms of the Developed/Developing Labels

  • Oversimplification: grouping more than 150 diverse countries as "developing" hides huge differences.
  • Income isn't everything: GDP per capita ignores inequality, environmental damage and wellbeing.
  • Implied hierarchy: the labels suggest that richer countries are a model everyone should follow.
  • Internal inequality: rich countries have pockets of deep poverty, and developing countries have wealthy cities and elites.
  • Outdated origins: many categories emerged in the mid-twentieth century and don't reflect today's world.
  • Political misuse: developing status can carry advantages in trade negotiations, encouraging countries to keep the label.

To address these issues, organisations have developed broader measures, such as the Inequality-adjusted HDI, the Multidimensional Poverty Index and the Sustainable Development Goals, which track 17 goals including poverty, health, education, gender equality, climate action and peace.

Why These Labels Matter in Everyday Life

Classifications might seem like abstract statistics, but they have real consequences for people.

  • Access to cheap finance: the World Bank's International Development Association provides grants and very low-interest loans mainly to the poorest countries. When a country's income rises above certain thresholds, it gradually loses access to these terms and must borrow on more expensive conditions.
  • Trade preferences: least developed countries can export many goods duty-free to major markets. Graduating from LDC status can mean losing some of these preferences after a transition period, which can affect exporters and workers.
  • Climate finance: international climate agreements distinguish between countries' responsibilities and capabilities, which affects who provides and who receives support.
  • Investment: investors often group countries by classification, so being labelled "emerging" or "frontier" can influence how much capital flows in.
  • National pride and policy: moving up a category is often celebrated, and governments set targets around these milestones.

How to Read Development Data Wisely

  • Check the source and year. Figures change annually, and different organisations publish different numbers.
  • Know what's being measured. Total GDP, GDP per capita and GDP at PPP tell very different stories.
  • Look at distribution. An average can hide large inequalities between regions, genders and social groups.
  • Compare trends, not just snapshots. The direction and speed of change often matter more than a single year's ranking.
  • Combine measures. Pair income data with health, education, poverty and environmental indicators.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a developed and developing country?

Developed countries have high incomes, long life expectancy, strong education and infrastructure, and economies based on services and advanced industry. Developing countries have lower incomes and face greater challenges in these areas.

Who decides if a country is developed?

There's no single authority. The World Bank, UNDP, IMF, UN and WTO each use their own systems for different purposes.

Is India a developed country?

No. India is classified as a lower-middle-income country with medium human development, though it's one of the world's largest and fastest-growing major economies.

Is China a developed or developing country?

The World Bank classifies China as upper-middle income, and the IMF lists it among emerging and developing economies. China still describes itself as a developing country, though this is debated given its economic size.

What does LDC mean?

LDC stands for least developed country, a UN category for low-income countries facing severe structural barriers to development.

What is the Global South?

The Global South refers broadly to lower- and middle-income countries in Africa, Latin America, Asia and Oceania. It's a political and economic grouping rather than a strict geographic one.

Which is a better measure of development: GDP or HDI?

Each has strengths. GDP per capita measures economic output, while HDI adds health and education. Neither captures inequality or sustainability fully, so it's best to look at several measures together.

Can a country become developing again?

Yes. Income-based classifications can move in either direction. Economic crises, conflict, currency collapses or sanctions have caused some countries to fall into a lower World Bank income group, sometimes temporarily.

Are rich oil-producing countries developed?

Several Gulf states, such as Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, are classified as high income by the World Bank and have very high HDI scores. However, some experts argue that heavy dependence on oil and gas and limited economic diversification make their development different from that of industrialised economies.

What is the middle-income trap?

It describes countries whose growth slows after reaching middle income, often because they can no longer compete on low wages but haven't yet built the skills, innovation and institutions needed to compete with high-income economies.

Final Thoughts

The labels "developed" and "developing" offer a quick way to describe global differences in income and living standards, but they hide as much as they reveal. Different organisations use different systems for different purposes, and modern measures look beyond income to health, education, inequality, sustainability and wellbeing. Understanding these classifications helps us see both how far many countries have come and how much remains to be done to ensure that people everywhere can live long, healthy and fulfilling lives.
