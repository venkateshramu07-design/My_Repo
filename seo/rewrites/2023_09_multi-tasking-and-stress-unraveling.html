Quick answer: Multitasking often increases stress and reduces productivity. The human brain can't truly focus on two demanding tasks at once; instead, it rapidly switches between them, and each switch costs time, accuracy and mental energy. Research shows that frequent interruptions and task-switching lead people to work faster but with more stress, frustration and mistakes. Heavy media multitaskers may also find it harder to filter distractions. To reduce stress, focus on one task at a time, batch similar tasks, schedule focused work blocks, minimise notifications, and build short breaks into your day.

Answering emails during meetings, scrolling your phone while talking to family, switching between a dozen browser tabs, cooking while helping children with homework: modern life seems to demand constant multitasking. Many people take pride in juggling several things at once. Yet they often end the day exhausted, scattered and feeling they achieved little.

This article explains what happens in the brain when we multitask, why it increases stress, when multitasking can work, and practical strategies to work more calmly and effectively.

What Is Multitasking?

Multitasking means attempting to handle more than one task at the same time, or switching rapidly between tasks. There are three main forms:

  • Simultaneous multitasking: trying to do two things at once, such as texting while listening in a meeting.
  • Task switching: moving back and forth between tasks, such as writing a report while repeatedly checking email.
  • Background multitasking: doing an automatic task, such as walking, while doing another, such as talking.

The first two usually involve tasks competing for the same mental resources, which is where problems arise.

What Happens in the Brain

The Myth of Doing Two Things at Once

For tasks that require conscious attention, such as reading, writing, listening carefully or problem-solving, the brain can't process them fully in parallel. Instead, it switches attention rapidly between them. This switching happens so quickly that it feels like simultaneous processing, but it isn't.

Switching Costs

Each time you switch tasks, your brain must disengage from one set of rules and goals and load another. Research by Joshua Rubinstein, David Meyer and Jeffrey Evans, published in 2001, found that switching between tasks caused measurable time losses, especially for complex or unfamiliar tasks. The American Psychological Association has summarised research suggesting that such switching can cost as much as 40% of someone's productive time.

Attention Residue

Management researcher Sophie Leroy described "attention residue": when you move from one task to another, part of your attention stays stuck on the first task, reducing performance on the second. This is especially strong when the first task is unfinished.

The Prefrontal Cortex Under Strain

The prefrontal cortex manages attention, planning and decision-making. Constant switching taxes this region, contributing to mental fatigue and making it harder to concentrate later in the day.

How Multitasking Increases Stress

Interruptions and Pressure

Research by Gloria Mark and colleagues at the University of California, Irvine, found that when people were interrupted, they often compensated by working faster, completing tasks in similar time but experiencing higher stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. Other research by Mark has found that it can take a significant amount of time, often cited as around 23 minutes, to return to an interrupted task, as people move through other activities before resuming.

Shrinking Attention Spans

In her book Attention Span, Gloria Mark reports that people's average attention on a single screen before switching has fallen dramatically over the past two decades, to well under a minute in some studies. Constant switching can become a habit that's hard to break.

Cognitive Overload

Juggling multiple demands overwhelms working memory, the limited mental space we use to hold information temporarily. Overload makes people feel scattered and anxious.

Lower Quality and More Mistakes

Divided attention increases errors, which create more work and more stress.

A Sense of Never Finishing

When attention is spread across many tasks, few get completed, leaving a lingering sense of unfinished business.

Effects on Relationships

Multitasking during conversations, such as checking phones while family members talk, can make others feel ignored and reduce connection, adding relational stress.

What Research Says About Heavy Multitaskers

A 2009 study by Eyal Ophir, Clifford Nass and Anthony Wagner at Stanford University compared people who frequently multitasked with media, such as texting, browsing and watching videos simultaneously, with those who rarely did. Surprisingly, heavy media multitaskers performed worse on tests of filtering irrelevant information and switching tasks. The researchers suggested that frequent multitasking may make it harder to ignore distractions. Later studies have produced mixed results, but the findings raised important questions about digital habits.

Are Some People "Supertaskers"?

Research by David Strayer and Jason Watson in 2010 identified a small group of people, about 2.5% of participants, who could drive in a simulator while doing a demanding memory task without performance declines. These "supertaskers" are rare; the vast majority of people show clear impairment.

Multitasking and Safety

Multitasking can be dangerous. Studies by Strayer and colleagues found that drivers talking on mobile phones, including hands-free, showed impairments in reaction times and attention, in some measures comparable to drivers at the legal alcohol limit. Texting while driving is even more dangerous. Many countries, including India, prohibit using handheld phones while driving.

The Hidden Cost of "Always On"

Beyond individual tasks, constant multitasking can create a background state of alertness that never fully switches off. When you're always monitoring email, chat, news and social media while doing other things, your stress response may stay partly activated throughout the day. Over time, this can contribute to fatigue, irritability, poor sleep and difficulty relaxing even when you have free time. Many people notice that their minds feel restless during quiet moments because they've grown used to constant stimulation. Deliberately creating periods without inputs, such as a walk without headphones or a meal without screens, helps the nervous system recover.

Multitasking in Indian Workplaces

In many Indian offices, employees juggle multiple projects, frequent calls across time zones, messaging groups and in-person interruptions in open-plan spaces. Expectations of quick responses on messaging apps, including outside office hours, add to the pressure. Teams can reduce stress by agreeing on response-time norms, using separate channels for urgent and non-urgent matters, scheduling focus hours without meetings, and respecting off-hours. Leaders who model these habits make it easier for others to follow.

Building Your Focus Muscle

If you're used to constant switching, sustained focus can feel uncomfortable at first. Treat it like training:

  • Start with 15 to 20 minutes of single-tasking and gradually extend.
  • When you feel the urge to check your phone, note it and wait a few minutes.
  • Read longer articles or books regularly to rebuild sustained attention.
  • Track how many focus sessions you complete each day.
  • Reward yourself with a short break after each session.

When Multitasking Can Work

Not all multitasking is harmful. It can work when:

  • One task is automatic and low-effort, such as walking while talking or listening to music while doing chores.
  • Tasks use different senses and don't demand deep thinking.
  • The stakes are low and mistakes don't matter much.

Problems arise when two or more tasks demand conscious attention, memory or decision-making.

Deep Work and Flow

Computer scientist and author Cal Newport, in his 2016 book Deep Work, argues that the ability to concentrate without distraction on cognitively demanding tasks is becoming both rarer and more valuable. He contrasts "deep work" with "shallow work", such as routine emails and logistics, which is easy to do while distracted but creates little value. Psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi described "flow", a state of complete absorption in a challenging task, as one of the most satisfying human experiences. Both deep work and flow require uninterrupted attention, exactly what multitasking undermines. Protecting time for them can reduce stress and increase satisfaction as well as productivity.

Common Myths About Multitasking

  • "I'm good at multitasking." Research suggests people who believe they're good multitaskers often aren't; the Stanford study found heavy multitaskers performed worse on some attention tasks.
  • "Multitasking saves time." For complex tasks, switching costs usually make it slower.
  • "Young people are natural multitaskers." Growing up with technology doesn't remove the brain's limits on attention.
  • "Women are better multitaskers." Studies on gender differences are mixed and small; the costs of switching apply to everyone.
  • "Being constantly responsive shows commitment." Constant availability often reduces the quality of important work.

The Role of Technology

Smartphones, messaging apps and notifications are designed to capture attention, making multitasking almost automatic. But technology can also help protect focus:

  • Use focus modes and scheduled "do not disturb" settings.
  • Try website or app blockers during work sessions.
  • Turn off badges and banner notifications for non-essential apps.
  • Use a single task list to capture ideas instead of switching to act on them immediately.
  • Set status messages to let colleagues know when you're in focused work.

Meetings and Multitasking

Many professionals multitask during meetings, especially online, by answering emails or chats. This reduces both meeting quality and the quality of the other work. If you find yourself multitasking in meetings regularly, it may be a sign that you don't need to attend, or that the meeting needs a clearer agenda. Teams can agree on norms such as "cameras on, other windows closed" for key discussions, shorter meetings, and fewer attendees, so that the people present can give full attention.

Strategies to Reduce Multitasking and Stress

1. Single-Task Deliberately

Choose one important task and give it your full attention for a set period.

2. Use Time Blocks

Schedule blocks for focused work, and separate blocks for email, calls and meetings. The Pomodoro Technique, working for 25 minutes followed by a 5-minute break, is one popular method.

3. Batch Similar Tasks

Group emails, phone calls, errands or administrative work into specific times rather than handling them throughout the day.

4. Minimise Notifications

Turn off non-essential alerts on your phone and computer. Check messages at set times.

5. Close Unnecessary Tabs and Apps

Reduce visual distractions on your screen.

6. Prioritise

Decide your most important tasks for the day and tackle them first, when your energy is highest.

7. Finish Before Switching

Where possible, complete a task or reach a natural stopping point before moving on. If interrupted, write a quick note on where you stopped to reduce attention residue.

8. Set Boundaries

Communicate focus times to colleagues and family. Use "do not disturb" settings.

9. Be Present in Conversations

Put your phone away during meals and conversations.

10. Take Breaks

Short breaks restore attention and reduce mental fatigue.

11. Practise Mindfulness

Mindfulness training strengthens the ability to notice distraction and return attention to the task.

An Illustrative Example

Imagine a marketing executive whose typical day involves writing campaign plans while answering chat messages, joining video calls with email open in another window, and checking social media metrics every few minutes. By evening, they feel exhausted and anxious, yet their most important plan is still unfinished. Small errors slip into emails, and they often work late to catch up.

They decide to experiment for two weeks. Each morning, they block 9 to 11 am for focused work, with chat set to "do not disturb" and email closed. They check messages at 11 am, 2 pm and 4:30 pm. During meetings, they close other windows and take notes by hand. At home, phones stay off the dinner table.

By the end of the fortnight, they finish their campaign plan days ahead of schedule, make fewer mistakes and leave work on time more often. Colleagues adjust quickly to slightly slower replies. This example is illustrative, but it reflects what many people discover: protecting attention reduces stress and often increases output.

A Simple Focus-Friendly Daily Plan

  • Morning: start with your most important task for 60 to 90 minutes, before opening email or chat.
  • Late morning: process messages and quick tasks in one batch.
  • Early afternoon: schedule meetings and collaborative work.
  • Mid-afternoon: a second focus block for lighter but important work.
  • End of day: a final message check, then write tomorrow's top three priorities.

Multitasking at Home

Multitasking isn't only a workplace problem. Parents often juggle cooking, children's homework, household chores and work messages at once, leading to frustration and fatigue. Some practical ideas: set specific times for checking work messages at home, involve family members in chores, give children short periods of full attention rather than constant partial attention, and accept that not everything needs to happen at once. Being fully present for a shorter time often feels more meaningful to children and partners than long periods of distracted presence.

Multitasking and Students

Many students study with phones nearby, music playing and social media open. Research has found that students who switch frequently between studying and digital distractions tend to learn less effectively and may take longer to complete work. Simple habits help: keep phones in another room during study, use focused study sessions with short breaks, close unrelated tabs, and save messaging for breaks. Studying with full attention for shorter periods is usually more effective than long, distracted sessions.

Tips for Managers and Organisations

  • Reduce unnecessary meetings and keep them focused.
  • Set norms for response times to messages.
  • Protect focus time for teams.
  • Avoid expecting employees to monitor multiple communication channels constantly.
  • Prioritise projects clearly to avoid spreading people too thin.

Signs Multitasking Is Hurting You

  • You feel busy all day but finish few important tasks.
  • You often forget what you were doing after an interruption.
  • Mistakes and small oversights are increasing.
  • You feel mentally exhausted by mid-afternoon.
  • You struggle to read or concentrate for long periods.
  • People close to you say you seem distracted when they talk to you.

Key Takeaways

  • The brain switches between demanding tasks rather than doing them simultaneously.
  • Switching costs time, accuracy and energy, and leaves attention residue.
  • Interruptions increase stress, frustration and time pressure.
  • Single-tasking, time blocks, batching and fewer notifications reduce stress.
  • Multitasking works best only for simple, automatic activities.

A One-Week Single-Tasking Challenge

  1. Day 1: notice and count how often you switch tasks or check your phone in one hour.
  2. Day 2: turn off all non-essential notifications.
  3. Day 3: schedule one 45-minute focus block for your most important task.
  4. Day 4: batch email and messages into three set times.
  5. Day 5: attend meetings with all other windows closed.
  6. Day 6: have one meal and one conversation completely phone-free.
  7. Day 7: review what changed in your stress, output and mood, and keep the habits that helped.

When to Seek Help

If you find it extremely difficult to focus, organise tasks or finish work, despite trying strategies like these, and this has been true since childhood, it may be worth speaking to a doctor about attention difficulties such as ADHD. Similarly, if stress from juggling responsibilities is affecting your sleep, mood or health, consider talking to a professional. In India, Tele-MANAS (14416) offers free mental health support.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does multitasking cause stress?

Yes. Frequent task-switching and interruptions are linked to higher stress, frustration, mental fatigue and more mistakes.

Can the brain really multitask?

Not for tasks requiring conscious attention. The brain switches rapidly between them, which costs time and accuracy.

How much productivity does multitasking cost?

Research summarised by the American Psychological Association suggests switching can cost up to 40% of productive time, depending on tasks.

What is single-tasking?

Focusing on one task at a time with full attention, often for a set period.

Is multitasking ever useful?

Yes, when combining simple, automatic tasks, such as listening to music while cleaning.

How can I stop multitasking?

Start small: choose one daily focus block, silence notifications during it, close unrelated tabs and keep your phone out of reach. Gradually extend focus periods.

Is listening to music while working multitasking?

For many people, instrumental or familiar music in the background doesn't interfere much with routine work, but music with lyrics can distract during reading or writing. Notice what works for you.

How long does it take to refocus after an interruption?

It varies, but research by Gloria Mark and colleagues found people often took a long time, frequently cited as around 23 minutes, to return to an interrupted task, because they moved through other activities before resuming.

Can mindfulness reduce multitasking?

Yes. Mindfulness practice helps you notice when attention drifts and return to the task, strengthening focus over time.

Is task switching the same as multitasking?

Task switching is the most common form of multitasking: moving rapidly between tasks, each switch carrying a cost in time and attention.

Why do I feel so tired after a day of multitasking?

Constant switching taxes the brain's attention and control systems, increasing mental fatigue even if the individual tasks aren't difficult.

Does multitasking affect memory?

Divided attention can reduce how well information is encoded into memory, so you may remember less of what you read or heard while multitasking.

What is attention residue?

A term from researcher Sophie Leroy describing how part of your attention remains on a previous task after switching, reducing performance on the new one.

Is it rude to not reply to messages immediately?

Not usually. Most messages aren't urgent. Agreeing on response expectations with colleagues and family, and offering a way to reach you for genuine emergencies, lets you focus without seeming unresponsive.

What is the Pomodoro Technique?

A time-management method developed by Francesco Cirillo in which you work for 25 minutes, take a 5-minute break, and take a longer break after four rounds, helping maintain focus and prevent fatigue.

Can multitasking cause anxiety?

Constant juggling and interruptions can raise stress and feelings of overwhelm, which may worsen anxiety in some people.

Does single-tasking really improve productivity?

For complex, important work, yes. Avoiding switching costs and attention residue usually means higher quality work completed in less total time.

Final Thoughts

Multitasking may feel efficient, but for most demanding work it increases stress, mistakes and fatigue while reducing real productivity. By focusing on one task at a time, managing interruptions, batching similar work and creating space for deep concentration, you can work more calmly, think more clearly and feel more in control of your day.
