Quick answer: Six clear signs it may be time to quit your job are: (1) your health is suffering, physically or mentally; (2) you dread work almost every day; (3) there's no room to grow or learn; (4) the workplace is toxic, with bullying, disrespect or unethical behaviour; (5) you're seriously underpaid and your requests for fair pay go nowhere; and (6) your values and goals no longer match the company's direction. Before resigning, try to fix what can be fixed, build a financial cushion of three to six months of expenses, and ideally secure your next role first.

Almost everyone has days when they want to quit. A difficult meeting, a demanding client or a frustrating week can make any job feel unbearable. But there's a difference between a bad day and a bad job.

This guide covers six signs that it's genuinely time to move on, how to tell temporary frustration from a deeper problem, what to try before quitting, and how to leave wisely when the time comes.

Sign 1: Your Health Is Suffering

Work stress affects the body and mind. Warning signs include:

  • Persistent exhaustion that doesn't improve after weekends or holidays.
  • Trouble sleeping, or lying awake worrying about work.
  • Frequent headaches, stomach problems or getting ill often.
  • Anxiety, low mood, irritability or panic, especially on Sunday evenings.
  • Relying on alcohol, caffeine or comfort eating to get through the week.

The World Health Organization describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that hasn't been successfully managed. It's characterised by exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism about one's job, and reduced professional effectiveness. Long working hours are also a serious health risk: a joint WHO and International Labour Organization study estimated that working 55 or more hours a week was linked to hundreds of thousands of deaths from stroke and heart disease worldwide in 2016.

No job is worth your long-term health. If work is consistently making you unwell, take it seriously. Talk to a doctor, and consider whether changes at work or a new job are needed.

Sign 2: You Dread Going to Work Almost Every Day

Everyone has off days, but if you feel a sense of dread most mornings, count the hours until the weekend, and feel relief only when you're on leave, something is wrong. Ask yourself:

  • Is the dread about specific tasks, people, or the job as a whole?
  • Has it lasted for months, not just weeks?
  • Did you once enjoy this work, and what changed?

If the problem is a single difficult project that will end soon, it may pass. If the dread is constant and growing, it's a strong signal.

Sign 3: There's No Room to Grow

Career growth isn't only about promotions. It includes learning new skills, taking on more responsibility and doing work that challenges you. Signs you've stopped growing:

  • You could do your job in your sleep and haven't learned anything new in a year.
  • Promotions consistently go to others, or there simply aren't any opportunities.
  • Your manager isn't interested in your development.
  • Your skills are becoming outdated compared with the market.
  • Requests for training, new projects or stretch assignments are repeatedly denied.

Staying too long in a stagnant role can make it harder to move later, because your skills and achievements stop keeping pace with the market.

Sign 4: The Workplace Is Toxic

A toxic workplace goes beyond the normal frustrations of working life. Red flags include:

  • Bullying, harassment, shouting or public humiliation.
  • A manager who takes credit for your work, blames you for their mistakes or constantly micromanages.
  • Gossip, favouritism and cliques that decide who gets ahead.
  • A culture of fear, where people are afraid to speak up or make mistakes.
  • Unrealistic workloads treated as normal, with expectations to be available at all hours.
  • Discrimination based on gender, caste, religion, age or other characteristics.
  • Pressure to act unethically or illegally.

Some toxic situations can be addressed through HR or by moving to another team. But if the toxicity comes from senior leadership or is deeply embedded in the culture, it rarely changes quickly. Harassment and unethical demands should be reported through proper channels; in India, workplaces with ten or more employees are required to have an Internal Committee under the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013.

Sign 5: You're Underpaid and It's Not Changing

Pay isn't everything, but feeling undervalued erodes motivation. You may be underpaid if:

  • Salary surveys and job listings show similar roles pay significantly more.
  • New joiners in your role earn more than you.
  • Your raises haven't kept up with inflation for several years.
  • Your responsibilities have grown without matching pay.

Before quitting over pay, ask for a raise with evidence: your achievements, market data and the value you bring. If the company can't or won't move, and the gap is large, changing jobs is often the fastest way to a meaningful increase. Surveys in many markets have found that people who switch jobs tend to see larger pay increases than those who stay.

Sign 6: Your Values and Goals No Longer Fit

Sometimes nothing is "wrong" with a job, but it's no longer right for you. Signs include:

  • You disagree with the company's direction, products or ethics.
  • Your life priorities have changed: you want more flexibility, a different city, more meaningful work or a different career entirely.
  • You feel envious of friends doing different kinds of work.
  • You keep thinking about starting your own business or studying again.

Values misalignment can quietly drain energy over time. If you've changed and the job hasn't, it may be time to find a better fit.

Other Warning Signs

  • The company is in trouble: repeated layoffs, delayed salaries, leadership turmoil or losing major clients.
  • Your relationships are suffering because work takes all your time and energy.
  • You've been sidelined: excluded from meetings, given fewer responsibilities, or your role is being quietly reduced.
  • Good colleagues keep leaving, and nobody seems to ask why.
  • You've stopped caring about doing good work.

A useful exercise: keep a simple diary for two to four weeks, noting how you feel each day and why. Patterns become much clearer on paper.

What to Try Before Quitting

Quitting is sometimes the right answer, but it's worth checking whether the problem can be fixed first:

  1. Talk to your manager. Explain specifically what's not working and what you'd like to change, such as workload, responsibilities, flexibility or development.
  2. Ask for a raise or promotion with clear evidence.
  3. Request a transfer to another team, location or project.
  4. Negotiate flexibility, such as hybrid work or adjusted hours.
  5. Set boundaries around after-hours availability.
  6. Use HR or grievance processes for harassment, discrimination or unfair treatment.
  7. Take a proper break to see if the problem is exhaustion rather than the job itself.
  8. Invest in learning through courses or side projects that bring back a sense of growth.

If you've tried these and nothing changes, you'll leave knowing you made an informed decision.

A Decision Framework: Should I Stay or Go?

When emotions run high, a structured approach helps. Score each area from 1 (very poor) to 5 (excellent) for your current job:

AreaQuestions to ask
Health and wellbeingCan I do this job without harming my physical or mental health?
ManagerDoes my manager treat me fairly and support my development?
GrowthAm I learning and moving toward my long-term goals?
Pay and benefitsAm I paid fairly compared with the market?
Culture and colleaguesDo I feel respected and safe?
Work-life balanceDo I have enough time and energy for my life outside work?
MeaningDoes the work feel worthwhile to me?
StabilityIs the company financially healthy?

If most scores are 1s and 2s, and attempts to improve them have failed, leaving is likely the right decision. If only one or two areas are low, it may be worth trying to fix those first. Some people also score a realistic alternative job the same way to compare.

The Hidden Costs of Staying Too Long

People often stay in unhappy jobs because leaving feels risky. But staying also has costs, even if they're less visible:

  • Health: chronic stress takes a toll on sleep, mood and physical health.
  • Career momentum: years without new skills or achievements can make your CV less competitive.
  • Earnings: staying underpaid compounds over time, because future raises and offers are often based on your current salary.
  • Relationships: stress and long hours spill over into family life and friendships.
  • Confidence: a toxic or demoralising workplace can make you doubt your abilities.

Weighing these costs alongside the risks of leaving gives a more honest picture of the decision.

Why People Stay in Jobs They Should Leave

  • Fear of the unknown: a familiar problem can feel safer than an uncertain future.
  • Sunk cost thinking: "I've invested so many years here, I can't leave now."
  • Financial pressure: loans, EMIs and family responsibilities make change feel impossible.
  • Loyalty and guilt toward colleagues or a manager.
  • Social expectations: pressure from family to keep a "stable" or prestigious job.
  • Low confidence about finding something better.

Recognising which of these is holding you back helps you address it, for example by building savings to reduce financial fear, or talking to recruiters to test your market value.

How to Job Hunt While Employed

  • Schedule interviews early in the morning, at lunchtime or after work where possible, or use leave days.
  • Use your personal phone, email and laptop for your job search.
  • Set your LinkedIn to signal openness only to recruiters, not publicly.
  • Keep doing good work in your current role; your reputation matters for references.
  • Be selective. Apply for roles that genuinely fix the problems you're leaving, rather than any job that gets you out.
  • Ask good questions in interviews about culture, management style, workload and growth to avoid repeating the same situation.

How to Quit the Right Way

1. Build a Financial Cushion

Ideally, have three to six months of living expenses saved before you resign, especially if you don't have another job lined up. Job searches often take longer than expected.

2. Secure Your Next Role First, If Possible

It's generally easier to find a job while you have one, and you'll negotiate from a stronger position. The exception is when your health or safety is at serious risk; then leaving sooner may be the right choice.

3. Update Your CV and Network

Refresh your CV and LinkedIn profile with recent achievements and measurable results. Reconnect with former colleagues, mentors and recruiters. Many jobs are filled through referrals.

4. Understand Your Contract

Check your notice period, any non-compete or confidentiality clauses, bonus or stock vesting dates, and whether you need to repay joining bonuses or training costs. In India, notice periods of 30 to 90 days are common, and some employers allow a "buyout" of the notice period.

5. Resign Professionally

  • Tell your manager in person or by video call first, then follow up with a short, polite resignation letter or email.
  • Keep it positive and brief. You don't need to list every complaint.
  • Offer to help with the handover.

6. Handle Counter-Offers Carefully

Your employer may offer more money to keep you. Before accepting, ask whether money was really the main reason you wanted to leave. If the underlying problems are culture, growth or management, a raise rarely fixes them for long.

7. Leave on Good Terms

Complete a thorough handover, thank colleagues, and stay professional until your last day. Industries are smaller than they seem, and you may need references or work with former colleagues again.

8. Take Care of Practical Details

Collect your relieving letter and experience certificate, settle your full and final pay, and transfer or withdraw your provident fund (PF) correctly. Download any personal documents you're entitled to keep, and make sure your health insurance doesn't lapse between jobs.

Interview Questions That Reveal a Good Workplace

To avoid jumping from one bad job to another, ask questions like these when interviewing:

  • "Why is this position open?"
  • "How would you describe the team culture?"
  • "What does a typical week look like, and how often do people work late or on weekends?"
  • "How do you support learning and career development?"
  • "How is performance measured, and how often is feedback given?"
  • "What have people who succeeded in this role done well?"
  • "How long have team members been here, on average?"

Also look up company reviews on sites such as Glassdoor and AmbitionBox, and if possible speak to current or former employees. Look for consistent patterns rather than single angry reviews.

Looking After Yourself During the Transition

Leaving a job, even a bad one, can be emotionally draining. Uncertainty, rejections and the stress of serving notice all add up. Keep a routine, exercise, stay connected with friends, and remind yourself that a job search is a numbers game. Each "no" brings you closer to the right "yes". If you take a break between jobs, use part of it to genuinely rest and reflect on what you want next.

What If You Want to Change Careers Entirely?

If the problem isn't just your current job but your whole field, a career change may be the answer. Steps that help:

  • Talk to people who work in the field you're considering.
  • Test the waters through short courses, freelance projects, volunteering or internships.
  • Identify transferable skills, such as communication, project management or data analysis.
  • Plan financially for a possible temporary pay cut.
  • Consider a gradual transition rather than an abrupt leap.

An Illustrative Example

Sneha had worked as a business analyst at the same company for six years. She felt exhausted, her manager dismissed her ideas, and she hadn't had a meaningful raise in three years. She kept a diary for a month, which confirmed that the frustration was constant, not occasional. She first asked for a transfer and a salary review; both were refused. She then built up four months of savings, updated her CV and spent two months interviewing. She accepted a role at another company with a 30% higher salary and a supportive manager. Looking back, she says the biggest mistake was waiting so long, and the best decision was planning her exit instead of resigning in anger.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it bad to quit a job without another one lined up?

It's riskier financially and can make job searching more stressful, but it can be the right choice if your health or safety is at risk, or if you have enough savings and a clear plan. Be ready to explain the gap positively in interviews.

How long should I stay in a job before leaving?

There's no fixed rule. Many people aim for at least a year to show commitment, but staying in a harmful or stagnant job just to tick a box isn't wise. A pattern of very short stints may raise questions with employers.

How do I explain leaving my job in an interview?

Focus on what you're moving toward rather than what you're escaping: "I'm looking for a role with more responsibility in data analytics" sounds better than criticising your old employer.

What should I say in a resignation letter?

Keep it short: state that you're resigning, give your last working day according to your notice period, thank your employer for the opportunity, and offer to help with the transition.

Should I tell colleagues I'm job hunting?

Generally, it's wise to keep it private until you've accepted an offer and informed your manager, to avoid it reaching the wrong people.

What if I feel guilty about leaving my team?

Feeling loyal is natural, but you're responsible for your own career and wellbeing. A professional handover is the best way to support your team as you leave.

Can I quit during my probation period?

Yes. Probation periods usually have shorter notice periods, sometimes as little as a week to a month. Check your offer letter or contract for the exact terms.

What if my employer refuses to accept my resignation?

An employer generally cannot force you to stay beyond your contractual notice period, but they may insist you serve it in full or pay in lieu. Keep communication in writing and, if there's a serious dispute over dues or relieving documents, consider seeking advice from a labour law professional.

Is it normal to feel anxious after resigning?

Yes. Even when it's the right decision, change brings uncertainty. Focus on your reasons for leaving and the steps you've taken to prepare.

Should I take a lower-paying job for better work-life balance?

It can be a good decision if your budget allows it and the trade-off improves your health and relationships. Work out your essential expenses first, and consider whether the new role still offers growth so your income can rise again over time.

Is "quiet quitting" a good alternative to resigning?

Doing only what your role requires and setting healthy boundaries can be reasonable. But if you've completely disengaged, it often means the job isn't right, and staying long term may hurt your reputation and wellbeing.

Final Thoughts

Quitting a job is a big decision, but staying in the wrong one can cost you your health, growth and happiness. If your well-being is suffering, you dread work daily, you've stopped growing, the environment is toxic, you're underpaid with no prospect of change, or your values no longer fit, take those signs seriously. Try to fix what you can, plan your finances and your next step, and leave professionally. The right move can transform not only your career, but your whole life.
