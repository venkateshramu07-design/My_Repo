Quick answer: Ikigai is a Japanese word that roughly means "a reason for being" or "what makes life worth living". In everyday Japanese use, ikigai can be found in small, ordinary things, such as caring for family, tending a garden, a morning routine, a hobby or one's work, not only in a grand life mission. The popular Western diagram that defines ikigai as the overlap of what you love, what you're good at, what the world needs and what you can be paid for is a modern adaptation rather than the traditional Japanese idea. You can explore your ikigai by noticing what brings you joy and meaning, starting small, nurturing relationships, staying active, being present and contributing to others.

Ikigai has become a global wellness buzzword, appearing in bestselling books, career workshops and social media posts. It's often linked to the long, healthy lives of people in Okinawa, Japan. But what does ikigai really mean, where did the popular diagram come from, and how can you apply it in your own life?

This guide explores the origins and meaning of ikigai, what research says about purpose and wellbeing, the difference between the Japanese concept and its Western interpretation, and practical ways to discover and nurture your own ikigai.

What Does Ikigai Mean?

The word combines iki, relating to life or living, and gai, meaning value, worth or effect. Together, ikigai refers to something that gives life meaning, joy or purpose.

In Japan, ikigai is a common, everyday word. People might say their ikigai is their grandchildren, their morning walk, their work as a craftsperson, their garden, playing music or meeting friends. Ikigai doesn't have to be a single, lofty purpose; people can have several ikigai, and they can be modest.

Origins of the Concept

The idea of ikigai has deep roots in Japanese culture. Psychiatrist Mieko Kamiya's 1966 book Ikigai-ni-tsuite ("On the Meaning of Life") explored ikigai as a source of meaning, especially for people facing hardship, drawing on her work with patients. Later, Japanese researchers studied ikigai in relation to health and ageing.

Neuroscientist Ken Mogi, in his book Awakening Your Ikigai, describes five pillars of ikigai:

  1. Starting small
  2. Releasing yourself (letting go of ego and self-importance)
  3. Harmony and sustainability
  4. The joy of little things
  5. Being in the here and now

Ken Mogi's Five Pillars Explained

1. Starting Small

Ikigai doesn't require dramatic life changes. It often begins with small, achievable actions, such as perfecting a recipe, caring for a plant or improving one aspect of your work, done with care and attention.

2. Releasing Yourself

Mogi emphasises letting go of ego and the need for recognition. Finding joy in an activity for its own sake, rather than for praise or status, allows deeper satisfaction.

3. Harmony and Sustainability

Ikigai is connected to living in harmony with others, the community and the environment. Sustainable habits and relationships support long-term wellbeing.

4. The Joy of Little Things

Appreciating small pleasures, such as the first sip of morning tea, seasonal changes or a friendly greeting, makes daily life meaningful.

5. Being in the Here and Now

Mindful presence, giving full attention to what you're doing, allows you to experience flow and contentment in ordinary moments.

Ikigai in Hardship

Mieko Kamiya developed her ideas about ikigai while working as a psychiatrist with patients at a leprosarium in Japan, people who had been isolated from society and faced great suffering. She observed that some patients found meaning and reasons to live despite their circumstances, through relationships, creativity, faith and helping others. Her work suggests that ikigai is not only for comfortable lives; it can be a source of resilience in the hardest situations, and can be rediscovered after loss.

The Popular Ikigai Venn Diagram

Many people know ikigai through a diagram of four overlapping circles:

  • What you love
  • What you're good at
  • What the world needs
  • What you can be paid for

Ikigai is shown at the centre, where all four overlap, with other overlaps labelled passion, mission, profession and vocation. This diagram is a Western creation. It was adapted from a "purpose" diagram by Spanish astrologer Andrés Zuzunaga, and blogger Marc Winn added the word "ikigai" to it in 2014. It spread rapidly online and in career coaching.

The diagram can be a useful tool for thinking about meaningful careers, but it differs from the traditional Japanese understanding, in which ikigai doesn't need to involve earning money or being exceptionally skilled. A retired person whose ikigai is caring for plants or spending time with grandchildren has ikigai just as much as a successful professional.

Ikigai and Longevity

Ikigai is often associated with Okinawa, a Japanese island region known for many people living into their nineties and beyond. Writer Dan Buettner included Okinawa among the "Blue Zones", regions with unusually high longevity. Okinawans traditionally emphasise purpose, social connection through groups called moai, plant-rich diets and daily physical activity.

The book Ikigai: The Japanese Secret to a Long and Happy Life by Héctor García and Francesc Miralles, published in 2016, became an international bestseller by linking ikigai with Okinawan lifestyles.

What Research Says About Purpose and Health

  • The Ohsaki Study: a large Japanese study published in 2008 by Toshimasa Sone and colleagues followed more than 40,000 adults for about seven years. People who reported not having a sense of ikigai had a higher risk of death, particularly from cardiovascular disease, than those who did, after adjusting for other factors.
  • Purpose in life research: studies in other countries, including research led by Patricia Boyle at Rush University, have linked a strong sense of purpose with lower risk of mortality and cognitive decline in older adults.
  • Wellbeing: purpose is associated with resilience, better coping with stress and greater life satisfaction.

These studies show associations, not proof that purpose alone causes better health, but they suggest meaning and wellbeing are closely connected.

How to Discover Your Ikigai

1. Notice What Brings You Joy

Pay attention to moments when you feel happy, absorbed or content. What were you doing, and with whom?

2. Reflect on What Matters to You

Ask: What do I care about deeply? What would I miss most if it disappeared from my life?

3. Start Small

Ikigai can grow from small daily rituals: making tea mindfully, a morning walk, cooking for family or practising a skill.

4. Explore and Experiment

Try new hobbies, volunteer, learn skills or join groups. Ikigai is often discovered through action, not just reflection.

5. Use the Four Questions as a Guide

For career thinking, the Western diagram's questions can help: What do I love? What am I good at? What does the world need? What can I be paid for? Just remember that ikigai can exist outside work too.

6. Connect With Others

Relationships are a common source of ikigai. Invest in family, friendships and community.

7. Contribute

Helping others, through work, volunteering or simple kindness, gives a sense of meaning.

8. Be Present

Mindfulness helps you notice and appreciate the small joys that make life worthwhile.

9. Let Ikigai Evolve

Your ikigai may change with life stages: studies, career, parenthood, retirement. That's natural.

Ikigai and Craftsmanship

Japanese culture places great value on craftsmanship and the dedication to improve one's work over a lifetime, an attitude sometimes described as shokunin spirit. Sushi chefs, potters, carpenters and sword makers may spend decades refining their skills, finding meaning in the work itself rather than only in recognition or reward. This mindset can apply to any job: taking pride in doing something well, continuously improving and serving others through your work can turn ordinary tasks into a source of ikigai.

Ikigai and Kaizen

The Japanese concept of kaizen, continuous improvement through small steps, complements ikigai. Rather than seeking dramatic transformation, kaizen encourages small, consistent improvements in habits, work and relationships. Applying kaizen to your search for ikigai means making small changes, such as spending ten minutes a day on a hobby or one evening a week volunteering, and building on them gradually.

Ikigai at Work

  • Connect daily tasks to the people they help.
  • Develop mastery in your craft; growing skill can bring satisfaction.
  • Seek "flow" experiences, where you're fully absorbed in meaningful work.
  • Build positive relationships with colleagues.
  • If your job doesn't provide meaning, look for ikigai in other parts of life, or explore gradual career changes.

Daily Practices That Nurture Ikigai

  • A meaningful morning ritual: begin the day with something you value, such as a walk, prayer, tea on the balcony or reading.
  • Mindful work: give full attention to at least one task each day and take pride in doing it well.
  • Connection: share a meal or conversation with someone you care about.
  • Learning: spend a little time developing a skill or curiosity.
  • Movement: gentle, regular activity such as walking, gardening or yoga.
  • Gratitude: notice and appreciate small pleasures before sleep.

Ikigai in Indian Life

Many Indians may recognise ikigai-like ideas in their own traditions and daily lives: the satisfaction of caring for family, the community spirit of festivals, the discipline of morning prayers or yoga, the pride of craftspeople and farmers in their work, and the value placed on seva, selfless service. Grandparents who continue to guide families, teach traditions and cook beloved recipes often embody ikigai. Rapid urbanisation and busy lives can erode these sources of meaning, making it worth consciously preserving rituals, relationships and community involvement.

Ikigai in Retirement

Retirement can bring a loss of identity and structure. Ikigai can come from hobbies, volunteering, mentoring, caring for grandchildren, learning, gardening and community involvement. In Japan, many older adults continue working part-time or engaging in community activities, maintaining purpose and social connection.

Journaling Prompts to Explore Your Ikigai

  • When do I lose track of time because I'm so absorbed?
  • What small moments in my week do I look forward to most?
  • What have people thanked me for or come to me for help with?
  • What problems in the world or my community do I care about?
  • What did I love doing as a child?
  • If money weren't a concern, how would I spend my days?
  • Who do I most enjoy spending time with, and why?

An Illustrative Example

Imagine a 45-year-old bank employee who feels her job is stable but uninspiring. After reading about ikigai, she initially tries to find a single grand purpose that fits the four-circle diagram and feels frustrated when nothing obvious appears.

She shifts approach and starts noticing small joys. She realises she loves her early-morning walks, feels energised when she teaches younger colleagues, and enjoys cooking for neighbours during festivals. She begins mentoring new joiners formally at work, joins a weekend walking group, and volunteers to teach basic financial literacy at a community centre once a month.

Her job hasn't changed, but her life feels richer and more meaningful, with several sources of ikigai woven through her week. This example is illustrative, but it reflects the traditional Japanese view that ikigai often lives in everyday activities and relationships.

Ikigai and Related Ideas

  • Flow: psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi described the deep satisfaction of being fully absorbed in a challenging activity, a common experience in ikigai.
  • Eudaimonia: the ancient Greek idea of flourishing through living according to virtue and purpose.
  • Svadharma: in Indian philosophy, the idea of one's own duty or path in life, discussed in the Bhagavad Gita, shares some similarities with finding meaningful purpose.
  • Logotherapy: psychiatrist Viktor Frankl, author of Man's Search for Meaning, argued that the search for meaning is a primary human motivation.

Lessons From Okinawa

  • Moai: lifelong social support groups that meet regularly.
  • Hara hachi bu: a traditional practice of eating until about 80% full.
  • Daily movement: gardening, walking and household activities rather than intense workouts.
  • Plant-rich diets with vegetables, tofu and sweet potatoes.
  • Respect for elders and continued roles in family and community.

Okinawa's lifestyle has changed in recent decades with modernisation, but these traditional practices remain inspiring examples of how purpose, community and daily habits can support wellbeing.

Using Ikigai to Guide a Career Change

If you're considering a new career direction, ikigai-inspired reflection can help, as long as you approach it practically:

  1. List your strengths and the skills others value in you.
  2. Identify interests that energise you and problems you care about.
  3. Research roles that combine these, and talk to people working in them.
  4. Test ideas cheaply through short courses, volunteering, freelance projects or side work.
  5. Plan finances before making a big leap.
  6. Remember balance: a job doesn't have to satisfy every circle; meaning can also come from outside work.

Ikigai for Parents

For many parents, children become a central source of ikigai. This can be deeply fulfilling, but it's also healthy for parents to maintain other sources of meaning, such as friendships, interests and personal growth, so that their sense of purpose doesn't rest entirely on their children, especially as children grow up and become independent. Sharing your own interests with children can also help them discover theirs.

Obstacles to Finding Ikigai

  • Busyness: constant rushing leaves little time to notice what brings joy.
  • Comparison: measuring your purpose against others' achievements can make your own sources of meaning seem small.
  • Perfectionism: waiting for the perfect calling prevents you from exploring.
  • Burnout: exhaustion dulls interest and joy; rest may need to come first.
  • Depression: persistent low mood and loss of interest are symptoms that deserve professional support, not just a search for purpose.

A 30-Day Ikigai Exploration

  • Week 1: each evening, note one moment that brought you joy or meaning.
  • Week 2: answer the journaling prompts above and look for patterns.
  • Week 3: try one new activity or revisit an old interest.
  • Week 4: do something meaningful for someone else, and reflect on how it felt.

At the end, identify one or two small practices to continue.

Common Misconceptions

  • "Ikigai must be one big life purpose." It can be small and multiple.
  • "Ikigai must be your job." Many people find ikigai outside work.
  • "Ikigai must earn money." This is part of the Western diagram, not the traditional concept.
  • "Once you find it, you're done." Ikigai can change throughout life.

Key Takeaways

  • Ikigai means "a reason for being" and can be found in both small joys and larger purposes.
  • The four-circle Venn diagram is a Western adaptation, useful for career thinking but not the full Japanese concept.
  • Research links having ikigai or purpose with better health and longevity.
  • Ken Mogi's five pillars emphasise starting small, letting go of ego, harmony, little joys and presence.
  • Ikigai evolves throughout life and is often discovered through action and relationships.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does ikigai mean?

Ikigai means "a reason for being" or "what makes life worth living" in Japanese.

Is the ikigai Venn diagram Japanese?

No. It's a Western adaptation popularised in 2014, based on an earlier purpose diagram.

How do I find my ikigai?

Notice what brings you joy and meaning, experiment with activities, nurture relationships, contribute to others and pay attention to small daily pleasures.

Does ikigai help you live longer?

Research such as Japan's Ohsaki Study links having ikigai or a sense of purpose with lower mortality risk, though other factors also matter.

Can I have more than one ikigai?

Yes. Many people find ikigai in several areas of life.

What is a moai?

In Okinawa, a moai is a social support group of friends who meet regularly throughout life, offering companionship and practical help.

Can young people find ikigai?

Yes. Students and young adults can find ikigai in learning, friendships, creative pursuits, sport and service, and it can evolve as life changes.

Who wrote the bestselling book on ikigai?

Héctor García and Francesc Miralles wrote Ikigai: The Japanese Secret to a Long and Happy Life (2016). Ken Mogi's Awakening Your Ikigai offers a Japanese neuroscientist's perspective.

What if I can't find my ikigai?

Don't force it. Focus on small daily joys, relationships and trying new things. Ikigai often reveals itself gradually. If you feel persistently empty or hopeless, consider speaking to a counsellor.

Is ikigai the same as passion?

Not exactly. Passion is intense enthusiasm; ikigai is broader and can include quiet, steady sources of meaning such as routines and relationships.

Can ikigai change over time?

Yes. Ikigai often shifts with life stages, such as studying, starting a career, raising children or retiring, and that's a natural part of growing.

How is ikigai linked to Okinawa?

Okinawa is known for high longevity, and researchers and writers have linked this partly to residents' strong sense of purpose, social connection and active lifestyles.

Does ikigai have to involve other people?

Not necessarily, but relationships and contributing to others are common sources of ikigai and are strongly linked to wellbeing.

How is ikigai different from happiness?

Happiness often refers to positive feelings, while ikigai focuses on what makes life worth living. Ikigai can include effort, responsibility and even struggle, as long as it feels meaningful.

Is the ikigai diagram still useful?

Yes, as a tool for thinking about meaningful work. Just remember it's a modern adaptation, and that ikigai can also be found in relationships, hobbies and everyday life.

Who was Mieko Kamiya?

A Japanese psychiatrist whose 1966 book Ikigai-ni-tsuite explored the meaning of life and the sources of ikigai, drawing on her work with patients facing great hardship.

Can ikigai help with stress?

A sense of purpose is associated with better coping and resilience. Having meaningful activities and relationships can provide perspective and support during stressful times.

What does "hara hachi bu" mean?

An Okinawan practice of eating until about 80% full, often cited as one of the region's healthy traditions.

Final Thoughts

Ikigai invites us to notice what makes life worth living, whether that's meaningful work, loved ones, a creative passion or simple daily joys. It doesn't require a grand mission or a perfect career; it grows from paying attention, staying engaged, connecting with others and contributing in ways that matter to you. Start small, stay curious and let your ikigai evolve with you.
