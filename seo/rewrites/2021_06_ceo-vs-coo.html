Quick answer: The CEO (Chief Executive Officer) decides where the company is going: strategy, vision, major investments and the relationship with the board, investors and the public. The COO (Chief Operating Officer) decides how the company gets there: running daily operations, building processes and making sure teams deliver on the plan. The CEO is the top executive and usually has more authority and higher pay, but the best companies treat the two roles as a partnership.

If you have ever looked at a company's leadership page and wondered why it needs both a CEO and a COO, you are not alone. Both titles sound like "the boss", both sit at the top of the organisation chart, and in small companies the same person often does both jobs. Yet in larger organisations the roles are deliberately split, because no single person can set long-term strategy and also manage thousands of daily decisions well.

This guide explains what each role does, who holds more power, how the two divide responsibility for money and people, how their pay compares, and what makes the partnership work. If you are planning a leadership career, it also covers the skills and career paths that lead to each seat.

What Does a CEO Do?

The CEO is the highest-ranking executive in a company. In most corporations, the CEO reports to the board of directors, and everyone else in the business ultimately reports to the CEO. The role is less about doing tasks and more about making the few decisions that shape everything else.

Core Responsibilities of a CEO

  • Setting the vision and strategy: deciding which markets to compete in, which products to build and which to stop, and what the company should look like in five or ten years.
  • Allocating capital: choosing where money goes, including acquisitions, large investments, hiring plans and dividends or buybacks.
  • Building the leadership team: hiring, developing and, when necessary, replacing senior executives, including the COO.
  • Representing the company: speaking to investors, analysts, regulators, major customers, partners and the media.
  • Shaping culture: setting the values and behaviours the organisation rewards, often more through example than through policy.
  • Answering to the board: reporting on performance and being accountable for overall results.

A useful way to think about it: the CEO is responsible for making the right bets. If the company is efficiently executing the wrong strategy, that is the CEO's problem.

What Does a COO Do?

The COO is usually the second-highest executive and is responsible for the internal engine of the business. Where the CEO looks outward and forward, the COO looks inward and at the present: are teams hitting targets, are processes working, are costs under control, and can the company scale?

Core Responsibilities of a COO

  • Running day-to-day operations: overseeing functions such as manufacturing, supply chain, customer service, logistics and often sales or HR.
  • Turning strategy into execution: translating the CEO's plan into budgets, targets, projects and timelines.
  • Designing processes and systems: building the workflows, tools and reporting that let the company operate reliably at scale.
  • Managing performance: tracking key metrics, spotting problems early and fixing bottlenecks.
  • Leading cross-team coordination: making sure departments work together rather than in silos.

The COO role varies more than any other C-suite job. In some companies the COO is effectively the CEO's chief of staff; in others, the COO runs most of the business while the CEO focuses on investors and strategy; and in some, the COO is the CEO-in-waiting.

Who Is More Powerful, the CEO or the COO?

In formal terms, the CEO is more powerful. The CEO sits above the COO in the hierarchy, usually has the final say on major decisions, and in many companies can hire or remove the COO. The CEO answers only to the board, while the COO answers to the CEO.

In practice, influence can be more balanced. A COO who controls most of the company's people and budget, and who has the CEO's trust, can have enormous day-to-day authority. Some well-known partnerships, such as Sheryl Sandberg's long tenure as COO alongside Mark Zuckerberg at Facebook, show how a COO can shape a company almost as much as its CEO. Still, when the two genuinely disagree on a major issue, the CEO decides, and the board decides if the CEO cannot.

How Authority Is Usually Divided

  • Decisions reserved for the CEO: company strategy, mergers and acquisitions, senior leadership appointments, major capital allocation and public commitments.
  • Decisions delegated to the COO: operating budgets, process changes, staffing within approved plans, vendor choices and resolving cross-team conflicts.
  • Shared decisions: annual plans, restructuring, large hiring changes and anything that affects both strategy and daily operations.

Clear "decision rights" matter. When people inside the company don't know whether to take a question to the CEO or the COO, decisions slow down and political games start.

How the CEO and COO Influence Financial Management

Neither role replaces the Chief Financial Officer (CFO), but both shape the company's finances.

  • The CEO sets financial priorities: growth versus profitability, how much risk to take, whether to raise capital, and how to communicate results to investors. The CEO usually signs off on the annual budget and is accountable for the company's overall financial health.
  • The COO controls much of the spending. Operating costs, headcount, supply chain contracts and productivity improvements typically sit under the COO, so the COO has a big impact on margins.
  • The CFO works with both, providing forecasts, controls and reporting so that strategy (CEO) and execution (COO) stay financially realistic.

Who Earns More, the CEO or the COO?

In almost every company the CEO earns more. CEO pay reflects the highest level of accountability and is usually weighted heavily toward bonuses, stock and long-term incentives tied to company performance. COOs are typically among the highest-paid executives after the CEO.

Actual figures vary enormously by company size, industry and country. At large listed companies, total CEO compensation can run into the millions or tens of millions per year, while COO pay is often a significant fraction of that. At startups and small businesses, both roles may pay modest salaries, with most of the reward coming from equity. If you are researching pay for a specific company, listed companies publish executive compensation in their annual reports or proxy statements.

Does Every Company Need Both?

No. Many companies don't have a COO at all. Whether one makes sense depends on the situation:

  • Fast-growing companies often add a COO when operations become too complex for the founder-CEO to manage alone.
  • Founder-led companies may hire an experienced COO to complement a visionary founder who is less interested in operations.
  • Succession planning: some boards appoint a COO to prepare a future CEO.
  • Small businesses usually have the owner or managing director doing both jobs, sometimes with an operations manager.

A COO is a poor fit when the role is created without clear responsibilities, or when the CEO is unwilling to delegate real authority. In those cases the COO becomes a costly middle layer.

What Makes a Strong CEO and COO Partnership

The relationship between the CEO and COO is one of the most important in any company. When it works, the CEO can think long-term while trusting the business to run well. When it fails, the organisation receives mixed messages and stalls.

Habits of Effective Partnerships

  • Complementary strengths: a big-picture CEO often pairs best with a detail-oriented, systems-minded COO.
  • Clearly written responsibilities: both know, and everyone else knows, who owns which decisions.
  • One voice in public: disagreements happen in private; teams see a united front.
  • Regular, honest communication: frequent one-to-ones focused on priorities, risks and people.
  • Mutual trust: the CEO doesn't micromanage operations, and the COO doesn't undermine strategy.

How to Become a CEO or COO

There is no single path, but common patterns exist.

  • COOs often come from operations, supply chain, consulting, engineering or general management, where they have run large teams and complex processes.
  • CEOs often come from roles with profit-and-loss responsibility, such as running a business unit or region, or they found their own companies. Many CEOs were previously COOs, CFOs or division presidents.
  • Useful skills for both: leading people, financial literacy, clear communication, decision-making under uncertainty and a track record of measurable results.
  • Education: an MBA or similar qualification is common but not required. Experience of leading teams and delivering results matters far more.

Common Misconceptions

  • "The COO is just an assistant to the CEO." In most companies the COO runs a large part of the business with real authority.
  • "The CEO does the real work." Strategy without execution is just a slide deck. Both roles are essential.
  • "A COO always becomes the next CEO." Some do, but many COOs are specialists in operations and prefer to stay in that role.
  • "Every company should have a COO." Plenty of successful companies don't. The role only makes sense when it solves a real problem.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the COO higher than the CFO?

Usually both report directly to the CEO, so they are peers. In some companies the COO is considered the second-in-command and has a broader remit, but this depends on the organisation.

Can one person be both CEO and COO?

Yes. In small and medium businesses it is very common for one person to hold both responsibilities. Companies usually split the roles as they grow.

Who does the COO report to?

The COO almost always reports to the CEO. The CEO in turn reports to the board of directors.

What is the difference between a COO and an operations manager?

An operations manager typically runs a specific site, team or function. A COO oversees operations across the whole company and sits on the executive team that sets company-wide plans.

Is a COO the same as a president?

Not always. Some companies use "President" for the person running operations, others combine titles such as "President and COO". The title matters less than the actual responsibilities.

Final Thoughts

The CEO and COO are two halves of the same job: deciding what a company should do, and making sure it actually gets done. The CEO holds the final authority and the outward-facing role; the COO turns plans into results inside the organisation. Whether you are trying to understand a company's leadership or planning your own path to the top, remember that the strongest companies are rarely built by one person. They are built by leaders who know their roles, trust each other and pull in the same direction.
