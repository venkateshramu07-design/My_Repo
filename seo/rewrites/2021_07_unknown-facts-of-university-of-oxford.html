Quick answer: The University of Oxford is the oldest university in the English-speaking world, with teaching dating back to at least 1096, although no exact founding date is known. It is made up of more than 30 independent colleges, has educated dozens of British Prime Ministers and many world leaders, and is associated with numerous Nobel Prize winners. Its Bodleian Library is one of the oldest libraries in Europe, and several of its buildings appeared in the Harry Potter films.

Oxford's spires, ancient colleges and centuries of tradition make it one of the most famous universities on earth. Yet beyond its reputation for academic excellence, Oxford has a long list of surprising facts and quirky traditions.

Here are some of the most interesting lesser-known facts about the University of Oxford.

1. Nobody Knows Exactly When It Was Founded

Oxford has no official founding date. There is evidence of teaching in Oxford as early as 1096, and the university grew rapidly after 1167, when King Henry II banned English students from attending the University of Paris. That makes Oxford the oldest university in the English-speaking world and one of the oldest continuously operating universities anywhere.

2. Oxford Is Older Than the Aztec Empire

Teaching was underway at Oxford by the late 11th century, while the city of Tenochtitlán, the heart of the Aztec civilisation, was founded around 1325. Oxford was already more than two centuries old by then.

3. Cambridge Was Founded by Oxford Scholars

In 1209, after a dispute between scholars and townspeople in Oxford, a group of academics left and settled in Cambridge, where they helped establish what became the University of Cambridge. The friendly rivalry between the two universities, sometimes called "Oxbridge", continues today, including the famous annual Boat Race on the River Thames.

4. It's a Collegiate University

Oxford is made up of more than 30 colleges and several permanent private halls. Each college is self-governing, with its own buildings, dining hall, library and community. Students belong to both the university and a college, and much undergraduate teaching takes place in small tutorials with one to three students.

5. The Oldest Colleges Date Back to the 13th Century

University College, Balliol and Merton all claim to be the oldest college, each founded in the 1200s. Each bases its claim on a different milestone, such as the date of the first endowment or the date its statutes were written.

6. The Bodleian Library Receives a Copy of Every UK Book

The Bodleian Library opened to scholars in 1602 and is one of the oldest libraries in Europe. As a legal deposit library, it is entitled to a copy of every book published in the UK and Ireland. Together, the Bodleian Libraries hold more than 13 million printed items. Historically, readers had to swear an oath not to bring fire into the library or damage its books, and new readers still take a version of this declaration.

7. It Has Produced Around 30 British Prime Ministers

Oxford has educated more British Prime Ministers than any other university, including William Gladstone, Clement Attlee, Margaret Thatcher, Tony Blair, David Cameron, Theresa May, Boris Johnson, Liz Truss, Rishi Sunak and Keir Starmer, who completed a postgraduate law degree there. Many other world leaders also studied there, including Indira Gandhi, Benazir Bhutto, Bill Clinton (as a Rhodes Scholar) and several Australian prime ministers.

8. Dozens of Nobel Prize Winners Are Linked to Oxford

Oxford's students, alumni and staff include around 70 Nobel Prize winners across chemistry, medicine, physics, economics, literature and peace. Dorothy Hodgkin, who won the 1964 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for determining the structures of penicillin and vitamin B12, worked at Oxford.

9. Women Couldn't Receive Degrees Until 1920

Women's halls opened at Oxford in the late 1870s, and women could attend lectures and sit exams, but they weren't allowed to become full members of the university and receive degrees until 1920. The last all-male colleges admitted women in the 1970s and 1980s, and St Hilda's, the last women-only college, admitted men in 2008.

10. Harry Potter Was Filmed There

Several Oxford locations appeared in the Harry Potter films. Christ Church's grand staircase was used in scenes at Hogwarts, and its dining hall inspired the design of Hogwarts' Great Hall. The Bodleian's Divinity School served as the Hogwarts infirmary, and Duke Humfrey's Library appeared as the Hogwarts library.

11. "The City of Dreaming Spires"

Poet Matthew Arnold described Oxford as the "sweet city with her dreaming spires" in his 1865 poem Thyrsis. The phrase "City of Dreaming Spires" has been used to describe Oxford ever since.

12. Famous Writers Studied and Taught There

J.R.R. Tolkien and C.S. Lewis both taught at Oxford and were members of a literary group called the Inklings, which met at pubs including the Eagle and Child. Other famous Oxford writers include Oscar Wilde, Lewis Carroll, whose Alice's Adventures in Wonderland was inspired by the daughter of a Christ Church dean, and Dorothy L. Sayers.

13. Unusual Traditions Survive

  • Subfusc: students wear formal academic dress, including gowns, for exams and ceremonies.
  • Carnations: students traditionally wear a white carnation to their first exam, pink for the middle ones and red for the last.
  • May Morning: on 1 May, the Magdalen College choir sings from the top of Magdalen Tower at 6 am to crowds below.
  • Hunting the Mallard: All Souls College holds a mallard ceremony once every century, most recently in 2001.

14. It Helped Develop a COVID-19 Vaccine

Oxford researchers at the Jenner Institute developed the Oxford–AstraZeneca COVID-19 vaccine, which was distributed widely around the world, including in large volumes manufactured in India as Covishield.

15. Getting In Is Highly Competitive

Oxford receives many applications for each undergraduate place. Admissions often include subject-specific tests and interviews designed to assess how candidates think rather than just what they know.

Frequently Asked Questions

How old is the University of Oxford?

Teaching has existed at Oxford since at least 1096, making it more than 900 years old.

Is Oxford older than Cambridge?

Yes. Cambridge was founded in 1209 by scholars who had left Oxford.

How many colleges does Oxford have?

Oxford has more than 30 colleges plus several permanent private halls.

Can international students study at Oxford?

Yes. Oxford has a large international student community, and scholarships such as the Rhodes, Clarendon and Reach Oxford programmes support students from around the world.

Final Thoughts

The University of Oxford is much more than a famous name. It's a living institution that has shaped science, literature, politics and medicine for over nine centuries, while preserving traditions that make it unlike anywhere else. Whether you dream of studying there or simply enjoy history, Oxford's story is full of surprises.
