Updated 21 October 2026

Quick answer: Currency values differ between countries because each currency is priced by supply and demand in the foreign exchange market, and that demand depends on things that vary from country to country: interest rates, inflation, economic growth, trade balances, government debt, political stability, central bank policy and how much the world trusts and uses the currency. Some currencies also look "expensive" or "cheap" simply because of the size of the unit; one US dollar buying around 150 Japanese yen doesn't mean Japan's economy is weak. And some countries don't let the market decide at all, fixing (pegging) their currency to another one, usually the US dollar.

Anyone who has travelled abroad, sent money to family overseas, paid fees for a child studying in another country or bought something online from a foreign seller has run into exchange rates. Why does one Kuwaiti dinar buy more than three US dollars, while one US dollar buys more than ninety Indian rupees? Why does a currency's value rise in one year and fall the next? And does a "strong" currency mean a strong economy?

This article explains what an exchange rate is, the main forces that push currency values up or down, how different countries manage their currencies, some common myths, key moments in currency history, and what all of this means for your own money.

What Is an Exchange Rate?

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. If the rate between the US dollar and the Indian rupee is 85, it means one dollar costs 85 rupees, or equivalently one rupee costs about 1.2 US cents.

Exchange rates are always quoted in pairs. The first currency is the "base" and the second is the "quote" currency. In the pair USD/INR, the dollar is the base. When the number goes up, the dollar has strengthened against the rupee (or the rupee has weakened against the dollar); when it goes down, the reverse has happened.

Currencies are traded in the foreign exchange (forex or FX) market, the largest financial market in the world. According to the Bank for International Settlements' triennial survey, average daily turnover was about $7.5 trillion in April 2022. Unlike a stock exchange, there is no single central marketplace: trading happens electronically between banks, companies, investment funds, central banks and individuals around the world, 24 hours a day on weekdays.

Several kinds of exchange rate are useful to know:

  • Spot rate: the rate for immediate exchange (in practice, settlement usually takes up to two business days).
  • Forward rate: a rate agreed today for an exchange at a future date, used by businesses to lock in costs.
  • Buying and selling rates: banks and money changers quote two prices. The difference, the "spread", is part of their profit, which is why you always get a little less than the headline rate when you change cash.
  • Nominal and real exchange rates: the nominal rate is the number you see quoted. The real exchange rate adjusts for differences in inflation, so it tells you what the currency can actually buy.

Why a "Low" Number Doesn't Mean a Weak Currency

A common misunderstanding is that a currency with a large number per dollar must belong to a weak or poor country. That's not true. The number partly reflects an accident of history: how big each unit of currency happened to be when it was created.

  • The Japanese yen has traded at somewhere between about 100 and 160 per US dollar for much of the past two decades, yet Japan is one of the world's largest and richest economies.
  • The South Korean won trades at well over 1,000 per dollar, and South Korea is a wealthy, high-tech economy.
  • The Kuwaiti dinar is usually the highest-valued currency unit in the world, worth over three US dollars, but that reflects how the dinar was defined and how Kuwait manages it, not that Kuwait's economy is bigger than America's.

Countries can even change the number overnight. In 2005, Turkey introduced the "new Turkish lira", removing six zeros, so that 1,000,000 old lira became 1 new lira. Nothing about the Turkish economy changed that day; only the unit did. This process is called redenomination.

What matters is not the level of the number but how it changes over time and what the currency can buy. A currency that falls from 70 to 85 per dollar has lost value; one that has sat at 3.75 per dollar for decades has been very stable.

The Basic Force: Supply and Demand

For currencies that float freely, the exchange rate is set by supply and demand, just like the price of anything else in a market.

Demand for a currency rises when:

  • foreigners buy the country's exports, because they need its currency to pay;
  • foreign investors buy the country's shares, bonds, property or businesses;
  • tourists visit and spend money;
  • people working abroad send money home (remittances);
  • central banks and investors want to hold it as a store of value.

Supply of a currency rises when:

  • the country's residents buy imports, which means selling their own currency to buy foreign currency;
  • local investors invest abroad;
  • foreign investors sell their holdings in the country and take their money home;
  • residents travel abroad or send money overseas.

When demand for a currency exceeds supply, its price rises (it appreciates). When supply exceeds demand, it falls (it depreciates). Every factor described below works by shifting this balance.

Factor 1: Interest Rates

Interest rates are one of the most powerful short-term drivers of exchange rates. Investors move money around the world looking for the best return for the risk they take. If a country's central bank raises interest rates, bonds and deposits in that currency pay more, so investors buy the currency to earn the higher return.

A clear example came in 2022. As the US Federal Reserve raised its policy rate rapidly to fight inflation, the dollar rose strongly against most currencies. The Japanese yen, whose central bank kept rates near zero, fell to its weakest level against the dollar in about three decades, at one point passing 150 yen per dollar.

Investors sometimes exploit interest rate differences directly through the "carry trade": borrowing in a low-interest currency and investing in a higher-interest one. This can push high-interest currencies up for long periods, but carry trades can unwind suddenly when markets become nervous, causing sharp moves.

Higher interest rates don't always mean a stronger currency, though. If rates are high because inflation is out of control or because investors demand extra compensation for risk, the currency may still fall. Investors care about real returns (after inflation) and about the safety of their money.

Factor 2: Inflation and Purchasing Power

Over the long run, inflation is one of the most important influences on currency values. If prices in one country rise faster than in another, its currency buys less, and over time its exchange rate tends to fall to reflect that.

This idea is formalised in the theory of purchasing power parity (PPP): in the long run, exchange rates should move so that the same basket of goods costs about the same in each country, once converted into a common currency. Suppose inflation in India is 5% a year and in the US 2%. PPP suggests the rupee would tend to lose about 3% a year against the dollar over the long term.

PPP is a rough guide rather than a precise law. Many goods and services, such as haircuts, rent and restaurant meals, can't be traded across borders, and labour is much cheaper in lower-income countries. That's why a rupee goes much further in India than its market exchange rate suggests, and why international organisations like the World Bank and IMF often compare countries' economies using PPP-adjusted figures as well as market exchange rates.

A well-known light-hearted test of PPP is The Economist's Big Mac index, published since 1986. It compares the price of a McDonald's Big Mac in different countries. If a Big Mac costs much less in a country (converted at market rates) than in the US, the index suggests that currency is "undervalued". It isn't meant to be precise, but it's a memorable way to show that exchange rates and local purchasing power often differ.

At the extreme, very high inflation destroys a currency. In hyperinflations, such as Germany in 1923, Zimbabwe in 2007 to 2008 and Venezuela in the late 2010s, prices rose so fast that the currency lost almost all its value, and people switched to foreign currencies, barter or goods.

Factor 3: Economic Growth and Productivity

A strong, growing economy tends to attract investment. Foreign companies set up factories and offices, international investors buy shares in growing companies, and all of this requires buying the local currency.

Productivity matters too. Countries whose workers and firms become more productive can produce more competitive goods, export more and support higher wages, which tends to support the currency over time. Economists call one version of this the Balassa-Samuelson effect: as poorer countries become more productive and richer, their price levels and real exchange rates tend to rise.

However, the relationship between growth and currency strength isn't automatic. A fast-growing economy that imports more than it exports, or that relies on volatile foreign investment, can still see its currency weaken.

Factor 4: Trade Balance and the Current Account

The current account measures a country's trade in goods and services, plus income from investments abroad and transfers such as remittances. It's a useful summary of how much foreign currency a country earns versus how much it spends.

  • Current account surplus: the country earns more foreign currency than it spends. This creates steady demand for its currency. Germany, Japan, Switzerland and several oil exporters have long run surpluses.
  • Current account deficit: the country spends more foreign currency than it earns, and must fill the gap by attracting foreign investment or borrowing. Large, persistent deficits can put downward pressure on the currency, especially if foreign investors lose confidence.

India usually runs a current account deficit, largely because it imports most of the crude oil it uses, as well as gold and electronics. Large remittances from Indians working abroad and strong exports of IT and business services help narrow that gap. When oil prices spike, India's import bill rises and the rupee tends to come under pressure.

The terms of trade, the price of a country's exports relative to its imports, also matter. When the price of what a country sells rises, it earns more foreign currency and its currency tends to strengthen.

Factor 5: Commodity Prices

For countries that export large amounts of oil, metals or farm products, commodity prices are a major driver of the currency. The Australian dollar, Canadian dollar and Norwegian krone are often called "commodity currencies" because they tend to rise when prices of commodities such as iron ore, oil and gas go up, and fall when those prices drop.

For commodity importers, the effect works the other way. A sharp rise in oil prices increases what countries like India, Japan and many European nations must pay for energy, which can weaken their currencies.

Factor 6: Government Debt and Fiscal Policy

Large government deficits and high public debt can make investors worried. They may fear that the government will struggle to repay, raise taxes sharply, or rely on the central bank to finance spending by creating money, which could lead to inflation. Any of these can weaken a currency.

A sudden loss of confidence can be dramatic. In September 2022, the UK government announced large unfunded tax cuts in a "mini-budget". Investors reacted badly: UK government bond yields jumped and the pound fell to a record low of around $1.03 against the dollar. The Bank of England stepped in to stabilise the bond market, and most of the tax plans were reversed within weeks.

Debt matters less when investors trust the government and the debt is in its own currency. Japan has very high public debt relative to its economy, but most of it is held by domestic investors and the Bank of Japan, so it hasn't triggered a currency crisis.

Factor 7: Political Stability and Institutions

Investors want to know that their money is safe. Countries with stable governments, predictable policies, independent central banks, strong legal systems and protection of property rights tend to attract more long-term investment and have steadier currencies.

Political crises, wars, sanctions, coups, contested elections and sudden policy U-turns can trigger capital flight, as investors sell the local currency and move money to safer places. Even within stable democracies, elections and referendums can move currencies sharply. The pound fell by more than 10% against the dollar in the hours after the UK's Brexit referendum in June 2016.

Factor 8: Central Bank Intervention and Foreign Exchange Reserves

Central banks don't just set interest rates; many also buy and sell currencies directly to influence exchange rates. To support a weakening currency, a central bank can sell some of its foreign exchange reserves (for example, US dollars) and buy its own currency. To stop a currency rising too fast, it can do the opposite.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is a good example. It holds large foreign exchange reserves, which reached over $700 billion in 2024, and it intervenes in the market to smooth sharp swings in the rupee, rather than defending a specific level. Large reserves act like a buffer, reassuring investors that the country can pay for imports and service foreign debt even in difficult times.

Intervention has limits. A central bank can't defend an exchange rate forever against strong market pressure if its reserves run low. That's what happened to the UK in September 1992, on "Black Wednesday", when the pound was forced out of the European Exchange Rate Mechanism despite heavy intervention and interest rate rises.

Factor 9: Speculation and Market Sentiment

Most daily forex trading isn't for trade or long-term investment; it's by banks, funds and traders betting on short-term movements. Their expectations can become self-fulfilling: if enough traders believe a currency will fall and sell it, it does fall.

Sentiment also drives "risk-on" and "risk-off" behaviour. When global investors feel confident, they buy riskier assets, including emerging-market currencies. When they're frightened, during a financial crisis or a war, for example, they rush into "safe-haven" currencies. The US dollar, Swiss franc and, traditionally, the Japanese yen tend to strengthen in times of global stress, even when the crisis starts in the US itself, as in 2008.

Factor 10: Reserve Currency Status

A few currencies are held in large amounts by central banks and used widely in international trade and finance. The US dollar is by far the most important: it makes up more than half of the world's official foreign exchange reserves, and most commodities, including oil, are priced in dollars. The euro is a distant second, followed by the Japanese yen, the British pound and others.

This status creates constant global demand for the dollar, which helps the US borrow cheaply and supports the currency's value. Economists sometimes call this America's "exorbitant privilege", a phrase associated with French finance minister Valéry Giscard d'Estaing in the 1960s. How Countries Manage Their Currencies

Not every exchange rate is set purely by the market. Countries choose different exchange rate regimes, and this is one of the biggest reasons currency behaviour varies from country to country.

Free-Floating Currencies

The value is determined by the market, with little or no regular intervention. The US dollar, euro, Japanese yen, British pound, Australian dollar and Canadian dollar float freely. Their values can move a lot, but they adjust automatically to economic shocks.

Managed Floats

The currency mostly floats, but the central bank intervenes to reduce volatility or lean against large moves. India's rupee is often described this way, as are many emerging-market currencies.

Pegged (Fixed) Currencies

The government fixes the currency's value to another currency, commonly the US dollar or the euro, and the central bank stands ready to buy or sell to keep it there. Examples include:

  • Saudi riyal: fixed at 3.75 riyals per US dollar since 1986.
  • UAE dirham: fixed at about 3.6725 dirhams per dollar since the late 1990s.
  • Hong Kong dollar: kept within a narrow band of 7.75 to 7.85 per US dollar under a "linked exchange rate system" dating back to 1983.
  • Danish krone: held close to a central rate against the euro.

Pegs provide stability for trade and investment and can help control inflation, but they require large reserves and mean the country largely gives up independent interest rate policy, because rates must track those of the anchor currency.

Currency Baskets and Crawling Pegs

Some countries manage their currency against a basket of several currencies, or allow it to move gradually along a pre-announced path. Singapore, for example, manages its dollar against an undisclosed basket of trading partners' currencies within a policy band, using the exchange rate rather than interest rates as its main monetary policy tool.

Currency Unions and Dollarisation

Some countries share a currency. Twenty European Union countries used the euro as of 2025. Others, such as Ecuador, El Salvador and Panama, use the US dollar as their official currency. These countries give up control of their own monetary policy in exchange for stability and lower transaction costs.

A Brief History of Exchange Rates

How currencies are valued has changed several times in the last 150 years:

  • The gold standard (roughly the 1870s to 1914): major currencies were defined as fixed amounts of gold, so exchange rates between them were fixed. The system broke down during the First World War and never fully recovered, collapsing during the Great Depression of the 1930s.
  • Bretton Woods (1944 to the early 1970s): at a conference in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, in July 1944, Allied nations agreed a system in which currencies were pegged to the US dollar, which in turn was convertible into gold at $35 an ounce. The IMF and World Bank were created at the same time.
  • The end of gold convertibility (1971): on 15 August 1971, President Richard Nixon suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold. By 1973, most major currencies had moved to floating exchange rates.
  • The modern era: today, most large economies float, while many smaller and commodity-exporting economies use pegs or managed arrangements. Currency crises, such as the 1997 Asian financial crisis, showed the dangers of fixed rates combined with large foreign borrowing.

The Indian Rupee: A Case Study

You may have seen the claim that one rupee equalled one US dollar in 1947. It's a popular myth. At independence, the rupee was linked to the British pound at 13.33 rupees per pound, and the pound was worth about $4.03, which works out to roughly 3.3 rupees per dollar.

Several key moments since then shaped the rupee's path:

  • 1966: after wars, droughts and a balance of payments crisis, India devalued the rupee to 7.50 per dollar.
  • 1991: facing a severe balance of payments crisis, with reserves enough for only a few weeks of imports, India devalued the rupee in two steps and launched major economic reforms. The exchange rate moved towards a market-determined system over the following years.
  • 2013: during the "taper tantrum", when the US Federal Reserve signalled it would slow its bond purchases, foreign investors pulled money out of emerging markets and the rupee fell sharply, briefly crossing 68 per dollar.
  • 2020s: the rupee weakened gradually, moving into the 80s against the dollar and then past 90 by 2026 (about ₹95 in October 2026), as US interest rates rose and oil prices fluctuated, while the RBI used its large reserves to smooth volatility.

The long-run decline mostly reflects India's higher inflation compared with the US over the decades, just as purchasing power parity predicts. It doesn't mean the Indian economy has shrunk; India's economy has grown enormously over the same period.

Strong Currency vs Weak Currency: Which Is Better?

There is no simple answer, because a currency's value creates winners and losers.

A stronger currency:

  • makes imports, foreign travel and overseas education cheaper;
  • helps keep inflation down, especially for countries that import energy;
  • reduces the cost of repaying foreign-currency debt;
  • but makes exports more expensive for foreign buyers, which can hurt manufacturers, farmers and tourism.

A weaker currency:

  • makes exports more competitive and can boost tourism;
  • increases the value of remittances in local-currency terms;
  • but raises the cost of imports and can feed inflation;
  • and increases the burden of foreign-currency debt.

Some countries have been accused of keeping their currencies artificially weak to boost exports, which can cause trade tensions. Most central banks aim for a currency that reflects economic fundamentals and moves without sudden, destabilising swings.

How Currency Values Affect Your Everyday Life

  • Fuel and electronics: in import-dependent countries, a weaker currency raises the price of petrol, diesel, mobile phones and laptops.
  • Studying abroad: for an Indian family paying $40,000 a year in fees, a move from 80 to 88 rupees per dollar adds 3.2 lakh rupees to the annual bill.
  • Remittances: if you work in the Gulf, US or UK and send money home, a weaker rupee means your family receives more rupees for the same amount sent.
  • Travel: a strong home currency makes holidays abroad cheaper; a weak one makes them more expensive but brings more foreign tourists to your country.
  • Investments: if you hold international mutual funds or foreign shares, currency moves can add to or reduce your returns, separately from how the investments themselves perform.
  • Jobs: export-oriented industries, such as IT services, textiles and pharmaceuticals, benefit from a weaker currency, which can support employment.

Practical Tips for Dealing With Exchange Rates

  1. Compare the total cost, not just the rate. Banks, money changers and transfer services make money from both fees and the spread. A "zero fee" service may give a worse rate.
  2. Avoid changing money at airports and hotels, which often offer poor rates.
  3. Be careful with "dynamic currency conversion" when paying by card abroad. If a terminal offers to charge you in your home currency, it often uses an unfavourable rate; choosing to pay in the local currency is usually cheaper.
  4. Spread large conversions over time if you have a big foreign-currency expense coming up, such as tuition. This averages out the rate and reduces the risk of converting everything at a bad moment.
  5. Don't try to predict short-term currency moves. Professional traders with sophisticated models often get them wrong.
  6. Consider currency diversification as part of long-term investing, for example through international funds, if it fits your goals and risk tolerance.

This is general information, not personalised financial advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the US dollar stronger than the Indian rupee?

The number of rupees per dollar reflects history and decades of higher inflation in India than in the US, as well as differences in interest rates, trade balances and the dollar's role as the world's main reserve currency. The exchange rate alone doesn't tell you which economy is "better".

Which is the highest-valued currency in the world?

The Kuwaiti dinar is usually the highest-valued currency unit, worth more than three US dollars, followed by the Bahraini dinar and the Omani rial. Their high value reflects how the units were defined and how their governments manage them, supported by oil wealth.

Can a government simply make its currency stronger?

Not easily or permanently. It can raise interest rates, intervene using reserves or peg the currency, but if the underlying economics don't support the value, these measures can fail, as many currency crises have shown.

Why don't all countries use the same currency?

Having its own currency lets a country set interest rates to suit its own economy and lets the exchange rate adjust to shocks. Sharing a currency, as in the eurozone, reduces costs and uncertainty but removes those tools.

Does printing more money weaken a currency?

Creating money faster than the economy grows tends to cause inflation, which reduces the currency's purchasing power and usually its exchange rate over time. Extreme money printing has been at the root of most hyperinflations.

What is a currency peg?

A peg is when a country fixes its currency's value against another currency, such as the Saudi riyal at 3.75 per US dollar. The central bank buys and sells currency to hold the rate.

How often do exchange rates change?

Floating exchange rates change constantly, every second the market is open. Pegged currencies stay fixed or move only within a narrow band.

What is purchasing power parity?

It's the idea that, over the long term, exchange rates should adjust so that the same goods cost the same in different countries. It's a useful long-run guide, but actual exchange rates often differ from it for years.

Final Thoughts

Currency values differ because countries differ: in their inflation, interest rates, growth, trade, debt, politics and how they choose to manage their money. The headline number on a currency board tells you little on its own; what matters is how the value changes over time and what the currency can actually buy.

Understanding the forces behind exchange rates won't let you predict tomorrow's rate, but it will help you make sense of the news, plan big foreign-currency expenses more calmly and see why a "weak" rupee or a "strong" dollar can be good news for some people and bad news for others.

If this explainer helped, share it with someone who's ever wondered why their money is worth more in one country than another.
