Quick answer: To control your emotions, first notice and name what you're feeling, then pause before reacting, using slow breathing to calm your body. Reframe the situation by asking whether there's another way to see it, and choose a response that fits your values. Over time, reduce emotional overload by getting enough sleep, managing stress, exercising, expressing feelings in healthy ways and practising mindfulness. Controlling emotions doesn't mean suppressing them; it means managing how you respond.

We've all said something in anger we later regretted, or felt so anxious we couldn't think clearly. Emotions are powerful signals, but when they take over, they can damage relationships, decisions and wellbeing.

Psychologists call the ability to manage emotions emotional regulation. It's a skill, not a fixed trait, and it can be learned. This guide explains why emotions sometimes feel overwhelming and offers practical strategies to manage them in the moment and in the long term.

Why Emotions Can Feel Overwhelming

When you perceive a threat, such as criticism, conflict or danger, the brain's emotional centres, including the amygdala, can trigger a fast stress response before the more reflective prefrontal cortex has fully processed the situation. Psychologist Daniel Goleman called extreme versions of this an "amygdala hijack". Stress, tiredness, hunger and unresolved problems all make these reactions stronger.

Controlling vs Suppressing Emotions

Suppressing emotions, pretending you don't feel them or bottling them up, tends to backfire. Research by psychologist James Gross and others suggests suppression can increase physical stress and harm relationships. Healthy regulation means acknowledging emotions and choosing how to express and act on them.

In-the-Moment Strategies

1. Name the Emotion

Simply labelling a feeling, such as "I'm frustrated" or "I feel anxious", can reduce its intensity. Brain imaging studies by Matthew Lieberman and colleagues found that "affect labelling" reduces activity in the amygdala.

2. Pause Before Reacting

Create a gap between feeling and action. Count to ten, step away from the conversation or say, "Let me think about that and get back to you." Even a short pause allows your thinking brain to catch up.

3. Breathe Slowly

Slow breathing activates the body's relaxation response. Try breathing in for 4 seconds and out for 6 to 8 seconds, repeated for one to two minutes. Longer exhales are especially calming.

4. Ground Yourself

The 5-4-3-2-1 technique pulls attention into the present: notice 5 things you can see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell and 1 you can taste.

5. Reframe the Situation

Ask yourself:

  • Is there another explanation for what happened?
  • How will this matter in a week, a month or a year?
  • What would a calm, wise person do here?

Reappraisal, changing how you interpret a situation, is one of the most effective regulation strategies identified in research.

6. Choose Your Response

Once calmer, decide what action fits your values and goals. You might express your feelings assertively, solve a problem or simply let it go.

Long-Term Strategies

Get Enough Sleep

Sleep deprivation makes emotional reactions stronger and self-control weaker. Studies show that after poor sleep, the brain's emotional centres react more intensely to negative events. Aim for seven to nine hours.

Manage Stress

Chronic stress keeps your nervous system on high alert, making outbursts more likely. Identify major stressors, set boundaries, delegate and make time for rest.

Exercise Regularly

Physical activity reduces stress hormones and releases mood-lifting chemicals. Even a brisk 20-minute walk can improve mood.

Practise Mindfulness

Regular mindfulness meditation helps you observe thoughts and feelings without immediately acting on them. Programmes such as Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction have been shown to improve emotional regulation.

Express Emotions Healthily

  • Talk to a trusted friend.
  • Write in a journal.
  • Use creative outlets like art, music or dance.
  • Cry if you need to. It's a natural release.

Identify Your Triggers

Notice patterns. Do certain people, topics, times of day or situations consistently trigger strong reactions? Knowing your triggers helps you prepare or avoid unnecessary conflict.

Look After Your Body

Hunger, dehydration, caffeine, alcohol and illness all affect mood. Regular meals, water and limiting alcohol help keep emotions steadier.

Managing Specific Emotions

Anger

Take a break before responding, use "I" statements to express what upset you and focus on solving the problem rather than winning the argument. Physical activity can help release angry energy.

Anxiety

Slow breathing, grounding and challenging worst-case thinking help. Break overwhelming tasks into small steps and focus on what you can control.

Sadness

Allow yourself to feel sad, reach out to supportive people and do small activities that usually bring comfort or meaning.

Jealousy

Ask what the jealousy tells you about your own desires, and focus on your progress rather than comparisons.

Emotional Control at Work

  • Wait before replying to emails that upset you.
  • Ask for time to consider difficult feedback.
  • Take short breaks during tense days.
  • Discuss conflicts privately and calmly.

When to Seek Help

If emotions frequently feel uncontrollable, lead to aggressive behaviour, self-harm or substance misuse, or significantly affect your relationships or work, talk to a doctor or mental health professional. Therapies such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) teach specific emotional regulation skills. If you ever feel at risk of harming yourself, contact emergency services or a crisis helpline immediately.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it healthy to control emotions?

Managing how you express emotions is healthy. Completely suppressing or ignoring them is not.

Why do I get emotional so easily?

Some people are naturally more sensitive. Stress, lack of sleep, hormonal changes and past experiences can also increase emotional reactivity.

How can I stop crying when I'm angry?

Crying when angry is common. Slow breathing, a short pause and naming the emotion can help. It's also okay to say, "I need a minute."

How long does it take to improve emotional control?

With regular practice, many people notice improvements within weeks. Deeper change develops over months.

Final Thoughts

Emotions are not the enemy; they're messages about what matters to you. The goal is not to stop feeling but to stop being controlled by feelings. Notice, name, pause, breathe and choose. Support yourself with sleep, exercise and stress management, and you'll find yourself responding to life's challenges with greater calm and confidence.
