Quick answer: Many professionals prefer working from home because it saves commuting time and money, gives them more control over their schedule, allows deeper focus away from office interruptions, and makes it easier to balance work with family, health and personal life. Research suggests remote and hybrid work can maintain or improve productivity for many roles and reduce staff turnover. But working from home also brings challenges: isolation, blurred boundaries, weaker mentoring and collaboration, distractions at home and fewer chances to be noticed. That's why hybrid work, combining some home and some office days, has become the most common arrangement for many office-based jobs.

Before 2020, working from home was a perk offered by a minority of employers. Then the COVID-19 pandemic forced millions of office workers to work remotely almost overnight. Many discovered they liked it, and when offices reopened, a large share of professionals wanted to keep at least some remote work. Debates about "return to office" mandates have continued ever since.

This article explores why so many professionals value working from home, what research says about productivity and wellbeing, the downsides, how hybrid work fits in, and practical tips for making remote work succeed for both employees and employers.

How Work From Home Became Mainstream

Remote work existed long before the pandemic, especially among freelancers, sales staff and some technology workers. But for most office employees, the office was the default. When lockdowns began in 2020, organisations had to adapt quickly, using video calls, cloud tools and collaboration software.

After the pandemic, many companies adopted hybrid models. Research by economists including Nicholas Bloom, Jose Maria Barrero and Steven Davis, who run the long-running Survey of Working Arrangements and Attitudes in the United States, found that work-from-home days settled at roughly a quarter of paid workdays, far above pre-pandemic levels. Similar shifts happened in many countries, including in India's IT and services sector, though the extent varies by company and industry, and some large employers have since pushed for more office days.

Why Professionals Love Working From Home

1. No Commute

For many people, the single biggest benefit is not commuting. In large cities, commutes can take one to three hours a day in traffic or crowded trains. An international survey by Barrero, Bloom and colleagues estimated that working from home saved employees roughly an hour a day on average in commuting time. That time can go to sleep, exercise, family, hobbies or work.

2. Saving Money

Remote workers save on fuel, public transport, parking, office clothes and lunches. For some, it also makes it possible to live in a cheaper area or hometown rather than an expensive city.

3. Flexibility and Control

Working from home often allows more control over when and how work gets done. Professionals can start early or late, take a break for school pick-ups or medical appointments, and arrange work around their most productive hours.

4. Fewer Interruptions and Deeper Focus

Open-plan offices can be noisy and full of interruptions. At home, many people find it easier to concentrate on complex tasks such as writing, coding, analysis or design.

5. Better Work-Life Balance

Parents can spend more time with children, carers can support family members, and people can fit exercise, cooking and rest into their day more easily.

6. Comfort and Personalisation

People can control their workspace: lighting, temperature, noise, chair, music and food. Comfort can improve mood and focus.

7. Health Benefits

Less commuting stress, more time for exercise and home-cooked meals, and the ability to rest when unwell without infecting colleagues can all support health.

8. Inclusion

Remote work can open opportunities for people with disabilities, chronic illnesses or caring responsibilities, and for those living in smaller towns or rural areas who might otherwise have to relocate.

9. Autonomy and Trust

Being trusted to manage one's own work is motivating. Many professionals feel more respected when judged on results rather than hours at a desk.

What Research Says About Productivity

The evidence on productivity is mixed, and it depends on the job, the worker and how remote work is organised.

  • The Ctrip experiment: in a well-known randomised study published in 2015, Nicholas Bloom and colleagues found that call-centre employees at the Chinese travel company Ctrip who worked from home were about 13% more productive, largely because they took fewer breaks and sick days and worked in quieter environments. Staff turnover also fell sharply. However, many of the home workers later chose to return to the office, citing loneliness.
  • Hybrid work at Trip.com: a later randomised trial at the same company, published in Nature in 2024, found that working from home two days a week had no detectable negative effect on performance reviews or promotions over two years, while reducing quit rates by about one third.
  • Collaboration effects: a study of Microsoft employees published in Nature Human Behaviour in 2021 found that firm-wide remote work made collaboration networks more static and siloed, with fewer connections across teams.
  • Fully remote work: some studies have found that fully remote work can reduce productivity in certain roles, especially where learning, mentoring and teamwork matter most.

The overall picture is that hybrid work tends to preserve productivity while improving retention and satisfaction, while fully remote work has stronger benefits for some roles and stronger drawbacks for others.

The Challenges of Working From Home

1. Isolation and Loneliness

Missing casual conversations, lunches and team moments can leave remote workers feeling disconnected, which can affect mental health and engagement.

2. Blurred Boundaries

When home becomes the office, it can be hard to switch off. Many remote workers report working longer hours and checking messages late into the evening.

3. Distractions at Home

Children, household chores, noise, deliveries and family members can interrupt work, especially in small homes.

4. Communication Gaps

Without face-to-face contact, misunderstandings can occur, and information may not reach everyone. Video call fatigue is also common.

5. Weaker Mentoring and Learning

Junior employees often learn by observing experienced colleagues and asking quick questions. Remote work can make this informal learning harder.

6. Career Visibility

Some remote workers worry that being less visible to managers could affect promotions, a concern sometimes described as "proximity bias".

7. Home Setup and Costs

Not everyone has a quiet room, good chair or reliable internet. Electricity and internet costs may rise at home.

8. Physical Health Risks

Poor ergonomics can cause back, neck and wrist problems, and less incidental movement, such as walking to meetings or the station, can reduce daily activity.

Working From Home in India

India's experience of remote work has its own features.

  • IT and services sector: India's large technology and business services workforce moved to remote work at scale during the pandemic. Afterwards, major employers adopted a range of policies, from flexible hybrid arrangements to requirements for several office days a week.
  • Return to hometowns: many professionals moved back from metros such as Bengaluru, Mumbai, Pune, Hyderabad and the National Capital Region to smaller cities and hometowns, enjoying lower living costs and time with family. This also drew attention to tier-2 and tier-3 cities as potential talent hubs.
  • Space constraints: in many urban homes, especially with joint families or children, finding a quiet, dedicated workspace is difficult.
  • Infrastructure: reliable broadband and electricity are essential. Power cuts and patchy connectivity in some areas lead people to invest in inverters, backup mobile data or co-working spaces.
  • Commuting relief: in cities known for heavy traffic, the time saved by not commuting is especially valuable.
  • Gender and caregiving: remote and hybrid work can help women stay in the workforce while managing caregiving, though it can also increase the burden if household work isn't shared fairly.

Is Working From Home Better for the Environment?

Fewer commutes can mean lower transport emissions, less traffic and less air pollution in cities. However, the overall environmental effect is more complex. Home energy use for heating, cooling, lighting and computers rises, and some remote workers make more non-work car trips or move further away from cities. Studies suggest the net impact depends heavily on how people commute normally, how homes are powered and how offices are used. Remote work is likely to be most beneficial when it replaces long car commutes and when companies also reduce office space and energy use.

Why Hybrid Work Has Become Popular

Hybrid work aims to combine the best of both worlds: focused work and flexibility at home, with collaboration, mentoring and social connection in the office. Many organisations use two or three office days a week, sometimes coordinating so teams are in on the same days. When done well, hybrid work can improve satisfaction and retention without harming performance.

Tips for Working From Home Successfully

Set Up a Proper Workspace

  • Use a dedicated area, ideally a separate room or corner.
  • Get a supportive chair and position your screen at eye level.
  • Use an external keyboard and mouse with a laptop.
  • Ensure good lighting, preferably natural light.
  • Invest in reliable internet and a good headset.

Create Structure

  • Keep regular start and finish times.
  • Get dressed for work to signal the start of the day.
  • Plan daily priorities.
  • Block time for deep work and turn off notifications.
  • End the day with a ritual, such as a walk, to mark the transition.

Communicate Clearly

  • Over-communicate progress and availability.
  • Use video for complex or sensitive conversations.
  • Write clear, concise messages with context.
  • Schedule regular check-ins with your manager.

Stay Connected

  • Have virtual coffee chats with colleagues.
  • Join team social activities.
  • Use office days for collaboration and relationship building.

Look After Your Health

  • Take regular breaks and stand up every hour.
  • Go outside daily for fresh air and daylight.
  • Exercise regularly.
  • Protect evenings and weekends from work.

Remote Work and Mental Health

Working from home can support mental health by reducing commuting stress, offering more control and making room for exercise and rest. For people who find offices overstimulating, or who experience social anxiety, it can be a relief. But it can also create risks.

  • Loneliness: people who live alone or have few social contacts outside work may feel isolated without daily office interaction.
  • Always-on pressure: laptops and phones at home make it tempting to keep working into the evening, increasing the risk of burnout.
  • Less movement and daylight: without a commute or walks between meetings, some people barely leave the house, which can affect mood and sleep.
  • Hidden struggles: managers and colleagues may not notice when someone working remotely is struggling.

Protective habits include scheduling social contact, going outside every day, keeping clear start and stop times, using video occasionally to see colleagues' faces, and speaking up early if you feel overwhelmed. Employers can help by checking in on wellbeing, not just tasks, and by promoting access to employee assistance programmes.

Working From Home as a Parent

  • Agree on a family schedule that includes your working hours and quiet times.
  • Use visual signals, such as a closed door or a sign, to show when you're on calls.
  • Plan demanding work for school hours or nap times.
  • Share childcare and household tasks fairly with your partner.
  • Communicate honestly with your manager about constraints and flexibility.
  • Accept that occasional interruptions are normal, and avoid guilt over them.

Skills That Help You Succeed in Remote Work

  • Self-management: organising your own time and priorities without close supervision.
  • Written communication: clear, concise messages and documentation.
  • Digital fluency: comfort with collaboration tools, video calls and shared documents.
  • Proactiveness: asking questions, sharing updates and raising problems early.
  • Relationship building: making deliberate efforts to connect with colleagues.
  • Boundary setting: protecting focus time and personal time.

How to Ask Your Employer for Remote or Hybrid Work

  1. Check company policy and how others in similar roles work.
  2. Prepare a proposal describing which days you'd work from home, how you'll stay available and how you'll measure results.
  3. Focus on business benefits: fewer interruptions for focused tasks, saved commuting time devoted to work, or improved retention.
  4. Offer a trial period, such as three months, with a review.
  5. Show your track record of reliability and results.
  6. Be flexible about attending important meetings or events in person.

An Illustrative Example

Imagine a software engineer who used to commute two hours a day across a busy city. When their team moved to a hybrid schedule of two office days a week, they reclaimed about six hours a week. They used some of it for exercise and family dinners and found they could concentrate better on complex coding tasks at home.

At first, though, they struggled. They worked late most nights, felt disconnected from newer colleagues and noticed back pain from working at the dining table. After a few months, they set fixed working hours, bought a proper chair and monitor, and used office days for design discussions, mentoring and lunches with the team. They also started a short walk at the end of each home workday to mark the transition.

With these adjustments, they felt both more productive and more balanced. This example is illustrative, but it reflects a common pattern: remote work's benefits are real, but they often depend on deliberately managing boundaries, workspace and relationships.

Roles That Suit Remote Work Best and Least

Remote work suits roles centred on computer-based, independent tasks, such as software development, writing, design, data analysis, digital marketing, accounting and many customer support functions. It's harder or impossible for roles requiring physical presence, such as healthcare, manufacturing, retail, hospitality, construction, laboratory work and many public services. Even within remote-friendly jobs, some tasks, like onboarding, brainstorming and relationship-building, often benefit from face-to-face time.

The Future of Work From Home

Remote and hybrid work appear to be lasting features of the labour market rather than temporary pandemic measures, though the balance continues to shift as companies experiment. Trends to watch include more purposeful office design focused on collaboration rather than rows of desks, growth of co-working spaces in smaller cities, better collaboration technology including AI tools, more global hiring, and continued debate about how to mentor and develop junior staff in distributed teams. The most successful organisations are likely to be those that design work around tasks and people rather than habit.

Tips for Employers and Managers

  • Focus on outcomes rather than hours online.
  • Set clear expectations for availability, response times and deliverables.
  • Provide equipment or allowances for home offices.
  • Make office days purposeful, with collaboration, training and team events.
  • Invest in mentoring and onboarding for new and junior employees.
  • Watch for signs of burnout and isolation.
  • Ensure remote employees have fair access to opportunities and promotions.
  • Document decisions and share information in accessible channels.

A Quick Remote Work Checklist

  • Do I have a comfortable, dedicated workspace with good lighting and posture?
  • Is my internet connection reliable, with a backup option?
  • Do I start and stop work at consistent times?
  • Am I taking breaks and moving during the day?
  • Have I spoken to a colleague about something other than work this week?
  • Does my manager know what I'm working on and what I've achieved?
  • Am I using office days, if I have them, for collaboration and connection?

Frequently Asked Questions

Are people who work from home happier?

Surveys generally find that people who can work from home at least part of the week report higher job satisfaction, mainly because of no commute and more flexibility. Fully remote workers are more likely to feel lonely, which is one reason hybrid work is popular.

Why don't some employers like working from home?

Common reasons are worries about collaboration and training junior staff, harder supervision, weaker company culture, and money already spent on offices. Many employers have settled on hybrid arrangements as a compromise.

Why do employees prefer working from home?

The main reasons are no commute, flexibility, cost savings, fewer interruptions and better work-life balance.

Is working from home more productive?

It depends on the job, person and setup. Studies show productivity can rise or stay similar for many roles, especially with hybrid arrangements, but collaboration and mentoring can suffer in fully remote settings.

What are the disadvantages of working from home?

Isolation, blurred boundaries, distractions, communication challenges, reduced mentoring and concerns about career visibility.

What is hybrid work?

Hybrid work combines working from home and from the office, typically with some fixed office days each week.

How can I avoid burnout while working from home?

Set clear working hours, take breaks, separate workspace from living space, stay connected with colleagues and protect time for rest and exercise.

Does working from home affect promotions?

It can, if managers favour people they see in person. Recent research on hybrid work found no negative effect on promotions in one large company, but remote workers can protect themselves by communicating achievements, maintaining relationships and using office days strategically.

Do companies save money with remote work?

Often, yes. Companies can reduce office space, utilities and facilities costs, and lower staff turnover, which is expensive. Some of these savings may be offset by home-office allowances, technology and travel for in-person meetings.

Is a co-working space a good alternative to working from home?

For people who lack space at home, feel isolated or struggle with distractions, a co-working space can provide structure, reliable facilities and social contact without a long commute. Many cities, including smaller ones, now offer flexible day passes.

How many office days a week is ideal?

There's no universal answer. Many organisations settle on two or three office days, coordinated so teams overlap. The right number depends on how much the work relies on collaboration, mentoring and in-person interaction.

Can I work from home permanently?

Some roles and employers offer fully remote positions permanently, particularly in technology, writing and digital services. Check contracts carefully, as policies can change.

Final Thoughts

Working from home has changed how many professionals think about work. Its benefits, from saved commuting time to flexibility and focus, are real and highly valued. But so are its challenges, particularly isolation, blurred boundaries and weaker collaboration. For many organisations and workers, the answer isn't choosing between home and office but designing thoughtful hybrid arrangements that give people flexibility while preserving the human connections that make teams strong.
