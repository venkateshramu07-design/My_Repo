Quick answer: A wormhole is a hypothetical tunnel through space-time that could connect two distant points in the universe, or even two different times, creating a shortcut. Wormholes are allowed by the equations of Albert Einstein's general theory of relativity, and were first described mathematically as "Einstein-Rosen bridges" in 1935. However, no wormhole has ever been observed. Most theoretical wormholes would collapse instantly unless held open by "exotic matter" with negative energy, which may not exist in the needed form. So wormholes remain a fascinating possibility in theory, not a proven reality.

Wormholes are a favourite of science fiction. Films such as Interstellar and series like Star Trek use them to send characters across galaxies in moments. But wormholes aren't purely invented; they emerge from real physics. The question is whether nature actually makes them, and whether anything could ever travel through one.

This guide explains what wormholes are, the science behind them, the main types physicists have imagined, the enormous challenges they face, their links to time travel and black holes, and what modern research says.

Space-Time: The Fabric Wormholes Bend

To understand wormholes, it helps to understand space-time. In 1915, Albert Einstein published his general theory of relativity. It describes gravity not as a force pulling objects together, but as the curving of space and time, combined into a four-dimensional "fabric", by mass and energy.

A common analogy is a trampoline. Place a heavy bowling ball in the middle and the surface curves downward. Roll a marble nearby and it curves toward the ball, not because of a mysterious pull, but because the surface is bent. Planets orbit the Sun because the Sun's mass curves space-time around it.

General relativity has been confirmed by many experiments: the bending of starlight observed during the 1919 solar eclipse, the precise orbit of Mercury, the slowing of clocks in stronger gravity (which GPS satellites must correct for), the detection of gravitational waves in 2015, and images of black hole shadows captured by the Event Horizon Telescope in 2019 and 2022.

Why We Want Shortcuts: The Scale of Space

Space is unimaginably large. Light, the fastest thing in the universe, travels about 300,000 kilometres per second, yet it still takes:

  • About 8 minutes 20 seconds to reach Earth from the Sun.
  • About 4.2 years to reach us from Proxima Centauri, the nearest star beyond the Sun.
  • About 26,000 years to reach us from the centre of the Milky Way.
  • About 2.5 million years to arrive from the Andromeda Galaxy.

Our fastest spacecraft travel at a tiny fraction of light speed. Voyager 1, launched in 1977 and now the most distant human-made object, would take tens of thousands of years to cover the distance to the nearest stars. Einstein's special relativity says nothing with mass can reach the speed of light. That's why wormholes are so appealing: they wouldn't break the cosmic speed limit, but they might let travellers take a shorter path.

What Is a Wormhole?

If space-time can be curved, could it be curved so much that two distant regions connect directly? That's the idea of a wormhole.

Imagine a sheet of paper representing the universe. Mark two points far apart on it. The normal route between them is a straight line across the paper. Now fold the paper so the points line up, and push a pencil through both. The pencil is a "wormhole": a shortcut through a higher dimension that connects the two points directly.

A wormhole would have two "mouths", one at each end, connected by a "throat". From outside, a mouth might look like a sphere through which you could see a distorted view of the far end of the tunnel.

A Brief History of Wormhole Science

  • 1916: Austrian physicist Ludwig Flamm, studying Karl Schwarzschild's solution to Einstein's equations, noticed it could describe a tunnel-like structure.
  • 1935: Einstein and Nathan Rosen described "bridges" connecting two regions of space-time, now called Einstein-Rosen bridges.
  • 1957: physicist John Wheeler coined the term "wormhole".
  • 1962: Wheeler and Robert Fuller showed that Einstein-Rosen bridges would pinch off too quickly for even light to pass through.
  • 1988: Kip Thorne and his student Michael Morris described traversable wormholes, which could in principle be held open long enough for travel, but only with exotic matter. Their work was inspired by Carl Sagan, who asked Thorne for a scientifically plausible way to send a character across space in his novel Contact.
  • 2013: Juan Maldacena and Leonard Susskind proposed the "ER = EPR" conjecture, suggesting a deep link between wormholes and quantum entanglement.
  • 2014: Kip Thorne served as scientific adviser on Interstellar, which depicted a wormhole and black hole based on real equations.
  • 2022: researchers used a quantum computer to simulate the dynamics of a simplified "holographic wormhole" model, attracting wide media attention and debate about what it really showed.

Types of Wormholes

Einstein-Rosen Bridges

These are the original theoretical wormholes, connected to black hole solutions. They are not traversable: they would collapse faster than anything, even light, could pass through. They're better thought of as mathematical features of black hole geometry than as usable tunnels.

Traversable Wormholes

Proposed by Morris and Thorne, these would remain open long enough for a person or spacecraft to pass through. The catch is that keeping the throat open requires matter with negative energy density, called exotic matter, which pushes space-time apart instead of pulling it together.

Intra-Universe and Inter-Universe Wormholes

An intra-universe wormhole would connect two points within our universe. An inter-universe wormhole, a more speculative idea, would connect our universe to another, if other universes exist.

Quantum Wormholes

Some physicists, including Wheeler, speculated that at the tiniest scales, around the Planck length (about 10-35 metres), space-time might be a frothy "quantum foam" in which tiny wormholes constantly appear and disappear. This remains speculative and untested.

Why Wormholes Are So Hard to Make

1. They Collapse

Gravity tends to pull a wormhole's throat closed. Without something pushing outward, a wormhole would pinch off almost instantly.

2. Exotic Matter

To hold a wormhole open, physicists believe you'd need matter or energy with negative energy density. Ordinary matter has positive energy. Quantum physics does allow small negative energy densities in certain situations, such as the Casimir effect, observed between two very close metal plates in a vacuum. But the amounts are tiny, and theoretical limits called "quantum inequalities" suggest it may be impossible to gather enough negative energy in one place to support a large, stable wormhole.

3. Instability

Even if a wormhole could be opened, the slightest disturbance, such as a spacecraft entering it, might cause it to collapse or become unstable. Some calculations suggest radiation entering a wormhole could be amplified dramatically.

4. Enormous Energy

Estimates of the energy needed to create and stabilise a human-scale wormhole are astronomically large, potentially comparable to the mass-energy of a star or more, depending on the model.

5. We Don't Know How to Create One

Even if exotic matter existed, nobody knows how to tear or reshape space-time to form a wormhole. Some theories speculate that primordial wormholes might have formed in the early universe, but there's no evidence for them.

What Would a Wormhole Look Like?

A wormhole mouth wouldn't look like the swirling funnel often shown in illustrations. Those funnel diagrams are two-dimensional "embedding diagrams" used to visualise curved space. In three dimensions, a wormhole mouth would appear as a sphere. Looking into it, you'd see a distorted view of the region at the other end, like looking into a crystal ball, while light from your own surroundings would be bent around the edges.

For Interstellar, Kip Thorne and the visual effects team at Double Negative used his equations to simulate how light would travel near a wormhole and a rapidly spinning black hole. The resulting images were so detailed that the team published scientific papers about the visualisation techniques.

Energy Conditions: The Rules Wormholes Break

Physicists use "energy conditions" to describe what kinds of matter and energy are considered physically reasonable. The null energy condition, for example, roughly says that energy density measured by any light ray can't be negative. Normal matter, light and other known forms of energy obey these conditions.

Traversable wormholes require these conditions to be violated at the throat. Quantum effects can violate them slightly and briefly, as the Casimir effect shows, but whether they can be violated enough, over a large enough region, for long enough, to support a wormhole is one of the key open questions. Most physicists suspect the answer is no for macroscopic wormholes, but the question isn't fully settled.

Black holes and wormholes are related in the mathematics of general relativity, but they are very different objects. Black holes are now firmly established: in 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope produced the first image of the shadow of a supermassive black hole in the galaxy M87, followed in 2022 by Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the centre of our own Milky Way.

Wormholes and Time Travel

One of the most mind-bending ideas is that a wormhole could act as a time machine. In 1988, Morris, Thorne and Ulvi Yurtsever showed how this might work in theory:

  1. Create a traversable wormhole with two mouths close together.
  2. Move one mouth away at nearly the speed of light, then bring it back. Because of time dilation in special relativity, less time passes for the moving mouth.
  3. Now the two mouths are out of sync in time. Entering one mouth could bring you out of the other at an earlier time.

This raises famous paradoxes, such as the "grandfather paradox": what happens if you travel back and prevent your own existence? Stephen Hawking proposed the chronology protection conjecture, suggesting the laws of physics prevent time machines from forming, perhaps because quantum effects would destroy a wormhole just as it became capable of time travel. He also joked that the strongest evidence against time travel is that we haven't been visited by tourists from the future.

Wormholes and Quantum Physics: ER = EPR

In 1935, the same year Einstein and Rosen described their bridges, Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Rosen wrote a separate paper about quantum entanglement, the strange link between particles that Einstein called "spooky action at a distance". In 2013, Maldacena and Susskind proposed that these two ideas might be connected: entangled particles might be linked by tiny, non-traversable wormholes. This "ER = EPR" conjecture is an active area of research in the quest to unite quantum mechanics with gravity.

Related work has explored how, in certain theoretical models, quantum effects might allow wormholes to become briefly traversable. In 2022, a team including physicists from Caltech and Google used a quantum processor to simulate a simplified model related to this idea. Some researchers described it as a "wormhole" experiment, while others argued that it was a quantum simulation of a toy model rather than a physical wormhole in space. No actual tunnel through space-time was created.

Could We Ever Detect a Wormhole?

Scientists have proposed ways a natural wormhole might reveal itself, if any exist:

  • Gravitational lensing: a wormhole mouth might bend light from background stars in distinctive ways, different from lensing by stars or black holes.
  • Unusual orbits: stars orbiting near a wormhole mouth might be influenced by objects on the other side, causing tiny deviations in their motion.
  • Gravitational waves: collisions involving wormholes might produce wave signals distinct from those of black hole mergers, detectable by observatories such as LIGO and Virgo.
  • Shadows: high-resolution images like those from the Event Horizon Telescope might, in principle, show features that differ between black holes and wormholes.

So far, every observation has been consistent with black holes, and no wormhole has been detected.

Common Misconceptions About Wormholes

  • "Black holes are entrances to wormholes." There's no evidence that real black holes lead anywhere. Anything that falls in is expected to be destroyed.
  • "Scientists have found wormholes." None has ever been observed.
  • "Wormholes would allow instant travel." Even in theory, travel through a wormhole would take time, just less than the long route.
  • "Wormholes are pure fantasy." They're a serious topic in theoretical physics, even if they may never exist in practice.

Wormholes in Popular Culture

  • Contact (1985 novel by Carl Sagan, 1997 film): inspired serious scientific work on traversable wormholes.
  • Interstellar (2014): showed a spherical wormhole near Saturn, visualised with help from Kip Thorne's equations.
  • Star Trek: Deep Space Nine: centred on a stable wormhole connecting distant regions of the galaxy.
  • Stargate: used devices creating wormholes between planets.
  • Marvel films: frequently use wormhole-like portals.

These stories often take creative liberties, but some, especially Interstellar, drew heavily on real physics.

Other Ideas for Faster Travel

Wormholes aren't the only theoretical shortcut. In 1994, physicist Miguel Alcubierre proposed a "warp drive" that would contract space in front of a ship and expand it behind, allowing effectively faster-than-light travel without the ship itself breaking the speed of light locally. Like traversable wormholes, the original design requires negative energy. Researchers continue to explore variations, but none is close to practical.

For now, realistic space travel relies on chemical and electric propulsion, with longer-term ideas such as nuclear propulsion, solar sails and laser-driven light sails. Projects like Breakthrough Starshot have proposed sending tiny probes to nearby stars using powerful lasers, a journey that would still take around 20 years to reach Alpha Centauri.

Why Study Wormholes If They Might Not Exist?

Studying wormholes helps physicists test the limits of general relativity and quantum theory. Thought experiments involving wormholes, time travel and black holes have led to important insights about causality, energy conditions, information and the nature of space-time. Even if wormholes never become a means of travel, they are powerful tools for understanding how the universe works.

Key Terms Explained

  • Space-time: the combination of three dimensions of space and one of time into a single four-dimensional continuum.
  • General relativity: Einstein's theory describing gravity as the curvature of space-time.
  • Event horizon: the boundary around a black hole beyond which nothing, not even light, can escape.
  • Throat: the narrowest part of a wormhole connecting its two mouths.
  • Exotic matter: hypothetical matter with negative energy density, needed to hold a traversable wormhole open.
  • Time dilation: the slowing of time for objects moving at high speed or in strong gravity.
  • Quantum entanglement: a connection between particles in which measuring one instantly correlates with the state of the other.
  • Casimir effect: a small attractive force between close metal plates caused by quantum fluctuations, an example of negative energy density.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a white hole in space?

A white hole is a theoretical opposite of a black hole: a region nothing can enter but matter and light can leave. They appear as solutions in Einstein's equations, but none has ever been observed.

Are wormholes real?

Wormholes are allowed by the mathematics of general relativity, but none has ever been observed. Whether they exist in nature is unknown.

Can humans travel through a wormhole?

In theory, a traversable wormhole held open by exotic matter could allow travel. In practice, we have no way to create, stabilise or find one, and the physics may forbid it.

Is a black hole a wormhole?

No. Black holes are real objects confirmed by observation. Some mathematical descriptions of black holes include wormhole-like structures, but these are not traversable and may not exist in real black holes formed by collapsing stars.

What would happen if you entered a wormhole?

Nobody knows. In an idealised traversable wormhole, you might emerge at a distant location. In most theoretical models, a wormhole would collapse or tidal forces and radiation could be deadly.

Did scientists create a wormhole in 2022?

No physical wormhole was created. Researchers simulated a simplified theoretical model on a quantum computer, which some described as having wormhole-like behaviour. The interpretation remains debated.

Is "warm hole" the same as wormhole?

"Warm hole" is a common misspelling. The correct scientific term is "wormhole", coined by physicist John Wheeler in 1957, after the image of a worm tunnelling through an apple.

Could a wormhole connect to another universe?

Some theoretical models allow wormholes connecting separate universes, but whether other universes exist at all is itself unknown. This idea remains highly speculative.

How big would a wormhole need to be for a person to pass through?

Wide enough to keep tidal forces, the stretching caused by differences in gravity, gentle on the human body. Morris and Thorne's analysis suggested throats would need to be very large or carefully designed to keep these forces survivable, which increases the amount of exotic matter required.

Does travelling through a wormhole break the speed of light?

Not locally. A traveller would never move faster than light within their own region of space. The wormhole would simply offer a shorter path, so the journey could take less time than light would need going the long way round.

Who are the leading scientists studying wormholes?

Important contributors include Albert Einstein, Nathan Rosen, John Wheeler, Kip Thorne, Michael Morris, Matt Visser, Juan Maldacena, Leonard Susskind and Daniel Jafferis, among many others working on gravity and quantum theory.

Will we ever build a wormhole?

With current knowledge, it seems extremely unlikely, perhaps impossible. But physics has surprised us before, and research into quantum gravity may one day settle the question.

What is the best book to learn about wormholes?

Black Holes and Time Warps by Kip Thorne and The Science of Interstellar, also by Thorne, explain wormholes and black holes for general readers.

Final Thoughts

Wormholes sit at the frontier between science and imagination. They are legitimate solutions to Einstein's equations, studied seriously by leading physicists, yet they may be forbidden by nature or require conditions impossible to achieve. Whether or not anyone ever travels through one, exploring wormholes has deepened our understanding of gravity, time and the quantum world, and continues to inspire scientists and storytellers alike.
