Updated 20 October 2026

Quick answer: Personal and dependent exemptions no longer reduce US federal income tax. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act set them to zero from 2018, and 2025 legislation made that permanent. Today, the main ways to lower your federal tax bill are the standard deduction or itemised deductions, "above-the-line" adjustments such as retirement and HSA contributions, newer deductions for tips, overtime, car loan interest and seniors, and tax credits such as the Child Tax Credit and Earned Income Tax Credit. Credits cut your tax dollar for dollar, so they are usually worth more than deductions of the same size.

Ask most people what a "tax exemption" is and they'll give a vague answer. That's understandable. US tax law uses the words exemption, deduction, exclusion and credit in specific ways, and the rules have changed several times in recent years. Many older articles still describe personal exemptions that no longer reduce anyone's tax.

This guide explains how the US federal income tax system works today, what happened to exemptions, and the deductions and credits that can legally reduce your tax bill. Figures are for the 2026 tax year (returns filed in 2027) unless stated otherwise. Tax rules change often, so always check the latest numbers on IRS.gov or with a qualified tax professional.

Exemptions, Deductions, Exclusions and Credits: What's the Difference?

  • Exemption: historically, a fixed amount you could subtract from income for yourself, your spouse and each dependent. Personal exemptions are now zero. The word is also used for income that is "exempt" from tax, such as interest on many municipal bonds.
  • Exclusion: income that never counts as taxable in the first place, such as qualifying employer health insurance, gifts you receive, or part of the gain on selling your main home.
  • Deduction: an amount subtracted from your income before tax is calculated. Its value depends on your tax bracket: a 1,000 dollar deduction saves 220 dollars if you're in the 22% bracket.
  • Credit: a direct reduction of the tax you owe. A 1,000 dollar credit saves 1,000 dollars. Some credits are "refundable", meaning you can receive money back even if you owe no tax.

What Happened to Personal Exemptions?

Before 2018, taxpayers could claim a personal exemption for themselves, their spouse and each dependent. In 2017 the exemption was 4,050 dollars per person, so a family of four could subtract 16,200 dollars from their income.

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) of 2017 reduced the personal exemption to zero for 2018 through 2025. To compensate, it nearly doubled the standard deduction and expanded the Child Tax Credit. Those provisions were due to expire at the end of 2025, but the 2025 tax law, often called the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, made the zero exemption and the larger standard deduction permanent.

In practice, most families are not worse off, because the bigger standard deduction and child credits replaced much of the value of the old exemptions. But it does mean that guides talking about "claiming exemptions" on your federal return are out of date.

How US Federal Income Tax Is Calculated

Understanding the order of calculation shows where each tax break fits:

  1. Gross income: wages, self-employment income, interest, dividends, capital gains, rental income and most other income.
  2. Minus exclusions: income that isn't taxed at all, such as pre-tax health insurance premiums.
  3. Minus adjustments ("above-the-line" deductions): such as traditional IRA and HSA contributions, student loan interest and half of self-employment tax. The result is your adjusted gross income (AGI).
  4. Minus the standard deduction or itemised deductions, plus certain other deductions such as the qualified business income deduction. The result is your taxable income.
  5. Apply the tax brackets to taxable income.
  6. Minus tax credits.
  7. Compare with tax already paid through withholding or estimated payments to see whether you owe more or receive a refund.

Federal Tax Brackets

The US uses a progressive system with seven brackets: 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%. The bracket thresholds are adjusted for inflation every year.

A common misunderstanding is that moving into a higher bracket means all your income is taxed at the higher rate. In fact, only the portion of income within each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate. Your marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar of income; your effective rate, total tax divided by total income, is always lower. A raise can never reduce your take-home pay because of brackets alone.

The Standard Deduction

The standard deduction is a fixed amount you can subtract from your income without listing specific expenses. For 2026, it is approximately:

Filing statusStandard deduction (2026)
Single or married filing separately16,100 dollars
Married filing jointly32,200 dollars
Head of household24,150 dollars

Taxpayers aged 65 or older, or blind, receive an additional standard deduction on top of these amounts. Around nine in ten taxpayers now take the standard deduction because it is larger than their itemised deductions would be.

Itemised Deductions

You can choose to itemise instead of taking the standard deduction if your eligible expenses add up to more. The main itemised deductions are:

Mortgage Interest

Interest on up to 750,000 dollars of mortgage debt used to buy, build or substantially improve your main or second home is generally deductible (older mortgages taken out before 15 December 2017 may qualify for a 1 million dollar limit).

State and Local Taxes (SALT)

You can deduct state and local income or sales taxes plus property taxes, up to a cap. The 2025 law raised the cap from 10,000 dollars to 40,000 dollars for 2025, increasing by 1% a year through 2029, with the higher cap phasing down for households with income above roughly 500,000 dollars. The cap is scheduled to return to 10,000 dollars in 2030.

Charitable Donations

Donations to qualified charities are deductible if you itemise, subject to limits based on your income. Keep receipts and written acknowledgements for larger gifts. From 2026, non-itemisers can also deduct a limited amount of cash donations: up to 1,000 dollars for single filers and 2,000 dollars for married couples filing jointly.

Medical Expenses

Unreimbursed medical and dental expenses are deductible to the extent they exceed 7.5% of your AGI. This mainly helps people with very high medical costs in a given year.

Other Itemised Deductions

These include casualty and theft losses from federally declared disasters, and gambling losses up to the amount of gambling winnings reported.

Above-the-Line Deductions

These adjustments reduce your income whether or not you itemise, which makes them valuable for almost everyone who qualifies:

  • Traditional IRA contributions: up to 7,500 dollars for 2026 (more if you're 50 or older), though the deduction phases out at higher incomes if you or your spouse have a workplace retirement plan.
  • Health Savings Account (HSA) contributions: if you have a qualifying high-deductible health plan. For 2026 the limits are about 4,400 dollars for self-only coverage and 8,750 dollars for family coverage, plus 1,000 dollars catch-up from age 55.
  • Student loan interest: up to 2,500 dollars a year, subject to income limits.
  • Self-employment deductions: half of self-employment tax, self-employed health insurance premiums and contributions to SEP-IRA or solo 401(k) plans.
  • Educator expenses: eligible teachers can deduct a few hundred dollars of classroom supplies.

New Deductions From the 2025 Tax Law

The 2025 legislation introduced several temporary deductions, generally available for tax years 2025 through 2028, whether or not you itemise:

  • Tips: workers in occupations that customarily receive tips can deduct up to 25,000 dollars of qualified tip income, phasing out at higher incomes.
  • Overtime: the premium part of qualified overtime pay (the "half" in time-and-a-half) is deductible up to 12,500 dollars, or 25,000 dollars for joint filers, with income phase-outs.
  • Car loan interest: interest on loans for new personal vehicles assembled in the US can be deducted up to 10,000 dollars a year, subject to income limits.
  • Seniors: taxpayers aged 65 and over can claim an extra deduction of up to 6,000 dollars per person, on top of the existing additional standard deduction, phasing out at higher incomes.

These rules have detailed eligibility conditions, and the IRS has issued guidance on how to report them. Check the current instructions before claiming them.

Retirement Accounts: Reducing Tax While Saving

Workplace plans such as 401(k)s and 403(b)s are among the most powerful tax tools available. Contributions to a traditional 401(k) are taken from your pay before income tax, reducing taxable income immediately. For 2026, the employee contribution limit is 24,500 dollars, with an additional catch-up contribution for those aged 50 and over, and a higher catch-up for those aged 60 to 63.

Roth 401(k)s and Roth IRAs work the other way around: no deduction now, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. Choosing between them depends on whether you expect your tax rate to be higher now or in retirement. Many people use both for flexibility.

Key Tax Credits

Child Tax Credit

For 2025 the Child Tax Credit is 2,200 dollars per qualifying child under 17, adjusted for inflation in later years. Part of it is refundable for families with earned income. It phases out above 200,000 dollars of income for single filers and 400,000 dollars for joint filers. Children need a valid Social Security number, and the 2025 law also requires at least one parent to have one.

Credit for Other Dependents

A non-refundable credit of 500 dollars is available for dependents who don't qualify for the Child Tax Credit, such as older children in college or dependent parents.

Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)

A refundable credit for low and moderate-income workers, worth several thousand dollars for families with children. Many eligible people don't claim it, so it's worth checking with the IRS's online eligibility tool.

Child and Dependent Care Credit

Helps working parents pay for childcare or care for a disabled dependent. The 2025 law increased the credit percentage for many families from 2026.

Education Credits

  • American Opportunity Tax Credit: up to 2,500 dollars per eligible student for the first four years of higher education, partly refundable.
  • Lifetime Learning Credit: up to 2,000 dollars per return for tuition, including graduate and professional courses.

Saver's Credit

Low and moderate-income workers who contribute to retirement accounts can receive a credit worth up to 50% of their contributions, up to a limit.

Energy Credits

Credits for home energy improvements and for new and used clean vehicles were ended early by the 2025 law. The vehicle credits ended for vehicles acquired after 30 September 2025, and the home energy credits end for property placed in service after 31 December 2025. Check the dates carefully if you made purchases around those deadlines.

Income That Is Exempt or Excluded From Tax

Some types of income are wholly or partly free from federal income tax:

  • Interest on most municipal bonds issued by state and local governments.
  • Employer-paid health insurance and certain other fringe benefits.
  • Gifts and inheritances you receive (the giver or estate may owe gift or estate tax in rare, large cases).
  • Life insurance proceeds paid on death.
  • Up to 250,000 dollars of gain on selling your main home (500,000 dollars for married couples), if you owned and lived in it for at least two of the past five years.
  • Qualified withdrawals from Roth IRAs and Roth 401(k)s.
  • Qualified HSA withdrawals for medical expenses.
  • Qualified 529 plan withdrawals for education.
  • Child support payments.

Some states also exempt certain income, such as Social Security benefits or military pensions. Nine states, including Texas and Florida, have no broad-based state income tax on wages.

Tax-Exempt Organisations

The term "tax-exempt" is also used for organisations. Charities, religious organisations, educational institutions and many non-profits recognised under section 501(c)(3) of the tax code don't pay federal income tax on income related to their mission, and donations to them can be deductible for donors who itemise. You can check an organisation's status with the IRS's Tax Exempt Organization Search tool before donating.

Worked Example: A Family of Four

Consider Maria and James, a married couple filing jointly with two children aged 8 and 12. Their combined wages are 110,000 dollars in 2026. Here's a simplified version of how their tax might work:

  1. They contribute 10,000 dollars in total to traditional 401(k) plans through work, so their taxable wages fall to 100,000 dollars.
  2. Their itemised deductions would be well below the 32,200 dollar standard deduction, so they take the standard deduction. Taxable income: about 67,800 dollars.
  3. Applying the 10% and 12% brackets for married couples, their tax before credits is roughly 7,700 dollars.
  4. They claim the Child Tax Credit for two children, worth 4,400 dollars.
  5. Their final federal income tax is roughly 3,200 dollars, an effective rate of under 3% of their gross wages.

This example is simplified and ignores state taxes, Social Security and Medicare taxes, and other details, but it shows how retirement contributions, the standard deduction and credits work together.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Relying on outdated information about personal exemptions, deduction limits or credits.
  • Itemising when the standard deduction is larger, or vice versa. Tax software will usually compare both.
  • Missing above-the-line deductions like HSA contributions or student loan interest.
  • Not claiming refundable credits such as the EITC.
  • Poor record-keeping, which makes it hard to prove deductions if the IRS asks.
  • Choosing the wrong filing status. Head of household, for example, offers a larger standard deduction than single status for qualifying unmarried parents.
  • Ignoring withholding. Big life changes such as marriage, a new baby or a second job should prompt you to update your Form W-4.

Practical Ways to Legally Reduce Your Tax Bill

  1. Contribute to your employer's 401(k), at least enough to get the full employer match.
  2. Use an HSA if you have a qualifying health plan; it offers a deduction now, tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals for medical costs.
  3. Consider an IRA, traditional or Roth depending on your situation.
  4. Check eligibility for every credit, especially if you have children, are a student or have a low to moderate income.
  5. Bunch charitable donations into alternate years if that lets you itemise in some years and take the standard deduction in others.
  6. Hold investments for more than a year to qualify for lower long-term capital gains rates, and use tax-loss harvesting where appropriate.
  7. If self-employed, track all business expenses and consider a SEP-IRA or solo 401(k).
  8. Use the IRS Free File programme or Volunteer Income Tax Assistance (VITA) if your income qualifies.

Important Dates

  • Mid-April: usual deadline for filing federal returns and paying any tax due for the previous year, and for making IRA and HSA contributions for that year.
  • Mid-October: extended filing deadline if you requested an extension. An extension gives more time to file, not to pay.
  • Quarterly estimated payments: usually due in April, June, September and January for self-employed people and others without enough withholding.

State Income Taxes

Most states have their own income tax with separate rules, deductions and credits. Some follow federal definitions closely, while others differ significantly. A few states still offer personal exemptions on state returns even though the federal exemption is zero. If you move between states during the year, you may need to file part-year returns in both.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I still claim a personal exemption on my federal return?

No. The federal personal and dependent exemption amount is zero, and the 2025 tax law made this permanent. Some states still have their own exemptions.

What does "exempt" mean on Form W-4?

Claiming exempt on Form W-4 tells your employer not to withhold federal income tax. You can only do this if you owed no federal income tax last year and expect to owe none this year. Claiming exempt incorrectly can lead to a large bill and penalties.

Is it better to take the standard deduction or itemise?

Choose whichever is larger. Most people now take the standard deduction, but homeowners with large mortgages, high state taxes or big charitable gifts may benefit from itemising, especially with the higher SALT cap.

Are Social Security benefits taxable?

Up to 85% of Social Security benefits can be taxable at the federal level, depending on your other income. Many people with modest income pay no tax on them. The new senior deduction can reduce tax for many older taxpayers.

Do non-US citizens pay US income tax?

US residents for tax purposes, including green card holders and many visa holders who meet the substantial presence test, generally pay tax on worldwide income like citizens. Non-resident aliens generally pay tax only on US-source income. Tax treaties between the US and other countries, including India, can affect how income is taxed.

How long should I keep tax records?

The IRS generally recommends keeping records for at least three years after filing, and longer in certain situations, such as seven years if you claim a loss from worthless securities. Keep records relating to property until you sell it and the period for that year's return has passed.

Should I use a tax professional?

Simple returns can often be filed with tax software or free IRS programmes. Consider a professional if you're self-employed, own rental property, have income from several countries, or experienced major life events such as selling a business.

What is the difference between a refundable and non-refundable credit?

A non-refundable credit can reduce your tax to zero but no further. A refundable credit can go beyond that, so you receive the excess as a refund. The Earned Income Tax Credit is fully refundable, and part of the Child Tax Credit is refundable.

Can I deduct my home office?

Self-employed people who use part of their home regularly and exclusively for business can usually claim a home office deduction. Employees working from home for an employer generally cannot claim it on their federal return.

Final Thoughts

US personal exemptions have been replaced by a larger standard deduction and bigger credits. The ways to reduce your tax bill haven't disappeared; they've just moved. Knowing the difference between deductions and credits, and using retirement accounts, HSAs and the credits you're entitled to, can save you thousands of dollars each year.

Tax laws change frequently, and the 2025 legislation added new deductions with their own rules and end dates. Review your situation every year, keep good records, and seek professional advice when your finances become more complex.
