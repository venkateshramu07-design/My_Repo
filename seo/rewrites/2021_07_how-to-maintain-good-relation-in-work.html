Updated 20 October 2026

Quick answer: To maintain good relationships at work, communicate clearly and respectfully, be reliable, show genuine interest in colleagues, give credit generously, listen before reacting, handle conflict early and privately, respect boundaries and avoid gossip. When relationships are strained, acknowledge the issue, apologise where appropriate and focus on shared goals. Strong workplace relationships improve job satisfaction, teamwork and career opportunities.

Most of us spend a large part of our waking hours at work, often with people we didn't choose. Those relationships have a huge effect on how we feel each day. Gallup's long-running workplace research has found that having a close friend at work is linked with higher engagement, and good relationships with managers and colleagues are among the strongest predictors of job satisfaction.

Good working relationships don't happen by accident. They're built through small, consistent behaviours. This guide covers how to build them, keep them strong and repair them when things go wrong.

Why Workplace Relationships Matter

  • Better collaboration: trust speeds up teamwork and problem-solving.
  • Less stress: supportive colleagues make difficult periods easier.
  • Career growth: people recommend and promote colleagues they trust.
  • Job satisfaction: positive relationships make work more enjoyable and reduce turnover.

1. Communicate Clearly and Respectfully

  • Be clear about what you need and by when.
  • Choose the right channel: complex or sensitive topics deserve a conversation, not a chat message.
  • Keep your tone polite, especially in writing, where messages can easily sound harsher than intended.
  • Respond to messages within a reasonable time, even if only to say you'll reply later.

2. Be Reliable

Trust is built when your actions match your words. Meet deadlines, follow through on promises and tell people early if you can't deliver. Reliability is one of the fastest ways to earn respect.

3. Listen Actively

Listening shows respect and prevents misunderstandings.

  • Give your full attention; put your phone away.
  • Let people finish before responding.
  • Summarise what you heard: "So the main concern is the timeline?"
  • Ask questions to understand, not to argue.

4. Show Genuine Interest in Colleagues

Learn about the people you work with: their roles, challenges and, where they're comfortable, their interests outside work. Remember details, ask how a project or family event went and offer help when they're busy. Small gestures build connection.

5. Give Credit and Appreciation

Acknowledge good work publicly and specifically: "Priya's analysis helped us catch the pricing error before launch." Saying thank you costs nothing and builds goodwill. Never take credit for others' work.

6. Respect Differences and Boundaries

  • Colleagues have different working styles, cultures, beliefs and life situations.
  • Respect people's time, including working hours and holidays.
  • Be mindful of personal space and privacy.
  • Keep jokes and comments inclusive and professional.

7. Avoid Gossip and Office Politics Traps

Gossip may create a sense of closeness in the moment, but it erodes trust. If someone gossips with you, they'll likely gossip about you. Stay neutral, change the subject or politely step away.

8. Be a Team Player

Offer help when colleagues are overloaded, share useful information and celebrate team successes. Focus on what's best for the team, not just on individual recognition.

9. Manage Your Emotions

Everyone has stressful days, but snapping at colleagues damages relationships. When you feel frustrated, pause before responding, take a walk or wait until you're calm before sending a difficult email.

10. Build a Good Relationship With Your Manager

  • Understand their priorities and preferred way of communicating.
  • Keep them informed of progress and problems early.
  • Ask for feedback and act on it.
  • Bring possible solutions along with problems.

11. Build Trust Through Small Moments

Trust grows from many small interactions rather than a few big gestures. Relationship researcher John Gottman described trust as built in "sliding door moments": small opportunities to connect or turn away. At work, these include answering a colleague's question patiently, noticing when someone seems stressed, sharing useful information without being asked, or covering for a teammate during an emergency. Each moment is minor, but together they create a reputation as someone people can rely on.

12. Respect Everyone, Regardless of Rank

How you treat cleaners, security staff, interns and junior colleagues says a lot about your character, and people notice. Be courteous to everyone, learn their names and thank them for their work. Many leaders say they judge job candidates partly on how they treat receptionists and assistants.

13. Understand Different Working Styles

Colleagues differ in how they communicate, make decisions and handle stress. Some prefer detailed written plans; others think best in conversation. Some want quick answers; others need time to reflect. Personality frameworks such as DISC or the Big Five can offer a helpful vocabulary, as long as they're not used to label people. Simply asking, "How do you prefer to receive feedback?" or "Do you like updates by email or a quick call?" can prevent many misunderstandings.

14. Navigate Cultural Differences

Modern workplaces are often multicultural, especially in global companies and cities. Cultural differences can affect:

  • Directness: some cultures value blunt feedback; others prefer softer, indirect communication.
  • Hierarchy: in some cultures, junior staff rarely challenge seniors openly; in others, open debate is encouraged.
  • Time: attitudes to punctuality and deadlines vary.
  • Relationships: in many cultures, building personal rapport comes before business.
  • Holidays and customs: respect colleagues' religious and cultural observances.

Approach differences with curiosity rather than judgement, and ask colleagues about their preferences.

15. Handle Gossip and Negativity Gracefully

When colleagues complain or gossip, you can avoid joining in without seeming superior. Try responses like "I haven't seen that side of her" or "Maybe we should talk to him directly about it". Steering conversations toward solutions protects your reputation and helps create a healthier culture.

Relationships With Different People at Work

With Your Peers

  • Collaborate rather than compete.
  • Share credit and information.
  • Support each other during busy periods.

With Your Team (If You're a Manager)

  • Hold regular one-to-ones focused on their goals and challenges.
  • Be fair and consistent.
  • Recognise effort and results publicly; give criticism privately.
  • Be approachable and admit your own mistakes.

With Senior Leaders

  • Be concise and prepared.
  • Bring data and recommendations.
  • Respect their time, and follow through quickly on requests.

With Other Departments

  • Learn what they do and what pressures they face.
  • Involve them early in projects that affect them.
  • Thank them when they help your team.

With Clients and External Partners

  • Communicate clearly and set realistic expectations.
  • Respond promptly, even if just to acknowledge a message.
  • Admit problems honestly and propose solutions.

How to Handle Workplace Conflict

Disagreements are normal and can even lead to better ideas. What matters is how they're handled.

  1. Address issues early: small problems grow when ignored.
  2. Talk privately: never criticise someone in front of others.
  3. Focus on behaviour, not personality: "The report arrived after the deadline" instead of "You're unreliable".
  4. Use "I" statements: "I felt overlooked when..." rather than "You always...".
  5. Seek shared goals: remind each other what you're both trying to achieve.
  6. Agree on next steps: clarify who will do what.
  7. Escalate if needed: involve your manager or HR for serious or repeated issues, especially harassment or discrimination.

How to Repair a Damaged Work Relationship

Research on workplace relationships suggests that repair depends on acknowledgement, genuine effort and time.

  • Acknowledge what happened: don't pretend the issue didn't exist.
  • Apologise sincerely if you contributed to the problem, without excuses.
  • Listen to their perspective.
  • Show change through actions over the following weeks.
  • Be patient: trust rebuilds gradually.

Difficult Colleague Types and How to Work With Them

The Credit Taker

Share progress in writing and in team meetings so your contributions are visible. Copy relevant people on key updates, and when presenting joint work, describe each person's role clearly. If it continues, raise it calmly and privately: "I noticed the report was presented as solely your work. I'd like us to make sure both our contributions are recognised."

The Constant Complainer

Listen briefly, acknowledge their frustration, then redirect: "That sounds frustrating. What do you think we could do about it?" Limit how much time you spend in complaint sessions.

The Micromanaging Colleague or Boss

Build trust by giving frequent, proactive updates. Ask what level of detail they want and agree on check-in points. Over time, consistent reliability often reduces micromanagement.

The Know-It-All

Acknowledge their expertise where it's genuine, back your own points with evidence, and ask questions rather than arguing directly. Focus discussions on the shared goal.

The Passive-Aggressive Colleague

Address issues directly but calmly: "I noticed your comment in the meeting. Is there something about the project you'd like to discuss?" Clear, open communication reduces the space for hidden hostility.

The Slacker

Clarify responsibilities and deadlines in writing. If their lack of contribution affects your work, discuss it with them first, then with your manager if needed, focusing on project impact rather than personal criticism.

How to Give Feedback Without Damaging Relationships

Feedback is essential, but poorly delivered criticism can harm trust. A simple, widely used framework is SBI: Situation, Behaviour, Impact, developed by the Center for Creative Leadership.

  • Situation: "In yesterday's client meeting..."
  • Behaviour: "...you interrupted the client several times..."
  • Impact: "...and I noticed they stopped sharing details about their concerns."

Then invite discussion: "How did the meeting feel from your side?" This keeps feedback specific, factual and focused on improvement rather than character. Balance it with regular positive feedback, so colleagues know you notice what they do well.

How to Receive Feedback Gracefully

  • Listen fully without interrupting or defending yourself.
  • Ask clarifying questions: "Can you give me an example?"
  • Thank the person for their honesty.
  • Take time to reflect before deciding what to change.
  • Follow up later to show you've acted on it.

Colleagues who receive feedback well are trusted with more responsibility, because people know they can be honest with them.

Emotional Intelligence at Work

Psychologist Daniel Goleman popularised the idea that emotional intelligence (EQ) is as important as technical skill for success at work. It has five components:

  1. Self-awareness: knowing your emotions, strengths and triggers.
  2. Self-regulation: managing your reactions, especially under pressure.
  3. Motivation: staying driven by goals and values, not just rewards.
  4. Empathy: understanding others' feelings and perspectives.
  5. Social skills: communicating, influencing, resolving conflict and building relationships.

The good news is that EQ can be developed through reflection, feedback, practice and, for some, coaching.

Networking Within Your Organisation

Strong internal relationships make it easier to get things done, learn about opportunities and get support for your ideas.

  • Join cross-functional projects to meet people outside your team.
  • Attend company events, clubs and volunteer activities.
  • Have coffee chats: ask colleagues in other departments what they do and what challenges they face.
  • Share useful knowledge: articles, tools or lessons learned.
  • Find a mentor outside your direct reporting line for broader perspective.
  • Stay in touch with colleagues who move to other teams or companies.

Approach networking with curiosity and generosity rather than as a way to get something. Genuine relationships tend to grow naturally from shared work and mutual help.

Celebrating Together

Shared celebrations strengthen bonds. Mark project launches, work anniversaries, birthdays and festivals in ways that are inclusive and respect different cultures and preferences. A team lunch, a handwritten card, a shout-out in a meeting or a small treat can go a long way. In diverse teams, invite colleagues to share how they celebrate their own festivals; it builds understanding and makes everyone feel included.

Keeping Work Relationships Healthy Under Pressure

Deadlines, layoffs, reorganisations and crises test relationships. During stressful periods:

  • Communicate more, not less, and share what you know.
  • Assume colleagues are doing their best under pressure.
  • Avoid blaming when things go wrong; focus on fixing problems.
  • Check in on teammates who seem overwhelmed.
  • Celebrate when the team gets through a difficult stretch together.

How to Apologise at Work

Everyone makes mistakes that affect colleagues. A good apology can strengthen a relationship rather than damage it. Effective apologies include:

  1. Acknowledgement: name what happened clearly. "I missed the deadline for the data you needed."
  2. Responsibility: avoid excuses or blaming others. "That was my responsibility."
  3. Impact: show you understand the consequences. "I know it delayed your report to the client."
  4. Repair: offer to fix what you can. "I've finished it now and can help you update the report this afternoon."
  5. Prevention: explain what you'll do differently. "I've set reminders for future deadlines."

Avoid "non-apologies" like "I'm sorry if you were upset", which shift responsibility to the other person.

Saying No Without Damaging Relationships

Constantly saying yes leads to overload and resentment, but a blunt "no" can feel unhelpful. Try these approaches:

  • Offer an alternative: "I can't take this on this week, but I could help next Tuesday."
  • Clarify priorities: "I'm working on the budget review for our manager. Should I pause that to help with this?"
  • Suggest someone else: "Arjun has worked on this tool before; he might be able to help faster."
  • Help partially: "I can't write the whole report, but I'm happy to review a draft."

Your First 90 Days in a New Job

The first few months shape how colleagues see you for a long time.

  • First 30 days: listen, learn names and roles, understand how decisions are made, ask lots of questions and meet key colleagues one-to-one.
  • Days 31 to 60: deliver a few early wins, clarify expectations with your manager and start contributing ideas.
  • Days 61 to 90: take ownership of larger tasks, offer help to others and build relationships beyond your immediate team.

Avoid criticising how things were done before you arrived until you understand the reasons behind them.

The Business Case for Good Workplace Relationships

Positive relationships aren't just pleasant; they improve performance.

  • Engagement: Gallup's research consistently links close workplace friendships with higher engagement, better customer service and lower staff turnover.
  • Team performance: Google's Project Aristotle found that psychological safety, built through trust and respect, was the most important factor in effective teams.
  • Health: supportive colleagues reduce stress, and research links social support at work with lower burnout.
  • Innovation: people share ideas more freely when they trust each other.
  • Retention: employees are less likely to leave when they have strong relationships with colleagues and managers.

Small Daily Habits That Build Strong Work Relationships

  • Greet people warmly when you arrive or log on.
  • Learn and use colleagues' names.
  • Ask one genuine question about someone's work or life each day.
  • Reply to messages promptly, even briefly.
  • Offer help when someone looks overloaded.
  • Say thank you specifically.
  • Keep your promises, especially small ones.
  • Join team lunches, celebrations and social events when you can.

Working Relationships in Remote and Hybrid Teams

  • Turn on your camera for important conversations when possible.
  • Schedule informal virtual coffees to replace hallway chats.
  • Over-communicate progress and availability.
  • Make the most of in-person days for collaboration and connection.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Overpromising and underdelivering.
  • Venting about colleagues on social media.
  • Ignoring messages from people you don't like.
  • Only reaching out when you need something.
  • Letting small resentments build up.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I be friends with my coworkers?

Friendly relationships are valuable, and close friendships can make work more enjoyable. Keep professional boundaries, especially if you manage or are managed by the person.

How do I deal with a colleague I don't like?

Stay polite and professional, focus on shared work goals and limit interactions to what's necessary. You don't have to like everyone to work well together.

What if my manager is the problem?

Try a respectful, private conversation about specific issues. If problems continue or involve mistreatment, consult HR or consider moving to another team.

How do I make a good impression in a new job?

Introduce yourself, ask questions, listen more than you speak at first, deliver on early tasks and learn how the team prefers to work.

How do I rebuild trust after a conflict with my manager?

Request a private conversation, acknowledge your part, listen to their concerns and agree on clear expectations going forward. Then consistently deliver on those expectations; trust is rebuilt through actions over time.

How do I handle a colleague who keeps interrupting me?

Calmly hold your ground in the moment: "I'd like to finish my point, then I'd love to hear yours." If it continues, raise it privately and kindly; many people don't realise they do it.

Should I connect with colleagues on social media?

Professional networks like LinkedIn are usually fine. For personal social media, it's a matter of comfort; you can politely keep some accounts private. Be mindful of what you post about work, colleagues and your employer.

How do I build relationships quickly in a new team?

Schedule short introductions with each team member, ask about their work and priorities, offer help early, remember personal details they share and follow through on small commitments in your first few weeks.

How can introverts build relationships at work?

Focus on one-to-one conversations, written communication and shared projects rather than large social events. Thoughtful questions, good listening and reliability build strong relationships without needing to be the loudest person in the room.

Is it okay to date a coworker?

Many workplaces allow it, but check your company's policy, especially if one person reports to the other. Keep the relationship professional at work and consider how a breakup might affect your working lives.

How do I set boundaries with colleagues without seeming rude?

Be clear and polite: "I'm focusing on a deadline until 3 pm. Can we talk after that?" or "I don't check messages after 7 pm, but I'll reply first thing tomorrow." Consistent, friendly boundaries are usually respected.

What should I do if I'm being bullied or harassed at work?

Keep a detailed record of incidents, including dates, what happened and any witnesses. Report it to your manager or HR, or to a designated committee where one exists, such as India's Internal Complaints Committee for sexual harassment cases. Seek support from trusted colleagues or professional advisers.

Final Thoughts

Good working relationships are built one interaction at a time: a clear message, a kept promise, a sincere thank-you, a conflict handled with respect. Invest in them consistently, and you'll enjoy work more, achieve more with your team and build a reputation that follows you throughout your career.

If you found these tips useful, share them with a colleague.
