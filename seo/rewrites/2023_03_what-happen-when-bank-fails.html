Quick answer: A bank fails when it can't meet its obligations to depositors and creditors, usually because of heavy losses, a run on deposits or a loss of confidence. Regulators then step in: they may restrict withdrawals, appoint an administrator, arrange a merger with a stronger bank, restructure the bank or close it. In India, deposits are insured by the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC) up to 5 lakh rupees per depositor per bank, covering principal and interest. In the US, the FDIC insures up to 250,000 dollars per depositor per insured bank for each ownership category. Money above these limits may be recovered only partly, or later, depending on how the failure is resolved.

Banks are the backbone of the financial system. We trust them with our salaries, savings and loans, usually without a second thought. But banks can and do fail. Recent examples, from PMC Bank and Yes Bank in India to Silicon Valley Bank in the US and Credit Suisse in Europe, have shown how quickly problems can arise and how important it is to understand what happens next.

This guide explains why banks fail, what regulators do, how deposit insurance works in India and other countries, what happens to depositors and borrowers, lessons from recent bank failures, and how to protect your money.

How Banks Work, and Why They Can Fail

Banks take deposits and use most of that money to make loans and investments. They keep only a fraction as cash or highly liquid assets. This system, called fractional reserve banking, works well as long as depositors don't all try to withdraw their money at once.

A bank can fail for several reasons:

1. Bad Loans

If many borrowers fail to repay, the bank's losses can wipe out its capital. In India, high levels of non-performing assets (NPAs) strained many banks in the late 2010s.

2. Liquidity Crisis and Bank Runs

Even a solvent bank can fail if too many depositors withdraw money at once. A bank run can be triggered by rumours or real problems, and in the digital age, withdrawals can happen very quickly through mobile apps.

3. Interest Rate Risk

Banks that invest heavily in long-term bonds can suffer large losses when interest rates rise, because bond values fall. If they must sell those bonds to meet withdrawals, the losses become real.

4. Fraud and Poor Governance

Mismanagement, hidden losses or fraud can destroy a bank. India's PMC Bank collapse in 2019 involved large undisclosed loans to a single troubled real estate group, reportedly concealed through thousands of fictitious accounts.

5. Concentration Risk

Banks that depend heavily on one industry, region or type of depositor are more vulnerable when that sector struggles.

6. Economic Shocks

Recessions, property crashes or financial crises can cause widespread loan defaults and falling asset values.

What Happens When a Bank Fails?

When a bank is in serious trouble, regulators act to protect depositors and the financial system. The exact process depends on the country and the type of bank, but common steps include:

  1. Early warning and supervision: regulators monitor banks' capital, asset quality and liquidity. In India, the RBI uses a Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework, placing restrictions on weak banks, such as limits on lending and expansion, until they improve.
  2. Restrictions on withdrawals: regulators may impose a moratorium or directions limiting withdrawals to stop a run while a solution is found.
  3. Administrator appointed: the regulator may supersede the bank's board and appoint an administrator to manage it.
  4. Resolution: the regulator seeks the best option, which may include:
    • Merging the weak bank with a stronger one.
    • Reconstruction with new capital from investors.
    • Transferring deposits and good assets to another bank.
    • Liquidation, closing the bank and selling its assets.
  5. Deposit insurance payout: if needed, the deposit insurer pays insured depositors up to the limit.
  6. Recovery for others: creditors and depositors with uninsured amounts may receive some money from the sale of the bank's assets, often over a long period.

Warning Signs a Bank May Be in Trouble

Ordinary depositors can't see inside a bank's books, but some signs are worth noticing:

  • RBI restrictions: placement under Prompt Corrective Action, directions limiting withdrawals or lending, or supersession of the board.
  • Rising bad loans and repeated losses in published results.
  • Falling capital ratios close to regulatory minimums.
  • Difficulty raising capital or repeated failed fundraising attempts.
  • Frequent management changes, auditor resignations or governance controversies.
  • Unusually high deposit rates compared with peers, which may signal a need to attract funds.
  • Credit rating downgrades of the bank's bonds.

None of these alone means a bank will fail, but they're reasons to make sure your deposits there stay within insured limits.

Deposit Insurance in India: DICGC

The Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC), a wholly owned subsidiary of the RBI, insures bank deposits in India. Key features:

  • Coverage limit: up to 5 lakh rupees per depositor per bank, covering both principal and interest. The limit was raised from 1 lakh rupees in February 2020.
  • Who is covered: all commercial banks, including branches of foreign banks in India, regional rural banks, small finance banks, payments banks, local area banks and most cooperative banks.
  • What is covered: savings, current, fixed and recurring deposits.
  • What is not covered: deposits of foreign governments, central and state governments, inter-bank deposits, and some others specified by DICGC.
  • Cost: banks pay the insurance premium; depositors don't pay anything.
  • Faster payouts: amendments to the DICGC Act in 2021 enabled depositors of banks placed under all-inclusive directions by the RBI to receive insured amounts within about 90 days, rather than waiting for the bank to be liquidated.

How the Limit Applies

  • The 5 lakh rupee limit applies per depositor per bank, combining all your accounts in the same capacity at that bank, including all branches.
  • Deposits held in different capacities, such as individually, jointly with another person, or as a partner in a firm, are insured separately.
  • Deposits at different banks are insured separately, so spreading large savings across banks increases protection.

What Happens to Different Customers?

Depositors

Insured deposits are protected up to the limit. Amounts above the limit may be recovered partly or fully, depending on the resolution. In mergers or rescues, depositors are often transferred to the acquiring bank and may not lose anything, though withdrawals may be restricted temporarily.

Borrowers

If you have a loan from a failed bank, you still owe the money. Your loan is usually transferred to the acquiring bank or the administrator, and you must keep making payments as per your agreement.

Shareholders and Bondholders

Shareholders usually suffer the biggest losses and may lose their entire investment. Some bondholders may also face losses. In the Yes Bank rescue, certain Additional Tier 1 (AT1) bonds were written down entirely, a decision that led to prolonged legal challenges.

Locker Holders

Items in safe deposit lockers are not bank assets and remain the property of the customer. Access may be temporarily affected during a crisis, but contents should be returned to their owners.

Recent Bank Failures and Rescues

India

  • PMC Bank (2019): the RBI imposed restrictions after large hidden loans were discovered. Withdrawals were severely limited for a long time, causing hardship for many depositors. PMC was later merged into Unity Small Finance Bank.
  • Yes Bank (2020): after mounting bad loans and failed attempts to raise capital, the RBI imposed a moratorium. A reconstruction scheme led by State Bank of India and other banks injected capital, and depositors were protected.
  • Lakshmi Vilas Bank (2020): placed under a moratorium and then merged with DBS Bank India, protecting depositors.

United States and Europe

  • Silicon Valley Bank (March 2023): SVB had invested heavily in long-term bonds that lost value as interest rates rose. When it announced losses, depositors, many of them technology start-ups with balances far above the FDIC limit, withdrew around 42 billion dollars in a single day. The bank was closed within 48 hours. US authorities invoked a systemic risk exception to protect all deposits, including uninsured ones.
  • Signature Bank and First Republic Bank (2023): also failed during the same period of stress. First Republic was sold to JPMorgan Chase.
  • Credit Suisse (March 2023): after years of scandals and losses, the Swiss bank faced a loss of confidence and was taken over by UBS in a government-brokered deal.
  • Lehman Brothers (2008): the collapse of this US investment bank during the global financial crisis triggered worldwide panic and remains the largest bankruptcy filing in US history.

Lessons From Bank Failures

  • Bank runs can be extremely fast in the digital age.
  • Interest rate risk matters, even for banks with low credit losses.
  • Concentration of depositors in one sector increases vulnerability.
  • Strong supervision and early action reduce damage.
  • Deposit insurance limits matter: most individual depositors are fully protected, but large balances may not be.
  • Governance and transparency are essential; hidden losses tend to grow.

What Happens During an RBI Moratorium?

When the RBI places a bank under a moratorium or all-inclusive directions, several things typically happen:

  • Withdrawal limits: depositors may only be able to withdraw a fixed amount for a period, with exceptions sometimes allowed for medical emergencies, education or weddings on application.
  • Restrictions on new loans and investments by the bank.
  • Loan repayments continue: borrowers must keep paying EMIs.
  • An administrator manages the bank while a resolution plan is prepared.
  • DICGC payout: eligible depositors can claim insured amounts within the time frame set by the amended DICGC Act.

If your bank is placed under such directions, read official RBI announcements and bank communications carefully, submit any required claim documents promptly, and beware of fraudsters claiming they can speed up access to your money for a fee.

The Role of Central Banks as Lender of Last Resort

Central banks can lend to banks facing temporary liquidity problems, a role known as the lender of last resort. This helps solvent banks survive sudden withdrawals without selling assets at fire-sale prices. During the 2023 banking stress in the US, the Federal Reserve created a new lending facility, the Bank Term Funding Program, allowing banks to borrow against government bonds at their face value. The RBI has various liquidity facilities for Indian banks. Such support aims to prevent panic from spreading, though it doesn't save banks that are fundamentally insolvent.

Bank Failures and the Wider Economy

Bank failures can affect the economy beyond depositors:

  • Credit crunch: banks become cautious about lending, making it harder for businesses and households to borrow.
  • Contagion: fear can spread to other banks, triggering runs even on healthy institutions.
  • Market turmoil: bank shares and bonds can fall sharply.
  • Government costs: rescues may require public money, raising concerns about moral hazard, the risk that banks take excessive risks expecting to be bailed out.

This is why regulators focus on preventing failures and resolving them quickly and in an orderly way when they occur.

How Safe Are Indian Banks?

Indian banks are regulated and supervised by the RBI, which sets requirements for capital, liquidity, asset quality and governance, including international Basel III standards. Since around 2018, Indian banks have significantly reduced bad loans and strengthened capital positions, with gross NPA ratios falling to multi-year lows according to RBI Financial Stability Reports.

The RBI also designates certain large banks, such as State Bank of India, HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank, as Domestic Systemically Important Banks (D-SIBs), subject to extra capital requirements because their failure would seriously harm the economy.

Cooperative banks have historically faced more governance problems than commercial banks. Amendments to the Banking Regulation Act in 2020 brought them under stronger RBI supervision.

An Illustrative Example: Spreading Deposits

Imagine a retired couple with 24 lakh rupees in savings. If all of it sits in one bank in individual accounts, only 5 lakh rupees per person, 10 lakh in total, may be insured if that bank fails, though actual outcomes depend on how the failure is resolved. By spreading their savings across three well-regulated banks, keeping each person's deposits at each bank within 5 lakh rupees, and putting some money into other safe options such as the Senior Citizens Savings Scheme or government securities, they can bring almost all their savings within insured or sovereign-backed protection. The trade-off is managing a few more accounts, a small price for peace of mind.

How to Protect Your Money

  1. Spread large deposits across several banks so each balance stays within the insured limit.
  2. Use different ownership capacities where appropriate, such as individual and joint accounts, understanding how insurance applies to each.
  3. Choose well-regulated banks and check their financial health through published results, capital ratios and any RBI restrictions.
  4. Be cautious with cooperative banks offering unusually high interest rates; higher returns often come with higher risk.
  5. Diversify savings beyond bank deposits, for example into government securities, debt mutual funds, PPF or other instruments suited to your goals.
  6. Keep an emergency fund accessible in more than one bank, so a temporary freeze doesn't leave you without cash.
  7. Stay calm during rumours; panic withdrawals can create the very crisis people fear. Rely on official information from the RBI and the bank.
  8. Update nominations on all accounts and deposits.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much of my money is safe if my bank fails in India?

Up to 5 lakh rupees per depositor per bank, including interest, is insured by DICGC. Amounts above this depend on how the bank's failure is resolved.

How long does it take to get insured money?

Under the 2021 amendment, depositors of banks placed under all-inclusive directions can receive insured amounts within about 90 days. In mergers, depositors usually keep access to their money through the acquiring bank.

Are fixed deposits safer than savings accounts?

Both are insured in the same way, up to the combined limit per depositor per bank. Fixed deposits aren't automatically safer.

Are mutual funds affected if a bank fails?

Mutual fund assets are held by custodians separately from the fund house and banks, so a bank failure doesn't directly affect mutual fund holdings, although funds holding a failed bank's bonds or shares could see losses.

Has a large Indian commercial bank ever failed?

Indian authorities have usually resolved troubled commercial banks through mergers or rescues, as with Global Trust Bank (merged with Oriental Bank of Commerce in 2004), Yes Bank and Lakshmi Vilas Bank, protecting depositors.

Are small finance banks covered by DICGC?

Yes. Small finance banks and payments banks are covered by DICGC deposit insurance up to the same limit as other insured banks.

Is money in a payments bank or wallet safe?

Deposits in payments banks are insured by DICGC up to the limit. Money held in prepaid wallets issued by non-bank companies is not bank deposit and is regulated differently, so it isn't covered by DICGC.

Does deposit insurance cover cooperative society deposits?

Deposits in cooperative banks registered with and regulated by the RBI are generally covered. Deposits with cooperative credit societies that are not banks are not covered by DICGC.

What should I do if I hear rumours about my bank?

Check official sources such as RBI announcements and the bank's own statements. Avoid acting on social media rumours, and make sure your deposits are within insured limits as a general precaution.

Final Thoughts

Bank failures are rare but real. When they happen, regulators work to protect depositors and the wider financial system through restrictions, mergers, rescues or orderly closure. Deposit insurance protects most ordinary savers, but only up to set limits. By understanding these rules, spreading large savings across banks, choosing well-regulated institutions and staying calm during rumours, you can keep your money safe even if a bank runs into trouble.
