Updated 3 November 2026

Quick answer: The American Opportunity Tax Credit (AOTC) cuts your federal tax by up to $2,500 per eligible student per year for the first four years of college. It covers 100% of the first $2,000 you spend on tuition, required fees and course materials, plus 25% of the next $2,000. Up to $1,000 of it (40%) is refundable, so you can get it even if you owe no tax. The full credit is available with modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) of up to $80,000, or $160,000 if married filing jointly. It shrinks to nothing at $90,000 or $180,000. From 2026 returns, the 2025 budget law requires valid Social Security numbers rather than ITINs. You claim it on Form 8863 using the student's Form 1098-T.

The AOTC is the most valuable education tax break for most families, worth up to $10,000 per student over four years. It is also one of the most often claimed in error, and the IRS can ban you from claiming it for two or even ten years if it decides a claim was reckless or fraudulent. Here is how it works, who qualifies, and how to get the full amount legitimately.

How the Credit Is Calculated

Qualified expenses you paidCreditRefundable part (40%)
$1,000$1,000$400
$2,000$2,000$800
$3,200$2,300$920
$4,000 or more$2,500$1,000

The formula: 100% of the first $2,000, plus 25% of expenses between $2,000 and $4,000. Spending above $4,000 adds nothing. The credit is per student, so a family with two eligible students in college can claim up to $5,000.

Who Qualifies

The student must:

  • Be pursuing a degree or other recognised credential.
  • Be enrolled at least half-time for at least one academic period (a semester, trimester or quarter) that begins in the tax year.
  • Not have finished the first four years of higher education at the start of the tax year.
  • Not have claimed the AOTC (or had it claimed for them) in four earlier tax years.
  • Not have a felony drug conviction at the end of the tax year.
  • Attend an eligible institution: almost any accredited college, university or vocational school that takes part in federal student aid.

The person claiming must:

  • Pay the qualified expenses, for themselves, a spouse or a dependent they claim.
  • Have MAGI under the limits below.
  • Not file as married filing separately.
  • From 2026 tax returns, have a valid Social Security number (as must a spouse on a joint return), with the student also having one, issued before the return's due date. ITINs no longer qualify.

Income Limits and the Phase-Out

Filing statusFull creditReduced creditNo credit
Single, head of householdMAGI up to $80,000$80,000-$90,000Over $90,000
Married filing jointlyMAGI up to $160,000$160,000-$180,000Over $180,000

Within the band, the credit shrinks in proportion. A married couple with MAGI of $165,000 is a quarter of the way through the $20,000 band, so they get 75% of the credit: $1,875 instead of $2,500. These thresholds aren't indexed for inflation.

What Counts as a Qualified Expense

Counts:

  • Tuition and fees required for enrolment.
  • Books, supplies and equipment needed for a course, even if you buy them from somewhere other than the school. This is a difference from the Lifetime Learning Credit.

Doesn't count:

  • Room and board, insurance, medical expenses and transport.
  • Any expense paid with tax-free money: Pell Grants and other scholarships used for tuition, employer education assistance, or tax-free 529 withdrawals.

Expenses paid with student loans do count, because the loan is your money. Tuition paid in December for a term starting in January, February or March counts for the year you pay it.

Coordinating With 529 Plans and Scholarships

You can't use the same dollar twice. A common, legal approach is to pay $4,000 of tuition from income, savings or loans to claim the full $2,500 credit, and use 529 money for the remaining tuition and for room and board, which 529 plans can cover but the AOTC can't.

Scholarships give another opening. If a student's scholarship can be used for either tuition or living costs, applying it to living costs makes that portion taxable to the student, often at a low or zero rate, but frees up tuition to qualify for the credit. Work through the numbers, or ask a tax preparer, before doing this.

You can't claim both for the same student in the same year, but you can claim the AOTC for one child and the Lifetime Learning Credit for another. Once a student's four AOTC years are used up, graduate school and later courses can move to the Lifetime Learning Credit.

Parent or Student: Who Claims It?

If the parents claim the student as a dependent, only the parents can take the credit, even if the student paid the tuition. If no one claims the student as a dependent, the student claims it. The refundable $1,000 part may not be available to students under 24 who claim it themselves, if their earned income was less than half their own support and a parent is alive. In that case the credit can only reduce tax they owe.

When the parents' income is above the phase-out, it can be worth comparing whether the student, not being claimed as a dependent, could take the credit instead. That means the parents give up claiming the student as a dependent, so run both versions.

How to Claim the Credit

  1. Get the student's Form 1098-T from the school, usually by 31 January. Box 1 shows tuition paid, and box 5 shows scholarships.
  2. Gather receipts for books and course materials not on the 1098-T.
  3. Complete Form 8863, including the school's employer identification number (EIN). Your tax software will do this if you answer its education questions.
  4. Keep records for at least three years in case the IRS asks for proof of enrolment and payment.

Mistakes That Trigger IRS Letters

  • Claiming a fifth year, or a year the student was below half-time.
  • Counting expenses that scholarships or 529 withdrawals already paid for.
  • Both a parent and the student claiming the credit for the same student.
  • Claiming room and board.
  • No 1098-T or school EIN on file.

If the IRS rejects a claim it considers reckless, you can be barred from the credit for two years. If it considers the claim fraudulent, the bar is ten years.

Frequently Asked Questions: American Opportunity Tax Credit

How much is the American Opportunity Tax Credit for 2026?

Up to $2,500 per eligible student, of which up to $1,000 is refundable.

Can I get the AOTC if I owe no tax?

Yes. Up to 40% of the credit, up to $1,000, is refundable and comes back as part of your refund.

Does the AOTC apply to graduate school?

No. It covers only the first four years of higher education. Graduate students can use the Lifetime Learning Credit.

Can I claim the AOTC for community college?

Yes, if the student is enrolled at least half-time in a programme leading to a degree or credential at an eligible school.

Do student loan payments count?

Tuition paid with loan money counts in the year the tuition is paid, not the year you repay the loan.

What changed for 2026?

The 2025 budget law requires valid Social Security numbers, issued before the return's due date, for the person claiming (and spouse on a joint return) and the student. Individual taxpayer identification numbers (ITINs) no longer qualify. The credit amount and income limits are unchanged.

Final Thoughts

For a family paying at least $4,000 of tuition from their own pocket or loans, the AOTC is a near-certain $2,500 a year, and $10,000 over a degree. The money is lost through avoidable mistakes: double-counting scholarship or 529 money, using up the four years carelessly, or both parent and student claiming it. Plan which money pays which bill before the year starts, keep the 1098-T and receipts, and claim it every year the student qualifies.
