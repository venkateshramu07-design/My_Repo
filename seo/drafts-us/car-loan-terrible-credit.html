Quick answer: You can get a car loan with terrible credit, but it costs a lot. Experian's data for the second quarter of 2026 puts the average rate for borrowers with scores of 300 to 500 at about 16.1% for a new car and 21.6% for a used one, against 6.2% and 8.8% for prime borrowers. On a $20,000 used-car loan over five years, that is about $548 a month and $12,900 in interest, compared with $413 a month and $4,800 for a prime borrower. You will usually get the best approval at a credit union or through a pre-approval you arrange before visiting a dealer. Keep the loan short, put money down, avoid add-ons, and refinance once your score recovers.

"Terrible credit" usually means a FICO score below about 580, or a recent repossession, bankruptcy, collection or a string of late payments. Lenders still approve these borrowers, because a car is collateral: if you stop paying, the lender takes it back. What changes is the price, and how many traps sit between you and the keys.

This guide explains what lenders actually approve, what it really costs, where to apply first, and how to avoid the deals that leave people owing more than the car is worth.

What That Costs: A Worked Example

Take a $20,000 used car financed over 60 months:

BorrowerAPRMonthly paymentTotal interest
Prime8.81%$413$4,800
Subprime19.10%$520$11,200
Deep subprime21.62%$548$12,900

The deep subprime borrower pays about $8,100 more for the same car. Two levers cut that sharply:

  • A shorter term. At 21.62%, the same $20,000 over 48 months costs $626 a month but about $10,000 in interest. Over 72 months the payment falls to $498, but the interest rises to about $15,900.
  • A down payment. Putting $5,000 down and borrowing $15,000 for 60 months at 21.62% costs $411 a month and about $9,700 in interest, saving roughly $3,200.

A cheaper car is the biggest lever of all. At high rates, every $1,000 you don't borrow saves around $640 in interest over five years.

What Lenders Look at Besides Your Score

Subprime auto lenders care less about the score itself than about whether you can make the payment. They check:

  • Income and how long you've had it. Many want at least $1,500 to $2,000 a month before tax, with proof such as pay stubs or bank statements. Six months or more at the same job helps.
  • Payment-to-income ratio. Most lenders want the car payment to stay under about 15% to 20% of gross monthly income. At $3,000 a month, that means a payment of roughly $450 to $600.
  • Debt-to-income ratio. Total monthly debt payments, including the new car, of under roughly 45% to 50% of income.
  • Residence and phone. Proof of address, and sometimes a set of personal references.
  • Recent history. A repossession in the last year or an open bankruptcy matters more than old collections. A Chapter 13 bankruptcy may need the trustee's permission before you borrow.

Where to Apply, in Order

1. Credit unions

Credit unions are member-owned and often more flexible with damaged credit, especially if you already bank with one. Federal credit unions are generally limited to an 18% APR on most loans under National Credit Union Administration rules. That limit alone can beat the 21% to 25% many dealers quote. Some run "credit builder" or "fresh start" auto programs.

2. Online pre-approval

Several online lenders and marketplaces pre-qualify you with a soft credit check, which doesn't hurt your score. A pre-approval tells you your real rate and maximum loan before you see a car, and gives you a number the dealer has to beat.

3. Dealer financing, but only to beat your pre-approval

Franchise dealers send your application to many lenders, including the subprime arms of the carmakers' own finance companies. They can sometimes beat your pre-approval. But the dealer may add a markup to the rate the lender offers, typically up to 2 to 2.5 percentage points, so negotiate the rate as well as the price.

4. Buy-here-pay-here, only as a last resort

At buy-here-pay-here lots the dealer is also the lender. Approval is easy, but prices are high, rates are often at the state maximum, and many install GPS trackers or starter-interrupt devices so they can repossess quickly. Some don't report your payments to the credit bureaus, so paying on time won't rebuild your score. If you use one, ask in writing whether it reports to Experian, Equifax and TransUnion.

How to Improve Your Odds Before You Apply

  • Check all three credit reports free at AnnualCreditReport.com, and dispute errors. Mistakes such as a debt listed twice or a paid account still shown as unpaid are common and can cost you points.
  • Save a down payment. 10% of the price, or $1,000, whichever is more, is a common subprime requirement. More lowers both the rate and the risk of owing more than the car is worth.
  • Add a co-signer with good credit if you have one. This can move you up a tier or two. The co-signer is fully liable if you don't pay, and the loan appears on their credit report too.
  • Bring proof of income and residence so the lender doesn't need to make "exceptions", which often come with a higher rate.
  • Do your rate shopping within 14 days. FICO counts multiple auto loan enquiries in a short window as a single enquiry, so shopping around costs you almost nothing in score.

Traps That Make a Bad-Credit Car Loan Worse

Focusing on the monthly payment

"What payment can you afford?" is the question that lets a seller stretch the loan to 84 months and pile on extras while the payment stays the same. Negotiate the out-the-door price first, then the rate, then the term.

Add-ons rolled into the loan

Extended warranties, paint protection, theft etching and credit insurance are often added at high markups and financed at your 20%-plus rate. GAP insurance can be worth having on a loan with little or no down payment, but it's usually far cheaper from your car insurer or credit union than from the dealer.

Spot delivery ("yo-yo" financing)

You drive home in the car, and days later the dealer calls to say the financing "fell through" and you must sign a new contract at a higher rate. Don't trade in your old car or leave a large down payment until the financing is final, and get the terms in writing.

Rolling over negative equity

If you owe more on your trade-in than it's worth, the difference is often quietly added to the new loan. You then start out owing far more than the car is worth, which makes it hard to sell or refinance later.

Refinance Once Your Credit Improves

A bad-credit car loan doesn't have to last its full term. After 6 to 12 months of on-time payments, and once your score has moved up a tier, you can often refinance at a much lower rate, especially with a credit union. Moving from 21.6% to 13.9% with $15,000 left over 48 months cuts the payment by about $60 a month and saves roughly $2,900 in interest. Before signing the original loan, check that it has no prepayment penalty.

Frequently Asked Questions: Car Loans With Terrible Credit

What credit score do I need for a car loan?

There's no official minimum. Lenders approve scores in the 300s and 400s every day, but at deep subprime rates averaging about 16% for new cars and 21.6% for used cars in mid-2026. Income, job stability and the down payment often matter more than the score at that level.

Can I get a car loan after a repossession?

Yes, but expect the highest rates and a larger down payment, particularly within the first year. A credit union you already bank with, or a co-signer, gives the best chance of a reasonable rate.

Is it better to buy a cheaper used car with cash?

If you can buy a reliable car outright for a few thousand dollars, you avoid interest entirely, but you also miss the chance to rebuild credit with on-time payments. A middle path is a small loan on a cheap car that you pay off quickly.

How much down payment do I need with bad credit?

Many subprime lenders ask for at least $1,000 or 10% of the price. Each extra dollar you put down cuts the rate risk and the interest.

Will a car loan help rebuild my credit?

Yes, if the lender reports to the credit bureaus and you pay on time every month. Installment loans paid as agreed are among the strongest positive marks on a credit report.

Are "no credit check" car loans legitimate?

Most are buy-here-pay-here arrangements. They're legal, but they usually charge much more, and they may not report your payments. Compare the total cost with at least one credit union offer first.

Final Thoughts

Terrible credit doesn't stop you buying a car. It makes the loan expensive, and that expense is where most of the damage happens. Get pre-approved before you shop, borrow as little as you can for as short a term as you can manage, refuse the add-ons, and treat the loan as a 12-month credit-rebuilding project that ends in a refinance. Done that way, the extra cost of bad credit is temporary.
