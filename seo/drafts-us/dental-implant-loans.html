Quick answer: A single dental implant, meaning the post, abutment and crown, typically costs $3,000 to $6,000 in the US in 2026, before any bone graft or extraction. The cheapest ways to pay for it, in order:

  • Insurance and HSA or FSA money.
  • An interest-free in-house plan from your dentist.
  • A personal loan from a credit union, or a fixed-rate personal loan. Average 24-month rates were about 12% in 2026.
  • A medical credit card such as CareCredit, only if you're certain to clear the promotional balance in time. Miss the deadline and interest is charged back to the purchase date at the card's standard rate, reported at 32.99%.

On a $4,500 implant, a 24-month loan at 12% costs about $212 a month and $580 in interest.

Implants are rarely fully covered by dental insurance, so most patients pay a large share themselves. How you finance that share can change the final price by hundreds or thousands of dollars. This guide compares each option with real numbers.

Step 1: Cut the Bill Before You Borrow

Dental insurance

Many plans now cover implants as a "major service", often at 50%, but almost all cap total benefits at about $1,000 to $2,000 a year. They may also have waiting periods of 6 to 12 months for major work, or a "missing tooth clause" that excludes teeth lost before you joined. Ask for a pre-treatment estimate.

Splitting treatment across two calendar years, for example the implant in December and the crown in January, can let you use two years' maximums.

HSA and FSA money

Implants that restore function count as a medical expense, so you can pay with pre-tax money from a health savings account or flexible spending account. That's effectively a discount equal to your tax rate. Purely cosmetic work doesn't qualify.

Dental schools

University dental schools often treat patients at a lower price, under specialist supervision. Appointments are longer and waiting lists can be long.

Ask about cash discounts

Some practices give 5% to 10% off for payment in full, which can outweigh the cost of a cheap loan.

The Deferred Interest Trap, With Numbers

Medical credit cards often advertise "no interest if paid in full within 12 months". That isn't the same as 0% APR. Interest is calculated all along at the standard rate and charged back if any balance remains when the promotion ends.

Say you put a $4,500 implant on a 12-month deferred interest plan and pay $350 a month. After 12 months you've paid $4,200 and $300 remains. Because the promotion wasn't met, about $850 of backdated interest at 32.99% is added at once, nearly three times the balance you had left. To avoid it, divide the total by the number of months ($4,500 ÷ 12 = $375) and set up that payment, or more, from day one.

Some medical cards also offer fixed reduced-rate plans for larger purchases, such as 24 to 60 months at a set APR. These have no deferred interest and are safer if you can't clear the balance quickly.

A personal loan has a fixed payment and a fixed end date, with no backdated interest. Pre-qualifying with a few lenders uses a soft credit check, so you can compare real offers without hurting your score.

Dental Loans With Bad Credit

With a low score, start with a credit union you already bank with, ask the dentist for an in-house plan, or add a co-signer. Be wary of "no credit check" dental financing and lease-to-own plans. Their effective APRs can exceed 100%. If you need several implants and can't finance them sensibly, ask about cheaper interim options such as a removable partial denture, and plan implants later.

Questions to Ask Your Dentist Before You Sign

  • What's included in the price, and what could be added (graft, sinus lift, temporary tooth)?
  • Who places the implant, and how many have they done?
  • What brand of implant is used, and is it widely supported if you move?
  • What happens, and what does it cost, if the implant fails?
  • Do you offer an in-house payment plan or a cash discount?

Frequently Asked Questions: Dental Implant Loans

Can I finance dental implants?

Yes, through in-house plans, personal loans, credit union loans, medical credit cards or 0% intro credit cards. Fixed-rate personal loans are the most predictable.

Is CareCredit good for dental implants?

Only if you'll definitely pay the full balance before the promotional period ends. If not, backdated interest at the standard rate, reported at 32.99%, makes it one of the most expensive options.

Does insurance cover dental implants?

Partly, on many plans. It's often 50% as a major service, up to an annual maximum of about $1,000 to $2,000, sometimes after a waiting period.

Can I use my HSA for dental implants?

Yes, when the implant replaces a missing or failing tooth for function, it's a qualified medical expense. Cosmetic-only treatment isn't.

What credit score do I need for a dental loan?

Many lenders approve scores from about 580 to 600, but the best rates usually need 670 or more. Below that, in-house plans and credit unions are the best options.

How much is a dental implant per month?

On a $4,500 implant: about $375 a month to clear a 12-month 0% plan, $212 on a 24-month loan at 12%, or $163 to $179 over 36 months at 18% to 25%.

Final Thoughts

The cheapest dental implant is the one you finance last. Use insurance, HSA or FSA money and any cash discount first, then an interest-free in-house plan. Borrow the remainder on a fixed-rate loan with a clear end date. Treat deferred interest offers as a tool only for amounts you can definitely clear in time, and get an itemised quote so the price you finance is the price you pay.
