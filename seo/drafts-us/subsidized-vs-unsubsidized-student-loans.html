Quick answer: Subsidized and unsubsidized federal student loans have the same interest rate, 6.52% for undergraduates on loans first disbursed between 1 July 2026 and 30 June 2027. The difference is who pays the interest while you're in school. On a subsidized loan, the government pays it while you're enrolled at least half-time, for six months after you leave, and during deferment. On an unsubsidized loan, interest builds from the day the money is paid out. A student who takes the maximum subsidized amount every year for four years avoids about $3,500 of interest that an unsubsidized borrower would owe. Always accept subsidized loans first, but only undergraduates with financial need can get them.

Both loans are Direct Loans from the US Department of Education. You apply for both through the FAFSA, and they appear together in your financial aid offer. They have the same fee and the same repayment plans, so the only question that matters is how much interest builds up before you start repaying, and what you can do about it.

The 2026-27 rates were set from the 10-year Treasury auction in May 2026 (4.468%) plus a margin fixed by law. Each loan keeps its rate for life, so a student who borrows in four different years will usually have four slightly different rates.

How Much Interest the Subsidy Saves

Take a $4,500 loan borrowed at the start of sophomore year, a common amount. At 6.52%, interest builds at about $24.45 a month. By the time repayment starts, after three years of school and a six-month grace period, the unsubsidized borrower owes about $1,030 in interest on top of the $4,500. The subsidized borrower owes nothing extra.

Across a full degree, the gap grows. A dependent undergraduate who takes the maximum subsidized amount each year ($3,500, $4,500, $5,500 and $5,500, or $19,000 in all) avoids about $3,500 of interest that would build up on the same amounts borrowed unsubsidized.

Repaying $19,000 over the standard 10 years at 6.52% costs about $216 a month. If the $3,500 of interest from an unsubsidized loan were added to the balance, the payment would rise to about $256 a month, and the total extra cost would be about $4,800 over the life of the loan.

Who Qualifies for Subsidized Loans

Only undergraduate students with financial need, as worked out from the FAFSA, can borrow subsidized loans. Your school calculates need as its cost of attendance minus your Student Aid Index (SAI) and other aid. If your need is smaller than the subsidized maximum, you get a smaller subsidized loan and the rest is offered as unsubsidized. Graduate and professional students haven't been eligible for subsidized loans since July 2012.

Dependent students whose parents are refused a Parent PLUS loan can borrow the independent-student amounts.

Graduate students can borrow only unsubsidized loans. Under the 2025 budget law (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act), graduate PLUS loans closed to new borrowers on 1 July 2026, and new graduate limits apply: $20,500 a year and $100,000 in total for most graduate programmes, and $50,000 a year and $200,000 in total for professional degrees such as medicine and law. Parent PLUS loans are now capped at $20,000 a year and $65,000 in total per student.

The Order to Accept Loans In

  1. Grants, scholarships and work-study first. This is money you don't repay.
  2. Direct Subsidized loans. These are the cheapest debt you'll ever be offered.
  3. Direct Unsubsidized loans. These carry the same rate, but interest builds while you study.
  4. Parent PLUS or private loans, last. PLUS loans cost 9.07% plus a 4.228% fee in 2026-27. Private loans depend on credit and lack federal protections such as income-driven repayment.

You don't have to accept the full amount offered. Borrow what you need for the year, not what you're allowed.

How to Shrink an Unsubsidized Loan's Cost

Pay the interest while you're in school

On a $4,500 unsubsidized loan, the interest is about $24 a month. Paying that each month from a part-time job means you leave school owing $4,500, not about $5,500. It turns an unsubsidized loan into a subsidized one, paid for by you.

Pay before the grace period ends

Even a lump sum at graduation, before repayment begins, wipes out interest that would otherwise be added to your balance under some repayment situations.

Borrow late, not early

Interest on a loan taken in senior year runs for about 18 months before repayment, compared with 54 months on a freshman loan. If you need to borrow unsubsidized money at some point, later costs less.

The Origination Fee

Both loans charge a 1.057% fee, taken out before the money reaches your school. Borrow $4,500 and about $4,452 is paid out, but you repay the full $4,500. Budget for the gap.

Repaying Your Loans

Repayment starts six months after you graduate, leave school, or drop below half-time enrolment. Since 1 July 2026, borrowers taking out new loans choose between a standard fixed plan and the new Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP), which bases payments on income. Older plans are being phased out for existing borrowers by July 2028. Your servicer will show the options when repayment approaches. Studentaid.gov's Loan Simulator compares them using your own balance and income.

Frequently Asked Questions: Subsidized vs Unsubsidized Loans

Is a subsidized or unsubsidized loan better?

Subsidized is always better when you can get it: same rate, same fee, but no interest while you're in school. Take subsidized first and unsubsidized only for what's left.

Do unsubsidized loans have a higher interest rate?

Not for undergraduates. Both are 6.52% for 2026-27. Graduate unsubsidized loans are 8.07%.

Do I have to pay interest on unsubsidized loans while in school?

No, payments aren't required, but interest builds. Paying it as you go keeps your balance from growing.

Why didn't I get a subsidized loan?

Either your FAFSA showed no financial need at your school's cost of attendance, you're a graduate student, or you've reached the $23,000 lifetime subsidized limit.

Can I switch an unsubsidized loan to subsidized later?

No. The type is fixed when the loan is made. If your need changes, a later year's offer can include more subsidized money.

Do these loans need a credit check or a co-signer?

No. Direct Subsidized and Unsubsidized loans don't require a credit check or co-signer. Parent PLUS loans check for adverse credit history.

Final Thoughts

The subsidized versus unsubsidized choice is mostly made for you by the FAFSA. What you control is the order you accept loans in, how much you take, and whether you let unsubsidized interest pile up. Accept subsidized loans first, borrow only what the year really costs, and pay the interest on unsubsidized loans as you go if you can. Together, those habits can save several thousand dollars.
